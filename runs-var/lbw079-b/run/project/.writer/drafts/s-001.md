I had to force the attic open.

Decision: the mirror leaned against the far wall beneath a small west window, trunks stacked in a line under the rafters. Decision: as a child I had never been told about Evie — the name was missing from our family stories.

The hatch stuck on the second pull. My hands were already raw from hauling boxes downstairs and answering questions I did not have the patience for, so I used the crowbar the estate agent had left in the garage and pried the thing up in two impatient breaths. A soft cold rose out of the dark as if the house had been holding its breath; the attic smelled of old oilcloth and wood that remembered damp. Nobody had been up here in years and, absurdly, the floorboards were almost clean: dustless in that particular way of places you avoid and thus never disturb.

Light came through a small west window in a narrow beam that slid across trunks and splinters of packing crate. The mirror was where I said I would picture it: tall, walnut-framed and full-length, the glass slightly warped so the rafters bent as if they were breathing. It stood propped against the far wall, catching the slant of the afternoon. The trunks were stacked along the left-hand side under the rafters, one on top of another like a spine of brittle leather.

The largest trunk yielded without drama. The leather flaked where my fingers pressed, and the brass catches sighed and opened. Inside, on a pad of yellowing tissue, lay a small, tarnished brass locket on a fragile chain and, beside it, a larger sepia photograph folded carefully in a moth-eaten handkerchief. I did not gasp. I turned things over like inventory — list, label, bag, move on.

The locket was colder than the air. I pried it open with my thumbnail and found a tiny photograph of two girls pressed flat inside. I had to hold the picture up to the light to make out faces: wind-stung cheeks, skirts blown at odd angles. On the back of the larger photograph, when I unfolded it, someone had written a single name in faded ink: Evie.

I said it aloud to the empty rafters because saying a thing aloud is how I make things real for myself. "Evie," I said, and the sound felt too small for the attic.

I turned toward the mirror as if to check how I looked — hair out of place, the same hands I had used to split the hatch — and the reflection made me do a double-take. There, at the far end of the attic where shadow pooled behind the trunks, the glass held a second figure: a girl standing a little way from the trunks, head tilted as if listening. The rest of the attic, the boards under my boots, the crowbar by the hatch, showed no one. I tested it the way you test a trick: I stepped left, then right, blinked, even leaned so my shoulder cut the angle of the light. The girl did not move in unison; she stayed at the far end, a pale, steady shape in the glass.

I told myself a dozen sensible things. Old frames can double images. Mirrors warp. Grief makes you see family in the wrong places. I was tired and practical and there were boxes to label. Still, I kept the locket in my palm and I could feel my pulse in the chain. The girl in the mirror had no label I could read, only the posture of a person waiting, and that waiting felt like an accusation.

I closed my fingers around the locket and put it in my pocket because the sensible thing was to put important things somewhere safe. Then I stood with the hatch open and listened to the house settle, and the mirror kept the girl.
