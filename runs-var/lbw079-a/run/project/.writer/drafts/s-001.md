The attic smelled of salt and new paint, a dry clean edge to the dust that made me cough when I tugged at the canvas. Light fell through slatted boards in knives; it made everything look like an exhibit I was supposed to catalog and not inherit. I set my bag down and began with what was nearest: trunks, trunks, trunks stacked like tired suitcases against the rafters.

The cedar trunk took up the left corner. The yellowing sticker along its lid read, exactly, May Harper — attic inventory — 1939. I tapped the brass catch, listened to the tiny, practical click it made — Aunt May was never theatrical — and eased the lid. Inside, cedar shavings smelled faintly of the same old varnish the house kept; a battered porcelain doll lay cushioned on a folded scrap of dress, its eyes sewn and its smile stitched with someone too steady to be kind.

I worked methodically. Open, name, note condition. Old trunks yielded order: a hat box with a catalogue tag, a bundle of woolens with a staple of inventory tape, a tin of photographs wrapped in oilcloth. In one small cedar parcel, stitched into the lining, a folded scrap of paper had been tamped like a secret. In Aunt May’s cramped capitals it read: sewn into coat. I folded the scrap back into its parcel and set it aside next to the doll; it felt like finding a bookmark in a book I didn't yet understand.

The diary was no surprise when it turned up — a small leather thing wedged between a shoebox and a stack of knitting patterns. The lock had long ago given up and the first page fell open in my fingers. A child’s hand had written, across a space like a breath: "Hush, the sea will sing you home." The line sat there like a rule.

I moved the mirror last because it filled the room whether it was there or not. It had been shoved into a boarded alcove and covered with canvas, a blank wrapped in its own history; even under the cloth the gilt frame ridged the air with expectation. I hauled the canvas away. The glass had that faint waviness older mirrors develop and a salt bloom at one edge, as if the sea had pressed the whole thing with its thumb. I left my hands on the frame because there is a thing about inherited surfaces — you hold them and they tell you what they will not.

I stood in front of it and did the practical checks. The attic was cold; my breath made a pale thread. I told myself it was the glass — the waviness, a delayed catch of light — or the draft through the boards playing tricks, or that the doll behind me might have moved in the same slatted beam of sun. I turned slowly, scanned for a seam, peered at the back for condensation or a second pane, called the room’s name with the small, steady voice I used when cataloguing fragile photographs.

When I stepped in front of it my reflection lagged by a breath and, for a second, a child's small hand pressed out from behind the glass as if from the other side. I froze hard enough that the room remembered it. The hand was small and wet-looking in the light, the fingers splayed like they were pushing on the inside of the world. I rattled off the possible ordinary answers in my head again — a trick of age in the glass, the angle of light, a delayed blink — and bent to look for the seam I was sure would be there, because certainty is a habit and habits feel like safety.
