### world-rules  <world/rules.yaml>

These govern what is TRUE. They do not say what anyone KNOWS.
A character knows a rule only where canon says so; otherwise they may be ignorant of it, wrong about it, or in the middle of working it out — and if the story is about someone discovering one of these, writing it into their thoughts early is how that story gets destroyed.

- Peri are effectively immortal and powerful, but they are bound by oaths, covenants, and the binding artifacts they make; they cannot simply will away mortal free will without legalistic or talismanic devices.
- Isle Jarrah is subject to the witches' fate-weaving: ordinary physical exit is barred by the island's enchantments. Leaving is possible only through specific bargains, rituals, or by altering the island's weaving from within.
- The Spindle can rewrite individual strands of fate — it can alter probabilities, choices, and the course of a single life-line — but every stitch or unpicking requires an exact metaphysical cost: something equivalent must be unmade, taken, or exchanged. It cannot resurrect the truly dead nor change cosmic constants.
- Bargains, names and promises have power. Spoken deals (explicit bargains) made in the island's terms are binding and will be enforced by the island and its practitioners; breaking an explicit bargain creates supernatural consequences.
- The Peri binding amulet enforces compliance but is not absolute: it can be resisted by strong mortal will, counter-magic from the witches, or by using the Spindle under the island's rules — each of those options will carry costs and tradeoffs.
- Magic in this world preserves a balance: gains require losses, and shortcuts to absolute gain (immortality, absolute control of many lives) are blocked by the weave of the world. Characters may negotiate or reroute the balance, but they cannot create value ex nihilo.

### previous-scene  <novel/chapters/ch-02/scenes/s-005.md>

We left at first light, when the city still smelled of embers and the gulls were too hungry to mind a shipful of men. The Qitaf made herself small and quick in the grey—canvas trimmed, lines belled, the kind of movement that looks like a prayer said without words. I cut the last rope with a knife that felt like a promise and the harbor drew itself out of our wake behind a slow, indifferent tide.

Yusuf held the folio like a talisman under his coat, fingers worrying the edge as if he could smooth certainty into it. He did not say the word 'Jarrah' aloud, but he said it in the way a man can when he lays a course: in the angle of his head, in the steadiness of his hands. Rasim leaned at the wheel and coughed into the back of his sleeve the way a man keeps a small worry to himself. The cough left him thinner somehow, like one of the sleepier lanterns we had drawn down for the trip.

The men were quiet in the first hours; nervousness travels on a ship like a scent. It was not the loud sound of fear—there was no shouting, no prayer-chant—but the small trades and jokes missing from the deck, the way nets were checked twice, the way a knife gleamed where yesterday it would have sat dull. They listened when Yusuf spoke of bearings, and they listened harder when he tapped the folio's smudged coastlines. A smudge had become a thinned scar on the map; fingers had worn confidence away until only a suggestion of land remained. "Keep it close," Yusuf muttered to me once, not the same phrase as before but the same meaning: keep the folio, keep the map of our lives.

There was a man at the rail—one I had not seen well in the market—but I knew his sort: the sort that hears the sea and counts it. He pointed at a stain on the folio with a thumb that trembled. "Men who left and never found the sea again," he said. His voice was small enough for the gulls to take, but big enough to settle on the deck like a stone. The words slid between men like a current; you could see the thought spread, a cautious lowering of shoulders and a holding of hands closer to belt and knife.

Azraq stood aft with the ease of something that does not have to pretend to belong. He had not fussed with our ropes or our jests; he barely watched the crew at all. His silver eyes were where he kept his watch—sometimes they skimmed the horizon like a man looking for a shore, sometimes they looked inward, as if he felt the ship's bones. He watched the sea as if he'd placed a small moon over the deck and let it hang there, light enough to show but too cold to comfort. When I caught his gaze, I felt the amulet at my throat answer him the way a bell answers a hammer: a thin, precise thrum that ran like a wire from skin to bone.

The amulet pricked me, not with a fury but with the patient insistence of a thing doing what it was made to do. It was a reminder and a law—my throat remembered it even when my head tried to forget. I could have made a show of pulling at it, of twisting free; I did not. There were reasons for it beyond the council's patience and my stubbornness. I had my men to think of, and making a scene would have been the sort of thing that cost a crew its nerve.

Azraq did not hold a device or point to the sky; there was no magic machine worked into our rigging. How he watched was a detail the quarterdeck could not explain: he simply watched. It turned the men quiet in a different way, like a weather change that presses the air flat. "He watches like a moon," Rasim muttered once, half to himself, and the men laughed with a sound that was less than a laugh—a venting of breath that was as much to release a held thought as to make a joke.

Hours slipped. We ate small things under the boom—flat bread, an onion softened by stove smoke—and the work took rhythm again. The sea was close and honest, all folds and small mercies; the sky was a wash of grey that promised nothing if you asked it for a straight answer. Yusuf steered sometimes, checked the makeshift compass against his memory, and folded the folio into a pocket like someone closing a thin coffin. He did not show weakness. I liked that about him: a man who carries terror quietly is a better helmsman than one who shouts it.

They traded lines of talk over the folio and the wreck log like sailors trading weather.

As noon spread across the water the fog began to show like breath on the edge of a hand. It came slow and polite at first, a gauze that blurred the world's hard lines, then deeper, holding promises and closing off explanations. Men went quiet again. The Qitaf slowed because the sea made her now with less appetite for racing; the sea took itself seriously when the fog came.

They said the island's name then, but not as a map's point—more as a thing that could hang in the air and wait. It moved along the deck like a rag passed between hands. One of the younger lads, someone who had not yet learned to hide the taste of fear behind a grin, let it slip out. "Jarrah," he said, the consonants light and dangerous. The word found others like a tradesman's cry; it sounded in the mouth and then lived between breaths.

There was a long moment where the name and the fog were the same thing—unseen but obvious. I watched the line where sea met mist and felt my hands want to be elsewhere: on the charts, on the compass, on a man's shoulder. That want was straightforward. Command is always a thing of hands and small fixes.

We had to do two things as the island closed in: keep the Qitaf seaworthy, and keep my crew whole in their heads. The two tasks are the same sometimes. I kept my orders short, my steps efficient. When a shout came I answered it in less than a breath; when Yusuf folded the folio under his jacket I made him meet my eye and gave him a nod that meant, simply: keep it hidden.

At dusk the fog took the world and the world took on a narrower shape. Out of it a dark crop rose like a hand's shadow: the island's headland, not yet a shape but a promise of a shape. We did not call it out; we did not need to. "Jarrah," someone breathed, and the name seemed to be the first honest thing since dawn.

I felt the amulet thrum again then, a reminder tied to the silver of Azraq's eyes and the Council behind him. I felt the weight of a promise I had not chosen and the weight of the promises I had chosen—a crew, a ship, a way of keeping men alive. We were in the fog and nearer to the thing that would bind our hands as much as the amulet could. The island gave no mercy for being right.

I stood on the quarterdeck and watched the hand that was Jarrah draw itself out of the grey, every cove and inlet a small algebra of hazard. There was a work ahead that would ask me a debt. For now, the sea took us forward and the amulet sat cold at my throat and kept time with Azraq's moon.
