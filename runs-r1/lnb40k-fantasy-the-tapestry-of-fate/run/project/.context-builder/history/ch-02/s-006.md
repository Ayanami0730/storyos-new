### world-rules  <world/rules.yaml>

These govern what is TRUE. They do not say what anyone KNOWS.
A character knows a rule only where canon says so; otherwise they may be ignorant of it, wrong about it, or in the middle of working it out — and if the story is about someone discovering one of these, writing it into their thoughts early is how that story gets destroyed.

- Peri are immortal and can bind mortals with tokens that enforce obedience in graduated stages; the token cannot be destroyed by ordinary means but can be 'unbound' by specific fate-work.
- The Spindle can rewrite fate only in precise, threadlike edits. Every change demands a proportional cost: a memory, a bond, a trade of consequence that cannot be fabricated.
- The Island of Qarrah enforces inescapability by altering memory, geography, and perception; physical exit is possible only when memory-anchors remain or fate-work alters the island's weave.
- Witches of Qarrah practice confined fate-work (repairs and calibrations) and guard artifacts. They will not allow wholesale erasure of whole peoples or broad cosmic retcons.
- Peri bargains are durable across time; their enforcement is metaphysical and not subject to mortal courts, but the principles of exchange (offer, counteroffer, price) are consistent and can be exploited by skilled fate-work.
- Magic has rules and friction: artifacts like the Spindle are not omnipotent; the more ambitious the rewrite, the more localized and invasive the cost. Miracles are narrow and expensive.

### previous-scene  <novel/chapters/ch-02/scenes/s-005.md>

Amina called the crew to the quarterdeck.

It was not an order so much as a small percussion against the day's work: a single call she used when business wanted shape and the ship's steady clamor needed to stop and listen. Men and women came the way they always did—by habit before curiosity—and the Simorgh's planks filled with the tight, particular smells of tar and rope and sweat.

Nasir was already there, the oilcloth-wrapped ledger under his arm, thumbs along the binding as if he could count safety by touch. He set the ledger on the rail and pried open a battered tin of lamp oil. Using a dented measure he had kept for years, he dipped, leveled the rim and wiped a drop back into the tin with the heel of his hand. "We can't be bleeding stores in port," he said, more to the figures than to Amina. He scratched numbers beside the usual lines: provisions, tar, rope, port fees.

He rolled his sleeve to show a thin white scar on the knuckle of his right hand. People thought scars were plain things; Nasir had given a story for this one once, when the drink had been bitter and the night long: he had walked away from a dice-game rather than pay a debt he had not earned, and had marked himself instead to keep the ledger straight in his head. The scar had nothing glamorous in it, and that was the point.

Ibrahim had a needle in his mouth and a length of frayed sheet across his knees. He traced the knots on the sheet's reefing line with his thumb as he worked—an old helmsman's habit that smoothed his hands and his temper—and hummed a thin lullaby under his breath. He threaded the needle with a small curse and stitched the ragged edge down into a straight seam, eyes half on the cloth and half on Amina. When she named Qarrah he did not look away. "I'll go," he said before she finished, eagerness sharpening his words.

Tariq balanced on the rail with a scrap of yellowed paper spread on his palm, ink flaking along its tear. The scrap had been the kind of thing he kept tucked away; the rest of his books lived beneath a loose plank in the forecastle, wrapped in oilcloth against salt. He smoothed the paper and pointed to a looped mark near its corner. "This one leans toward memory," he said, his voice careful. "It's not an uncommon coastal rune—nothing exotic. It reads like a direction more than a demand."

Amina let the map and the token wait beneath her shirt like an ache she would not show them yet. She watched her crew assemble into a ledger of their own—the man who tamed numbers and kept grudges, the boy who learned a wheel and hummed a song to steady him, the scholar who kept private scraps under plank and could coax meaning from a single mark. The gulf between the Peri Council's rooms and the quarterdeck was measured in different kinds of hush; here the hush had the shape of work.

"They laid a ledger on me," she said finally, feeling the token's patient arithmetic at her throat like a palm across beads. "Azad and Sahr choose a thing they say must be returned—an object called a spindle. They gave me a map and this," and she tapped the silver disc beneath her kerchief. "They said if I refuse, their counting will find other payments."

Nasir balanced the dented measure on his knee, a line of oil flashing on the tin. "You sail with that on you, they can find us by what you stop doing," he said. His voice was the voice of accounts; it named cost. "Cargo's first. If they take a thing we need, repair what we can, burn what gives them an excuse. I'll reclass the ledgers. Provisions first, then tar, then rope, then port fees. We keep the rolls neat and the truth small."

Ibrahim's needle paused. "If you ask me—" he began, and the lullaby in his chest tightened. "If you ask me to take the deck, I'll learn whatever the island asks of me. I can read the sea's face for tricks; I'll watch the woods' edges for footprints. Let me be the one to go with you."

Tariq folded the scrap and slid it back into the sleeve of his coat. "The spindle is dangerous," he said, not as a boast but as an inventory. "That rune—" he tapped the looped mark he had said leaned toward memory—"it suggests the island moves by what people carry in their heads. If the spindle rewrites threads, it will pay in what people remember. We should be careful who we bring and what they carry with them."

Amina felt the ghost at the seam of her thoughts like salt at a wound: the phrase was as old and steady as a promised reef. "Hold the line, Mina." it said, and the words filled her like ballast. She let the map rest beneath her hand, its paper warm from her shirt. "I'll take it," she said, not as resignation but as strategy. "Not for the promise of being free. For the Simorgh. For the people under her. If the council wants an account, I'll write its terms and keep their reach from the others."

Nasir brushed his thumb along the ledger's spine as if to count that vow into the books. "Then we'll make you the account they have to take alone. We'll move the manifests, hide the extra rolls, lie if we must. We'll make it so they must take you and not the ship."

Ibrahim's hands were steady on the sheet now, the needle stitching purpose as neatly as cloth. Tariq's eyes were on the map's sketching of currents and a single, small rune that, to him, meant something like a direction for remembering. The Simorgh's smells folded around them—tar, rope, toasted cumin; ordinary things that had weight.

Amina folded the map back into her shirt and rolled her braid around her wrist until it sat like a knot. The token's counting did not speed. It was a slow arithmetic; it had time. She had chosen the ledger she would carry. The ghost's phrase softened the edges of the choice into a seam she could trust.

"Do it quickly," she told them. "Quietly. Odd tides. No new hands."

They moved then with the particular business of a crew who had been counted together so long the work came before the talking—Nasir rearranging the ledger, Ibrahim stowing the mended sheet, Tariq tucking the scrap back into his oilcloth wrap beneath the loose plank. Amina stayed on the quarterdeck a little longer, the map and the token settled against her like the weight of a promise she had chosen to keep.
