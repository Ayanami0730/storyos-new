### world-rules  <world/rules.yaml>

These govern what is TRUE. They do not say what anyone KNOWS.
A character knows a rule only where canon says so; otherwise they may be ignorant of it, wrong about it, or in the middle of working it out — and if the story is about someone discovering one of these, writing it into their thoughts early is how that story gets destroyed.

- Peri are effectively immortal and powerful, but they are bound by oaths, covenants, and the binding artifacts they make; they cannot simply will away mortal free will without legalistic or talismanic devices.
- Isle Jarrah is subject to the witches' fate-weaving: ordinary physical exit is barred by the island's enchantments. Leaving is possible only through specific bargains, rituals, or by altering the island's weaving from within.
- The Spindle can rewrite individual strands of fate — it can alter probabilities, choices, and the course of a single life-line — but every stitch or unpicking requires an exact metaphysical cost: something equivalent must be unmade, taken, or exchanged. It cannot resurrect the truly dead nor change cosmic constants.
- Bargains, names and promises have power. Spoken deals (explicit bargains) made in the island's terms are binding and will be enforced by the island and its practitioners; breaking an explicit bargain creates supernatural consequences.
- The Peri binding amulet enforces compliance but is not absolute: it can be resisted by strong mortal will, counter-magic from the witches, or by using the Spindle under the island's rules — each of those options will carry costs and tradeoffs.
- Magic in this world preserves a balance: gains require losses, and shortcuts to absolute gain (immortality, absolute control of many lives) are blocked by the weave of the world. Characters may negotiate or reroute the balance, but they cannot create value ex nihilo.

### previous-scene  <novel/chapters/ch-01/scenes/s-001.md>

The Qitaf smelled of singed rope and sea-sour musk, and Port Aden kept its usual throb of hawkers and cartwheels like a fever under everything. We had come in on a tide that rinsed the docks with blood-salty water and the taste of copper still clung to my tongue; the raid's clatter had not yet left my bones. I remember the way the dawn threw the city's awnings into hard relief, the way a gull made a single, obscene call and then was gone. I remember, too, the quiet on my ship—the kind that settles after you pull a job clean and your crew counts what they kept and what they lost.

I was on the Qitaf, tied to Port Aden's eastern quay. My boots left salt on the planks; Rasim, who never quite believed the sea would swallow him, was on the helm like a prayer, hands grafted to the tiller. Yusuf moved through the deck with the slow certainty of an old watch spring—checking lines, looking at faces. He was the measure against my recklessness; when I looked at him I could tell how badly the last run had bitten us.

We had hauled in two chests and a war-standard that would fetch half a market; we had taken candy from a governor's store-room and a knife that reminded me of my father's hand. There were groans down below and the smell of vinegar on a seamstress's bundle; Port Aden would hum another story by noon. I had meant to drink, to count, and to let the crew stray into the market until we could put distance between ourselves and the smell of hot iron.

Instead, a shadow stepped onto our gangway.

He did not belong to the port or to the sea. Azraq moved as if corners were courtesy he did not need; the hem of his robe skimmed the planks without a single salt flake. His eyes were not a sailor's—silver and sharp, like weatherglass. The crew fell quiet because they always fall quiet for what they cannot name. He carried two small things: a paper, dark with salt and thumb-smear, and a folded folio that looked as if it had bled through with other men's maps.

He did not make a grand entrance. He set the papers on a crate between the hammocks and let them be seen. His voice was cool enough to make the smoke of our brazier duck away.

"I am Azraq," he said—not the elaborate Peri titles you read in the old stories, just the one word, a thing delivered and firm. "The Peri Council sent me."

Names have weight; they are knots you can neither unknot nor tie unless you have the rope. Azraq did not hand me a rope. He offered me, instead, a condition.

He drew the torn log up from the folio and spread it with a thumb that left a smudge of dark salt. The paper was thick with other people's panic—ink run into the weave, a diagram scratched out and half-smudged. The map sat folded like a sleeping thing: margins full of notes in different hands, a coastline recast and rescored. I watched the crew lean toward those two objects the way people lean toward a strange illness—curious, afraid for their reasons.

He used the papers as proof. He did not read them to us the way a judge reads a will; he let us see the smears and the lines. He said, simply, that they had been pulled from a wreck near Isle Jarrah, that they told of a loom at the island's heart and a thing that turns threads into fate. I listened with a captain's half-skepticism and a thief's hunger. How much had been survived, how much had been invented between desperate hands and salt—those were questions for later. For the moment, the papers were enough.

"We require a mortal to do what we will not," Azraq said. "You will take a single thing."

There was no theatrical flourish when he named it. He gave the mission like a nail: precise, inevitable.

He named my task: "steal the spindle from Isle Jarrah."

Uproar is a poor word for the way noise re-entered the Qitaf. Yusuf's fingers tightened on the rail until the bone showed. Rasim's face lost the color it had clung to. The crew exchanged looks like sailors reading water. To take something from an island that men whispered about and then never left—this was not a job, it was a sentence.

"The Peri Council has no notion of risk the way we do," Azraq added. There was no plea in it, no apology; the permanence in his tone made the very timbers under our feet feel less stubborn. "We do not bargain with time. We cannot thread this ourselves. That is where you come in."

Yusuf stepped forward. He does not show fear the way a child does; his fear is practical. "And if we refuse?" His voice cut like a reef.

Azraq tilted his head, as if considering a trinket. "Refusal will be answered," he said. "But there is a means to accept."

He lifted the smaller thing, a pendant set with silver-blue glass, rimmed like a choked moon. He did not explain its making—peri law does not translate into our markets—but he took the amulet to me as if it were an invoice.

He closed the amulet at my throat.

It sank like cold coin into my skin and the world learned to care about obedience. There was a sharp, concrete pain, the precise metallic bite of something that knows the shape of oaths. For a moment I felt my hands not as my own but as if someone else had balanced them in the air and were weighing their capacity to harm. My wrists tightened instinctively; the amulet hummed once, a thin sound like a gull's wing against rope.

"You will do what you are told," Azraq said. "You will not leave your charge."

I found my mouth and managed a laugh that came out dry. "And if I—"

When I tried to wrench it free, a hot spike flared under my ribs and something deep and old drew a line through my head. The pain was a command in another language. It did not merely hurt; it argued. My knees folded a hair. I swallowed so hard I tasted iron.

Yusuf caught my arm. His face was an animal map of all the memos and debts that had led us here. "You right with that thing on you?" he asked, not angry so much as calculating. "You right with them sending their teeth to us?"

Azraq's gaze slid to him like a blade checking for purchase. "It is binding," he said. "It will enforce compliance. Resistance will be costly. If you succeed, the Council's terms will be as they are stated."

I had never been a woman to bow to the things that wanted what mortals have. I had grown used to measuring threats with the edges of knives and the clarity of a gun's barrel. This was not like that. This was a law glued to my throat.

The papers remained on the crate between the hammocks. They were not offered as payment and they were not tucked away. They stayed there while we argued, a small, steady ruin of someone else's fate. I touched the folio without thinking and salt skin roughened my thumb.

In the end, it was not bravery or thirst for legend that bent me. It was the arithmetic of the crew. Yusuf's jaw is a map of our ledger; when he decides something is worth paying for, he knows the price by the way men will sleep. I thought of Rasim, hands clinging to a wheel that had carried us through storms that did not care what gods watched. I thought of the groans below that told me someone had hurt in the raid but would live, and I thought of the city, of the markets and the mothers who had nothing.

"We go for the spindle," I said finally. My voice sounded like a rope dragged over wood—frayed, too loud. "But we go my way as much as I can make it so. I keep my crew. You keep your interest hands off while I'm on her."

Azraq's mouth tightened the fraction it needed. The silver in his eyes did not change. "You keep your crew, within the terms we set," he said. "The Council watches."

So it began: a stolen morning at Port Aden, a binding at my throat, a scrap of paper and a folded map on a crate like a promise I had not asked for. The Qitaf rocked in her berth as if testing the sea's patience. The harbor lay open and indifferent. I tasted copper and obligation, and the world felt both smaller and vaster at once.
