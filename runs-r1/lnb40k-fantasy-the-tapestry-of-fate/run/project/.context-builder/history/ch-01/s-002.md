### world-rules  <world/rules.yaml>

These govern what is TRUE. They do not say what anyone KNOWS.
A character knows a rule only where canon says so; otherwise they may be ignorant of it, wrong about it, or in the middle of working it out — and if the story is about someone discovering one of these, writing it into their thoughts early is how that story gets destroyed.

- Peri are immortal and can bind mortals with tokens that enforce obedience in graduated stages; the token cannot be destroyed by ordinary means but can be 'unbound' by specific fate-work.
- The Spindle can rewrite fate only in precise, threadlike edits. Every change demands a proportional cost: a memory, a bond, a trade of consequence that cannot be fabricated.
- The Island of Qarrah enforces inescapability by altering memory, geography, and perception; physical exit is possible only when memory-anchors remain or fate-work alters the island's weave.
- Witches of Qarrah practice confined fate-work (repairs and calibrations) and guard artifacts. They will not allow wholesale erasure of whole peoples or broad cosmic retcons.
- Peri bargains are durable across time; their enforcement is metaphysical and not subject to mortal courts, but the principles of exchange (offer, counteroffer, price) are consistent and can be exploited by skilled fate-work.
- Magic has rules and friction: artifacts like the Spindle are not omnipotent; the more ambitious the rewrite, the more localized and invasive the cost. Miracles are narrow and expensive.

### previous-scene  <novel/chapters/ch-01/scenes/s-001.md>

When the Simorgh swung at her moorings under the blistering sun of Port Rahim, Amina al-Sirafi stood at the rail as if the ship were a thing she could read with her hands. The deck smelled of hot tar and rope, of sweat and toasted cumin from the market stalls beyond the quay. Gulls thinned the air like thrown coins. She let the heat press at the back of her neck and listened to the harbor: men cursing, a bell that had lost its clapper, the slap of a loose sail against a yardarm.

Her fingers found the dented brass of the compass she still carried—so familiar it had grooves where her thumb had worn them. She turned it once, like a benediction, and slipped it back into the pocket of her smoked-leather coat. A length of braided hair, knotted and wrapped until it looked more like rope than ornament, was looped around her wrist. She had braided it herself the week after the funeral and had not taken it off since.

Laila crossed the deck with the steady, sledgehammer step of someone who had never been given to hurry. She wound her hand through the same handful of knots in the rail every time she passed, a small, private motion Amina had seen a thousand times and learned to trust as punctuation. Laila's eyes slid up to Amina and she smiled without ease.

"You still pinch the compass when the wind changes," Laila said, as if reciting an old recipe. "Still think you can feel the weather in your bones." Her voice carried a history—an edge of amusement that had no place on a stranger's tongue.

Amina let the smile come and then go. "And you still count the ropes by twos," she answered. "You do it in the dark like my mother made you. You'd still rather tie than talk, Laila. If you ever spoke to the rigging like it was a man, I'd be jealous."

That drew a look: a flash of something like affection so raw it was almost private shame. Amina felt it like heat under her ribs. They had been children once, hiding behind the same empty barrel in a different port, stealing olives and sharing the one sweet bite between them. The boat had carried them both since they could walk along a deck without falling, and that length of shared youth sat between them like ballast.

Nasir lurked by the binnacle, the old oilcloth-wrapped ledger tucked under his arm, his fingers black at the nails from ink and tar. A thin white scar ran along the knuckle of his right hand—there wasn't a sailor aboard who hadn't nicked himself somewhere—but that little line told Amina more than the ledger ever did: it was where he had been foolish and where he would be cautious next. He nodded at her without ceremony.

"Will she hold for the afternoon heat?" he asked, eyes on the horizon. His voice had the precise grain of someone who balanced ledgers and grudges in the same hand.

"She'll hold," Amina said. She always said that; words that steadied the men were part of what she was paid to do. But she did not mean only the ship. She meant herself too.

A gust picked up out of the east and snapped a pennant. The men moved with the practiced choreography of a crew who had been saved once by a captain who thought faster than the sea. A loose halyard sang and began to run; a quartermaster cursed as the wind took a sheet from a fresh coil.

Amina moved without thinking. She seized the running sheet with one hand and the knife at her hip with the other, planted her feet, and hauled the line down through the block with a command that was all short words: "Hold! Lock the lee block—haul!" The knife flashed; she nicked the foul edge of the sail to free the snarled line, looped it round a cleat, and brushed a man's shoulder as she pivoted, sending him to the tackles. The deck answered—hands fell to rope, a man grunted, the sheet bit and took the wind. The moment passed; the pennant stilled.

She had once trimmed a mizzen in a sudden squall, cutting with a knife that had belonged to her father until the sail rode small and the spar stayed—there were rooms of the crew's memory where that night lived like a map.

The ghost arrived not with thunder or a chill but like the memory of a lullaby: a line threaded through the air that only she could hear. It was not an image so much as a small, familiar pressure against the inside of her teeth. "Hold the line, Mina," it said, and the voice was not spoken aloud, and it was the voice she loved and could not bring back.

She folded that voice away and let her hands find the rail. It was always there—the absence—prepared to be everything she had left. It made her restless in the belly, a boat that would never be still.

A trader hailed from the quay, voice lifted in the old way of every market: news, gossip, prices. Amina turned her face to the sun and to the smell of cumin, and the city leaned at her like a promise with teeth. The Peri Council's token had not yet found her, no immortal hand weighed on her throat this morning; those bargains were for other streets and other nights. Today was for the small, manageable work: sails, loads, the hum of men who trusted their captain because she had already carved risk into something everyone could read.

She let Laila take the run of the deck and moved to the ropes with the easy motions of the woman who had trimmed a sail in a squall and lived to count the lines. That day she steered by touch and memory, by the dented compass in her pocket and the braid on her wrist. In the space between a gull's cry and the slap of a loose sail she felt, for a moment, that the past could be borne like ballast—useful, but not all of her.
