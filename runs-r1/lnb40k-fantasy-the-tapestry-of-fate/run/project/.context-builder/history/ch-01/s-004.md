### world-rules  <world/rules.yaml>

These govern what is TRUE. They do not say what anyone KNOWS.
A character knows a rule only where canon says so; otherwise they may be ignorant of it, wrong about it, or in the middle of working it out — and if the story is about someone discovering one of these, writing it into their thoughts early is how that story gets destroyed.

- Peri are effectively immortal and powerful, but they are bound by oaths, covenants, and the binding artifacts they make; they cannot simply will away mortal free will without legalistic or talismanic devices.
- Isle Jarrah is subject to the witches' fate-weaving: ordinary physical exit is barred by the island's enchantments. Leaving is possible only through specific bargains, rituals, or by altering the island's weaving from within.
- The Spindle can rewrite individual strands of fate — it can alter probabilities, choices, and the course of a single life-line — but every stitch or unpicking requires an exact metaphysical cost: something equivalent must be unmade, taken, or exchanged. It cannot resurrect the truly dead nor change cosmic constants.
- Bargains, names and promises have power. Spoken deals (explicit bargains) made in the island's terms are binding and will be enforced by the island and its practitioners; breaking an explicit bargain creates supernatural consequences.
- The Peri binding amulet enforces compliance but is not absolute: it can be resisted by strong mortal will, counter-magic from the witches, or by using the Spindle under the island's rules — each of those options will carry costs and tradeoffs.
- Magic in this world preserves a balance: gains require losses, and shortcuts to absolute gain (immortality, absolute control of many lives) are blocked by the weave of the world. Characters may negotiate or reroute the balance, but they cannot create value ex nihilo.

### previous-scene  <novel/chapters/ch-01/scenes/s-003.md>

The deck smelled of tar and coffee; the city behind us was a low, confused thing of smoke and market cries. I was on the Qitaf's deck when I called them up. The map lay between us, its paper thin and folded into a shape that had learned to be useful. The folio's edges were soft with handling; I spread the battered folio-map on the hatch and ran my thumb along the smudged coastline until the shapes in it stopped looking like geography and started looking like a problem I could hold.

They came in groups of two or three, dragging sleep out of their faces and the crawl of the hold from their joints. I did not call them to frighten them; I called them because truths are worse when they are hidden and because the thing at my throat did not belong in the dark. Yusuf was there by the mast, his palms white on the rail. There are men who become the ledger of a ship, and Yusuf had always been more of a balance than a book—he did not leap, he measured.

I told them what Azraq had said. I called the crew up and told them what Azraq had said: we were bound to fetch the Spindle from Isle Jarrah. I watched their faces in the wavering lantern-light—some went slack, some hard, the rest a kind of knitting together of fear and appetite. "Isle Jarrah," someone muttered, as if naming the island would make it less a sentence.

The amulet at my throat hummed once against my skin and stung when I let my thoughts stray to walking away. I had accepted the mission because the amulet made saying no a luxury I could not buy. Saying it aloud did not make it smaller; it only moved the feeling into a place where I could see its edges.

Yusuf folded his arms, jaw set. "We keep the ship," he said, the words flat as a bilge plank. "We don't throw her away for someone else's war."

He did not say it like a question. He said it like a condition. I wanted to argue, to fling some heroic image into the air and make them believe it was a choice I had chosen. That was not the truth tonight. The truth was nearer and colder: a Peri had fastened metal and law to my throat and called it duty.

So we argued the numbers without numbers. I talked about what losing the Qitaf would mean in stories, and Yusuf answered with the city-side of things—medicine for Rasim's cough when it came, a replacement rope when the last raid frayed the old, a chef's pay for the men so the cooking stayed decent. Our conversation was arithmetic without sums; we used the words of risk instead of figures because the figures were ours and private. I made them count what mattered in boats: bread, repair, warm beds for the winter. "We are worth more intact than a spindle is worth to a council who will not look us in the eye," Yusuf said. It was not bravado. It was prudence.

I told them plainly: I keep the Qitaf and my command. I will not send them to a death she did not need to take. I had told Azraq's envoy that his agents would keep their hands off the ship while she was mine. I meant it. Saying it did not change the binding at my throat, but it shaped what I could bargain away and what I could not.

They watched me say it. Saying a thing to a man is different from swearing it to a god; men can hold you to what you said. I wanted them to anchor to that sentence so none of us could drift into an excuse later.

Still, the amulet made itself remembered. Every time the thought of leaving to save my own skin uncoiled, a hot pricking woke along my ribs. It was a small tyranny, mechanical and exact. Pain is tidy that way. It made the choice a calculation: I would go because my throat was given no good alternative.

There was a softer, fiercer promise under the calculation. "If we do this," I said, and the map's paper scraped under my palm, "we do it because we have to, and because we will do it in a way that keeps what cannot be replaced. We bring the Spindle back if it lets us, but not at the cost of the Qitaf. Not at the cost of you lot down there."

A low laugh came from the cockroach of a man who had been in the mizzen the night the roof of a warehouse had caved. It was not brave. It was the familiarity of people who knew their captain's softness as well as her stubbornness. Yusuf nodded once.

Preparations followed talk as obediently as tides follow the moon. We made the Qitaf ready to go: canvas lashed, lines checked, the hold stowed. Lanterns were trimmed and hung where they would not blow out, the bilge was run, the anchor's windlass oiled. Men I had not named by name moved with the certainty of people who have done the same routine a hundred times and know every error by heart. I handed the folio back to Yusuf and said, "Keep a copy of where we think the reefs lie. We'll trust nothing we are not ready to forgive when it bites." He folded the map and kept it under his arm like a thing to be guarded.

Before we dropped the last of the ropes I ran my fingers over the silver-blue glass at my throat. The amulet was patient as a storm and as indifferent. It would not be reasoned into forgetting. It would only be moved by the two things Azraq had named: the witches' craft or the Spindle itself. That was a future I could not yet buy, but it was a road we had to walk.

We cast off at a time that was not proud nor theatrical; there was no trumpeting. The Qitaf slid from the berth with the mild indignity of something who did the same thing every day and had learned to be graceful about it. The city fell behind, thin as a breath, and I kept watch on the horizon with the worn map between my knees. I had a crew who knew what they had signed up for and a binding at my throat that would remind me of the price of answering a Peri's call.
