### world-rules  <world/rules.yaml>

These govern what is TRUE. They do not say what anyone KNOWS.
A character knows a rule only where canon says so; otherwise they may be ignorant of it, wrong about it, or in the middle of working it out — and if the story is about someone discovering one of these, writing it into their thoughts early is how that story gets destroyed.

- Peri are immortal and can bind mortals with tokens that enforce obedience in graduated stages; the token cannot be destroyed by ordinary means but can be 'unbound' by specific fate-work.
- The Spindle can rewrite fate only in precise, threadlike edits. Every change demands a proportional cost: a memory, a bond, a trade of consequence that cannot be fabricated.
- The Island of Qarrah enforces inescapability by altering memory, geography, and perception; physical exit is possible only when memory-anchors remain or fate-work alters the island's weave.
- Witches of Qarrah practice confined fate-work (repairs and calibrations) and guard artifacts. They will not allow wholesale erasure of whole peoples or broad cosmic retcons.
- Peri bargains are durable across time; their enforcement is metaphysical and not subject to mortal courts, but the principles of exchange (offer, counteroffer, price) are consistent and can be exploited by skilled fate-work.
- Magic has rules and friction: artifacts like the Spindle are not omnipotent; the more ambitious the rewrite, the more localized and invasive the cost. Miracles are narrow and expensive.

### previous-scene  <novel/chapters/ch-01/scenes/s-003.md>

The place the Peri called a council did not feel like a room so much as a pause the world had taken to listen. Amina stepped from the market's heat and found a floor that drank light, a row of seats that hung like ribs, and a ceiling threaded with a slow-feathered wind that smelled faintly of salt and old paper. The air tightened the way a sail does before a tack; it held her shape and asked her to be smaller in it.

They sat in a circle with nothing between them but the hush. Wings were not banners here so much as instruments: Azad's were the length of a master's cloak, silver-edged and precise; Sahr's were narrower and darker, folded close like a ledger. Azad smiled as if remembering a joke he had told the stars, and when he spoke the sound of his voice unpicked the hush like a hand drawing a thread.

"Captain Amina al-Sirafi," he said, ringed fingers making a courteous arc, "the Peri Council chooses you."

The words were not a question. They landed on Amina as if slid from one palm to another. She might have laughed, if laughter were something to trade, but instead she tasted copper and the old salt of the quay and remembered a child's paper boat. The ghost stood at the edge of her sight, less a figure than a tug at a seam. Hold the line, Mina, it said, and the voice was the same small keel that had kept her steady in storms.

Sahr did not smile. She had the stillness of a ledger finally closed and the exactness of a sentence copied three times over. From a fold in her robe she drew a disc of silver, not coin-flat but feather-etched, and held it between finger and thumb like proof that accounts could be kept by hand.

She spoke the binding as if reading the hours: "By feather and ledger, by promise and keeping: in the name of the Council and the mark we keep, you shall be bound to the token's counting. Bring us the Spindle from Qarrah and the clasp shall be loosed. Fail, and the clasp will take a currency you cannot coin without giving more than you own."

The words themselves were invention—legal, final, with the kind of specificity that turns threats into contracts. Azad stepped forward and unfurled something on his knee: a ragged chart, paper gone grey at the edges, lines like migrating currents drawn in ink that breathed. The island's name sat on the paper like a bruise: Qarrah.

He tapped a path with a finger that left no wet mark but seemed for a moment to pull the map's lines toward his touch. "These are the ways," he said. "The chart tells you where the currents bend to what the island makes of memory. Take it; it will point and it will lie in equal measure."

The map warmed in her hands, not like cloth but like a pocket of summer wind: the trace of currents left a faint salt on her palm. When she looked up, Sahr's thumb was already at the silver disc again. "For the pledge," she said, and pressed it to Amina's throat.

The touch was small and clean. A glassy warmth ran under Amina's skin and struck the back of her teeth; a metallic taste flooded her mouth like tidewater. The pressure that followed did not crush but numbered: it counted each breath and laid a thread against the hollow of her collarbone. It was as if the token took inventory of a promise, and every time Amina thought refusal the token made a small, patient reply.

She felt it in the way one feels a rope go taut at a man's grip: the certainty of being held and the knowledge that the hand would not slacken because it had law behind it. The token's pressure did not crush; it counted. When she swallowed the taste of iron, she knew the binding was more than threat. It marked her.

Azad's smile softened into the kind of attention that made danger look like etiquette. "We do not bargain for sport, Captain. The Spindle is kept on Qarrah. Bring it here. You will be unbound. You will not bring it—" He let the alternative hang like a ship's keel catching a shoal.

Amina thought of the Simorgh's deck and of Laila's hands on the ropes. She thought of Gul's voice at the market and the way the coin had flashed. A ghost tugged at her ribs with that phrase she kept like a stitch. Hold the line, Mina. The words steadied her like one last reefing.

She opened her mouth to refuse; the token replied with the same small, inexorable arithmetic. Sahr's eyes did not leave her face. "These are our terms," she said. "You carry the map; you carry the clasp. The Council's patience is a ledger that accrues, Captain. Do the accounting."

Bargain was the wrong word for what happened next. Azad inclined his head in a motion that felt like both invitation and sealing. He pressed the map into her hands with a gentleness that felt like a loan and touched the rim of the silver disc as if bestowing an obligation.

When she left—because leaving was the only motion permitted to her by the shape the chamber had made—Amina's palm still held the map and the token hummed against her throat with a fine, quiet insistence. The ghost's voice came again, soft and nearer: Hold the line, Mina. She held it. She did not promise; she closed her jaw and counted the cost instead.

Outside, the quay's smell found her as if nothing had moved at all. The market's cries would go on, ignorant and beautiful. Inside her, the token kept its account.
