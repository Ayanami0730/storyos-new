### world-rules  <world/rules.yaml>

These govern what is TRUE. They do not say what anyone KNOWS.
A character knows a rule only where canon says so; otherwise they may be ignorant of it, wrong about it, or in the middle of working it out — and if the story is about someone discovering one of these, writing it into their thoughts early is how that story gets destroyed.

- Peri are effectively immortal and powerful, but they are bound by oaths, covenants, and the binding artifacts they make; they cannot simply will away mortal free will without legalistic or talismanic devices.
- Isle Jarrah is subject to the witches' fate-weaving: ordinary physical exit is barred by the island's enchantments. Leaving is possible only through specific bargains, rituals, or by altering the island's weaving from within.
- The Spindle can rewrite individual strands of fate — it can alter probabilities, choices, and the course of a single life-line — but every stitch or unpicking requires an exact metaphysical cost: something equivalent must be unmade, taken, or exchanged. It cannot resurrect the truly dead nor change cosmic constants.
- Bargains, names and promises have power. Spoken deals (explicit bargains) made in the island's terms are binding and will be enforced by the island and its practitioners; breaking an explicit bargain creates supernatural consequences.
- The Peri binding amulet enforces compliance but is not absolute: it can be resisted by strong mortal will, counter-magic from the witches, or by using the Spindle under the island's rules — each of those options will carry costs and tradeoffs.
- Magic in this world preserves a balance: gains require losses, and shortcuts to absolute gain (immortality, absolute control of many lives) are blocked by the weave of the world. Characters may negotiate or reroute the balance, but they cannot create value ex nihilo.

### previous-scene  <novel/chapters/ch-01/scenes/s-002.md>

I had not expected to find the answer to an immortal's patience in the thin scrape of a fingernail. Azraq had sat on my crate like an accusation, and when the light hit the silver at his throat it made the boards look cheap. He spoke without hurry, as if explaining the weather, but everything in the room tightened to hear him.

He uncapped nothing; he only unfolded the map again, letting the paper look ragged and earnest in the brazier's glow. "The Council wants one thing," he said. His voice was small enough to be cruel. "A single spindle."

He said the word and let it sit between us. I had heard it before—the mission phrased out of the wreck-map that had bled into the folio a day earlier—but the second time it landed differently, like a nettle placed on the palm. "The Spindle," I repeated, because names were the handles you used on troubles. "From Isle Jarrah."

He nodded. "Isle Jarrah. The island with its own rules. The witches' work is there. We cannot—must not—enter their loom. We require a mortal hand."

Here was the part I had expected and the part I had not. The expected was the terse clarity of what they wanted; the unexpected was how little else he would say. He explained that the Peri Council had reasons; he would not make them mine. "Our reasons are not for your keeping," he said. "They are urgent. You must understand urgency without understanding why."

I had been a woman who measured bargains in coin and in the lives that coin bought. Urgency dressed up as a fact would not have moved me on its own. But urgency said aloud by a Peri did not feel like a fact so much as a law: it bent the air.

"And if we refuse?" Yusuf asked. His voice had the bluntness of a man who preferred problems with edges. He did not ask aloud what I had already been thinking—the kind of avarice that asked whether you could sell your life cheap and still sleep—but he wanted to know the shape of the answer.

Azraq's hands, pale as bone, did not tremble. "Refusal will be answered," he said. He did not explain the answer. He let the silence do the rest. "It will be answered by covenant. By consequence. By measures that make refusal a worse balance than acceptance."

The words did not tell me what that balance was. They were a promise of consequence and nothing more. That was a kind of threat the sea understood by smell, and I remembered the look on Rasim's face when I first told him we chased storms for work.

I had started to ask whether "answered" meant exile, or curses on cargo, or worse. Instead, I reached for the amulet at my throat like a woman checking a burn. The silver-blue glass sat cold on my skin where Azraq had locked it shut. When I thought about prying it loose, a quick, hot thing stabbed under my ribs as if someone had found the seam of me and slackened it.

Pain was a blunt instrument. It did more than fold my hands for a moment; it argued a law into my head. My fingers went clumsy, like a man who had his tools taken away, and the room reeled for a breath.

Azraq watched me with that same thin patience. "It is binding," he said. "It enforces compliance. Resistance is costly."

I tried to make a joke because anger liked air, but the amulet's tug made my laugh break in half. "And unbinding?" I asked. "Is there a way round a peri's glue?"

He leaned forward then, the only movement that suggested interest. "Not none. Not absolute. The instrument we affix is a law, not a god. It can be resisted by a strong mortal will, it can be countered by the witches' craft, and it can be made irrelevant by what you seek—the Spindle—if used under the island's terms."

Those three things landed like possible exits: will, witchcraft, the Spindle. I imagined the dark needle of the spindle altering the stitch that had closed my throat and the idea made my mouth dry. The Peri had just named the tools that could unmake their leash. That was information. It changed the shape of my anger into a kind of calculation.

"You cannot do what you ask because you are unwilling," Azraq said at last, answering a question he had not been asked. "Not because you lack hands. The Council cannot thread the island's craft without breaking its own laws. That is why you, a mortal with a ship and crew, are the instrument."

There was still more he would not say. He would not speak of what the Peri would gain, only that it was necessary and not the kind of necessity it would be polite to explain. The opacity of it was part of their power; they could claim moral distance by refusing to name the ends.

I thought of the crew below, of Yusuf's slow, ledgered calculations, of Rasim's hands on the wheel and the small jokes he hid in the ropes. I thought of the groans that had come up from the hold after the raid and the men who would not wake if I sold them some dangerous notion of freedom for a better future. The amulet bit a second time when my thought bent toward a way out that involved walking away; the spike had a memory like a compass—that if I pulled from a binding, the bond would remind me why it had been there.

"You want me to go," I said. Saying it made the word solid. "You want the Spindle from Isle Jarrah. You have bound me to that task. I will go. But I go to keep my crew. I keep my command. Your enforcement stays on my throat, but your hands stay out of my ship while she is mine."

Azraq's silver eyes did not soften, but the two corners of his mouth shifted the littlest. "You keep them, within the terms the Council sets," he said. "And you will deliver."

That was the turn: a knife of acceptance and the rust on its blade. I had not been proud of every bargain I had struck in my years, but I had kept my promise to the men who sailed under me, and I had meant to keep this one. The room smelled of boiled coffee and tar; the map looked like someone else's dying hope, a thing neither dead nor alive. Azraq folded the folio back into itself and let it go.

When he left, the amulet hummed once against my skin—a small, private sound. It was not a scream, nor was it a benediction. It was a machine setting itself to work.

I had a map, a crew, and a binding on my throat. I had also, now, a sliver of knowledge: that the very thing the Council wanted might also be the thing that could make the Council's hold useless. That thought was a weight and a blade both.
