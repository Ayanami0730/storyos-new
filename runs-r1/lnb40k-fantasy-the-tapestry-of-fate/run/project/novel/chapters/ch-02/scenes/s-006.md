The sea did something you could feel in your teeth before it gave you a sight to match it: a fold that was not a wave so much as a hinge in the water, a quick, obscene doubling where the Qitaf's bow met a second skin of sea and the two moved for a breath as if to replace one another. I had felt enough wrong seas to name this one by the set of small betrayals it kept—light gone a degree ahead of us, a swell that smelled of old oil—but the way the world folded stopped me in my work.

The compass needle spent a breath where it had been still, then trembled and wandered like a thirsty thing. Binnacle glass went white and then clear as if someone had exhaled between us and the world. The chronometer on the rail juddered; its second hand missed, caught, and then stuttered on as if time itself had hiccuped. These were not the polite errors of weather to be calmed with a rope and patience; they looked like the sea making a decision.

Yusuf kept his thumb on the folio when the instruments betrayed us, as if touching paper could anchor a needle. He did not open it at first—he would not let the map speak while the deck was noisy with broken things—but when he did, the smudged crosses fell under our course like a second chart: the little dark Xs lined where the sea folded, exactly where my hands felt the deck dip. I did not read the map as new; I read my own memory against it, and the remembered lines in the wreck log that had no proper words left but a torn diagram in my head. The jagged fold in the water matched the torn diagram I'd smoothed from the wreck log.

Azraq watched from aft as if a pale, patient eye hung where the rigging met the fog, sweeping the horizon with a glance that made the deck feel measured. When he looked my way the amulet at my throat answered: a thin, precise thrum, the sort that sat under the skin and made the bone remember. It had the quality of law: quiet, implacable, and not mine. The thrum did not dictate sail trim, but it counted the cost of a choice the way a ledger counts coin.

Rasim's cough cut into the moment like a small bell. He held the wheel with both hands until the veins stood in them; the cough took a bit more from him each time and left him paler around the mouth. He tried to laugh it off, but the sound came out thin. "Keep her head to the swell," I told him, not because the compass said so but because the swell could be listened to with the body. He wheezed, coughed, bent his head back and took the order, one more thin man following a thin order on a narrower deck.

The crew moved with a single, urgent economy. Men re-tied lines with fingers that smelled of tar and fear; a pair shifted a coil of chain so we rode a half degree to starboard; someone lashed a canvas that had started to flog. I eased a sheet, then another, and told Yusuf to watch the folio but not to speak its crosses aloud. "Not here," I said—my voice had to be a task—and he folded the paper like a thing that might be carried into a grave. "Pull left on my call," I told Rasim, and when the sea folded again I felt the Qitaf answer through her timbers.

The reckoning was one of hands, not of instruments. The chronometer stuttered twice more and then kept time doggedly; the binnacle fogged and cleared; the needle spun a many-pointed hesitation. We compensated with the only things that had never failed us: sail trim, weight, and the small human measures of seamanship. I eased the sheet, shifted a coil of chain to starboard, and told Rasim to steer to the swell with his ear more than his eye.

It felt like reading a body. The Qitaf answered the weight and the cloth and the placement of crew like a patient horse. Yusuf kept his thumb along the folio's gutter as if it were a rosary, and in the quiet between the folds I heard the wreck-log memory kick at the back of my skull—no words, only shapes: the jag of a diagram, the suggestion that the island did not merely lie where the map pointed but made the sea do its lying for it. I had no right to make the wreck man's scrawl into a law, but the fold was a proof that did not need ink.

The folio itself felt like a body that had been handled too much. Pencil lines softened to scrawl; smudges had turned decisive marks into blind gestures. The marginal Xs, dark and blurred, were like teeth marks on a shore I had not seen in living memory; where they clustered the water felt heavy and cold under our feet. Yusuf traced one with his finger and flinched like a man touching a scar. "They mark the falls," he said, and then corrected himself into silence. Whether he meant the charts or the wreck nobody could tell, and that was all right. Not everything needed naming for us to act.

Azraq's eyes moved with the ease of something that had no need to pretend—an appraisal that did not seek our consent and did not ask for it. The amulet thumped when he tilted his head; I felt it in the hollow of my throat the way one feels a far drumbeat. It made the choice sharper: obey, bargain, break—none of them costless. For now my choice was immediate and small: keep the ship, keep the men, keep a map folded under a coat.

Rasim coughed again as we came through a shallow curl and his breath came out like a small surrender. I thought of the Qitaf as a single thing we all lent ourselves to—its hull and our habits braided—because the instruments had become unreliable and our bodies were the only machine left wholly our own. The men worked with hands that knew their jobs better than their mouths, a tight, moving thing that kept the Qitaf a single body.

Then, a hand's-breadth of wood—salt-soft, heat-scrambled, flecked with old black tar—bobbled against our hull and scraped past. It spun away with the haze and vanished as if swallowed by the same fold that had flipped the sea. For a moment no one spoke; the sight of flotsam is a small, private argument with the world. My throat tightened. The map had the crosses; the wreck had its half-diagram; the instruments had their sudden forgettings; and a piece of wood, carried by whatever made the sea fold, came close enough to touch.

I told Rasim to keep steering by the sound of the swell. "Watch for anything else," I said to Yusuf. The amulet at my throat kept its quiet time; Azraq's eye did not break its sweep. The island was near enough now that the fog tasted of it, a metallic salt that could have been anything—iron, old promises, the witches' loom. We had been delivered moments enough to choose which debts to spend.
