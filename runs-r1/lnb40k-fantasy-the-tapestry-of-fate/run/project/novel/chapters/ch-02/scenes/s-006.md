They slipped the ropes loose before the sun had fully cleared its throat above Port Rahim, and the Simorgh answered like a body remembering its shape. Amina stood on the quarterdeck with the map pressed flat beneath her palm, the paper warm through the shirt as if some small tide ran through the fiber. The smell of tar and rope and the ship's particular cumin hung close; the world narrowed to wind and the paper and the slow arithmetic at her throat.

The map warmed and pulled at its inked lines under her palm as if feeling for currents. She let her thumb rest on a narrow ribbon of ink the map had drawn thin and pale: One ribbon the map marked Eshra's Thread, a pale line that ran like a finger toward the west, and Amina could feel, absurdly and usefully, the suggestion of its direction under her skin. She had been given a chart that did not only show routes; it sniffed the sea.

Ibrahim rode the helm with the same small rituals he'd kept since she first let him near the wheel—thumb tracing knots, a hum at the back of his mouth. He glanced at her when the map's lines tugged. "The birds are wrong," he said, eyes on the sky. "They're skimming sideways, like they're reading a different map." He pointed to a handful of terns that rode the glass; their wings tossed without finding land. The comment was a lantern; she answered with the plan she had already set in her head.

They slipped their berth and took the sea. The port fell away in a smear of roof and waving men; ropes creaked into work and the yards filled. The Simorgh rode the first odd tide the map led them toward—Eshra's Thread unfurling like an instruction across the paper. Amina kept the map under her shirt, fingers touching the warm corner, feeling the map's attention as if it were a second compass.

When the light changed on the water she knew it before anyone else named it. Lights rose off the water in a whirl—silver-blue motes that feathered outward and trailed like shaken ash. For a slant of breath the deck turned narrow; the world pulled itself toward that brightness.

The token at her throat tightened as the lights drew close, a pressure that counted her breaths quicker. She felt the counting in the hollow of her collarbone and, as always, the pressure translated to a ledger she could hold: the council measured; she owed. The token's glassy warmth slid under the kerchief and pinched in response. It had been a private thing back in the council chamber; here it registered the Peri's presence as readily as any man. Between the map and the token, Azad's reach sat on her shoulders like a ledger she could not fold away.

The first touch of the whirl wasted no time on politeness. Lines of light braided the sea like fishing nets and looped at the hull; the halyards thudded and ran as if unseen hands had threaded through them. The ropes writhed as if a wind ran through their fibers and the yards answered with a sound she had not heard at sea before—a soft, feathered scraping that set teeth on edge. Ibrahim's compass needle shivered and refused to settle. It swung like a thing uncertain of which north to obey.

"Hard alee!" she called, and her voice was the only thing aboard that did not falter. The Simorgh came to better trim; Ibrahim caught the wheel and let the lee fall in. She watched the crew move with the careful speed of people who knew not to trust a miracle. Hands went to blocks, to belaying pins; a man went to the fore and sang an old cutting-line as if song steadied wood and mind.

The lights did not strike with claws or heat. They prodded and tested: tugging at compass needles, feathering the ropes, running under the planks like a tide that would not lift. When one moted strand licked the rail, it did not burn; it tasted of old salt and stories, and for an instant Amina could have sworn she heard a voice folded in it—Azad's voice, smooth as ledger ink and as indifferent. The thought flashed and dimmed. Ghosts did not speak plainly on the wind.

Her ghost—soft-voiced, the sibling who had filled so many endings—was there in the seam of her attention. "Hold the line, Mina," it said, as if from the belly of the sea itself, and the phrase folded around her like ballast. She answered with the work of hands.

They met the supernatural with seamanship. Where the lights tugged the ropes Amina sent men to pay and tie, to ease rather than resist; where the needle wavered she ordered them to trust sight and sound rather than iron and to steer by the birds' odd flight when the needle lied. Ibrahim shut his lips and steered with a thumb-writing of knots, a small truth. The Simorgh was a shape made of people as much as of wood, and its skin was their practiced habit.

For a while the whirl braided them with no sign of malice. It felt like a test—Peri work never wasted effort on brute force; it proded the edges of what a ship could be made to do. The map under her shirt warmed where the Eshra's Thread ran, and the ink seemed to brighten under her palm as if it had agreed to tell her one honest thing even as the sea lied in other ways.

Ibrahim laughed once, a short sound that was more a release than a joke. "They're curious," he said. "Like children with a new toy. They don't know what to take, so they pinch at the edges. We'll give them nothing." His confidence was a rope tied to the rail; she made a note of it.

When the lights finally drew back they did so like a tide folding over itself, leaving a scatter of motes across the dark water that winked and were gone. The halyards slowed their running. The compass eased into a grudging point. The Simorgh carried on, not unbruised but whole, the crew holding their positions with the same stubbornness that had kept them through storms and taxes and needlework of sail.

Amina slid the map from beneath her shirt when she could finally move her fingers without waiting for the token's slow arithmetic to settle. The paper had lost none of its heat and the pale line of Eshra's Thread looked like a scar the sea could not wash out. She cupped the map in one hand and felt—she could not know if it was the map or the token or the memory that shaped the moment—the council watching with an appraising patience.

She spoke then, quiet enough that the sound belonged to the Simorgh rather than to the sea. "We don't turn back," she said. "Not for lights and not for counting. Follow the Thread. Keep to the map's suggestion and keep your heads."

Ibrahim answered with a nod and a knot under his thumb. The ghost said nothing further; it did not need to. The Simorgh had met its first test, and the sea—made stranger by Peri hands—had taught them the same lesson that port had: the Peri measured, and the price would be exact. The map had tugged them onto a path named Eshra's Thread; the token had tightened and then eased. The voyage had begun in earnest.

When the sun leaned higher, the port behind them looked like a note in a book. Ahead, the pale ribbon of the map unspooled. Amina wrapped the map back into her shirt and rolled the braid on her wrist like a knot she'd already tightened. The math at her throat kept its quiet count. The Peri's ledger waited, and she would meet it on terms she could hold.
