Amina left the Simorgh as if she were stepping from one weather into another. The heat of the deck eased into the market's reflux: a wall of smells and voices, hot tar and rope diluting into stall smoke and the sharp brightness of citrus. Gulls threaded the air like coins and the bell from the quay rang on without a clapper, a hollow sound that made the traders talk faster.

Stalls shipped stacks of saffron, sieves of sumac, strings of dried lime, and the familiar brown smell of cumin. Cloths hung in a careless rainbow: indigo-dyed linen, saffron-yellow silk, and the coarse hemp burlap of sailors' sacks. A chorus of cries rose and fell: 'Dried lime! Maps and charts! Brass that remembers the sea!'

Above the bustle, a notice board showed the Harbormaster's seal; Rashid ibn Nuri's hand still made the rules here, by reputation if not always by law.

Gul's stall sat on the end of a row, the kind of neat jumble that read like an advertisement for careful hands. Under a patched blue awning his wares hung on hooks and lines above a low counter of crates. Gul's stall was a neat jumble of brass trinkets, sea-glass, folded maps, and jars of preserved lemons. He himself was small-boned, quick-eyed, and given to a half-smile that stopped just short of friendship.

He greeted Amina with a half-smile, palms open as if displaying not goods but trust, and his thumb stroked the edge of a brass compass with a practiced, idle motion. They had done business before—not much, a broken astrolabe traded for three jars of lemons—but familiarity, not friendship, sat between them. He tipped a cup of tea toward her as if to show she could sit; she let the heat move her instead, hands loose at her sides.

The market moved like tide under feet: bodies leaning, wares shifting, a child's cry folding into an old man's laugh. Amina felt the ghost in the marrow, a soft pressure at the back of her teeth like a thumb on a string. Hold the line, Mina, the voice said as plainly as a hand on her shoulder. She folded the whisper into the same pocket she kept her compass in and kept walking.

A flash of silver broke the hedgerow of shoulders—a disc, feather-engraved, caught and gone between a pair of fingers. Someone jostled past the stall and the coin wobbled in a fist before it disappeared into a ragged sleeve. Gul's smile thinned.

"That's no ordinary coin—see the feathered cut," he said, and his voice fell like a sail. He leaned forward, palms flat on a crate, and looked her in the eye as if measuring tides. "Keep your pockets and your promises, captain. When the peri come to bargain, count your teeth." His quip landed in the space between them sharp as a thrown bone.

Around them the market's talk shifted: names dropped like small coins. Azad. Sahr. Men who sold fish and men who sold knots spoke the names quick and low, the syllables clipped as if the wind itself might carry them away.

Amina's answer was a narrowed look rather than words. Then she asked, quietly, so that only Gul and the crate of preserved lemons heard: "How do you know?"

Gul's fingers paused on the brass rim of a compass. He blinked like a man dividing the sea into remembered routes. "A silver flash at dawn on the outer quay, like a gull with no shadow," he said. His voice was lower after that, the sort a man used when naming a thing that made him uneasy. A hawker halfway through a cry coughed and then fell quiet; a child stopped chasing a paper boat and watched them with sudden intent.

Her fingers closed on the coin on the counter. She did not take it; she turned it once between thumb and forefinger so the feathered cut glinted, and then she laid it back down with less care than she had picked it up. Her fingers found the outline of her compass beneath her coat and tightened, a private loop of muscle she did not bother to hide. For a heartbeat her cheek tightened too, as if she had been given a wind to tack on.

Gul watched that small change like a man who knew the worth of a tightened hand. "They come like weather," he said, softer. "Quiet first, then every tongue in the market knows the forecast. Keep your purse closed, Captain. If they ask for more than coin, don't hand it over." He tapped a finger against the crate as if making a ledger entry with the motion.

She let the warning sit between them like a knot undone and not yet threaded. When she moved on, she left a coin on Gul's counter and the memory of his warning like a folded map in her hand. The city's heat pressed back up around her, and she walked toward the quay again with the knowledge that stories had become the sort of cargo that could sink you if you loaded them into your hull.

At the edge of the market a child chased a paper boat into a puddle and an old woman called the child's name with the kind of gentleness that belonged only to things that had outlived their fear. Between those two movements the world kept bargaining with itself—some deals were small, some were not; some names were safe to say, others were coins you did not toss in the open.
