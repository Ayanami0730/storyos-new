The market smelled of sun-baked spice and old tar; men shouted over yards of cloth and the bellows of the fishmongers spat a salt that hung on the back of my throat. I moved through it like a woman counting coins she meant to spend—quick hands, brief smiles, a face that said business and nothing more. We could not afford vanity in our purchases. We could afford only what would keep hull and hand from failing us at sea.

I bought oil that wouldn't blacken the wicks in a storm, wax to bed the seams, a coil of good line that felt like it might outlive a man's patience, and a wrapped bundle of bitter herbs the apothecary said would steady a coughing chest. The herbs were for Rasim, because Yusuf had said last night, flat and clean, that if we lost the Qitaf then Rasim's cough would go without a salve and the rest of us would sleep on harder things. I kept my hand on the coins until the merchant's fingers were closed, then let the night swallow the small trade and the change.

When I came back to the ship the deck smelled different—sea and tar and the up-throb of men waiting for a rhythm—and the folio lay open on the hatch like a private thing left unattended. Yusuf was already there, sleeves hitched, the map loosened between his palms as if he were balancing a thought.

They came in twos and threes, blinking the market dust from their eyes. Rasim pulled his coat tighter and coughed once as he took the top step, like a man testing whether he could keep standing if the world decided to press on him. A handful of faces leaned over the paper while lantern-light pooled on its folded edge.

We'd already argued the numbers; there was nothing to be gained by saying them again. The map and the log were what we had instead: paper that whispered of mistakes people had left behind. The folio had been handled so much that the coastline had gone soft where hands had smudged it; there were crosses darkened into blurs and pencil lines that started with confidence and ended in a scrawl. It suggested navigation rather than instructions—finger-thin errors sketched in ink, not the sort of handwriting you followed without a rope and a prayer.

A man—one I had seen at the mizzen, his face a shadowed thing—leaned until his knuckle nearly brushed a smudged cross. He pointed with a thumb that shook. "Men who left and never found the sea again," he muttered, the words so small I could have missed them if I hadn't been listening for anything that might stitch the rest of the night together.

Yusuf looked at the same blur and tapped the paper with a forefinger, as if feeling for the weakness of a seam. He folded the folio and slipped it under his arm like a thing to be guarded. "Keep it hidden—no curious eyes," he said, low enough that the map and the men and the sea between us could all be part of the same secret.

The wreck log lay beside the folio, folded smaller and worse for the sea. The diagram in it had been eaten by a torn edge; what was left looked like a hand that had tried to keep something from going away. Words bled at their margins; an attempt at a phrase had been scrubbed to a smudge and the paper had the desperate weight of a man who had run out of time.

They traded lines of talk over the two papers like sailors trading weather. "Reefs that move when you ain't looking. Lights that call a man off his course," someone said, and it was the sort of thing men said when they wanted to make danger sound less like fate and more like a mistake you could plan for.

Rasim's cough came again, harder this time. He reached for the coin I'd handed him and it slipped from his fingers, clicking once on the deck and skittering toward the hatch. Yusuf's boot caught it with a practised sweep; I caught Rasim's hand and dropped the bitter herb bundle into it. The smallness of it—two coins, a bundle of herbs, a steadying palm—was more expensive than any loud speech.

I felt the amulet at my throat prickle, a small and accurate pain. The world had rules I did not want broken; the amulet's law made them very clear. I answered without theatrics. "Azraq can fasten the council to my throat," I said, "but he cannot fasten my crew to the deck. That's mine to keep."

We prepared in the way that keeps a ship alive: the canvas rolled and lashed, lanterns trimmed so they would not gutter, a rope checked with a sailor's thumb. I gave a nod to Yusuf—firm, quick—and he looked at me the way a man looks at another when an oath is being kept between them. The Qitaf did what ships do when their captain decides a course: she learned her lines and braced herself.

I folded my hands on the rail and watched the city go thin as a moth's wing. "We would leave at dawn," I told the men, the plan small and sharp. Yusuf slipped the folio beneath his jacket and went to the binnacle to check the compasses; he moved like a man with a secret and a duty both. I told the men to stand ready to cast off.

A gull called once and the sound stole the last of the market's noise from my ears. The map was tucked, the stores stowed, and the men moved into the small, stubborn rituals that keep a ship together. When I walked the deck for the last check, my thumb brushed the amulet and the glass under it felt like a cold eye. I gave the word to stand by. It was a simple order. It was the one that would let us stop being talk and be a ship again.
