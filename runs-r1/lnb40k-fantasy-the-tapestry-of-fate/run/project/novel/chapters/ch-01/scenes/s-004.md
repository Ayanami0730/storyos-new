She woke to the ship's bones. The narrow aft cabin beneath the quarterdeck held her like a cupped palm; the beams leaned in close and the morning came as a thin wedge through a shutter.

The token was a silver disc, feather-etched, resting against her skin and catching the slant of light. A glassy warmth slid beneath her collar; a metallic taste flooded her mouth like cold sea. It did not crush—its force was arithmetic, not violence. She could feel each breath tracked as if a hand moved bead after bead along a string. The hum at her collarbone was intimate, a private counting; it did not speak aloud and no one on deck could hear it.

Laila was on the quarterdeck when Amina climbed the short companionway, patched canvas trousers tucked to the calf, a dark waistcoat that had seen too many storms, and a faded kerchief knotted at her throat. Her hands moved by habit—counting lengths of rope two by two—while her face rehearsed the shape of anger. She had been roused; the way she stood said she had refused to be half-asleep for what mattered.

"You let them lay that on you?" Laila asked before the words smoothed into anything gentler. It was disbelief braided thinnest with accusation. "You let them press it to your throat?"

Amina sat on the ladder's lip and, in the small rocking world of the ship, gave the facts without theatre: the council had chosen her; Azad and Sahr had spoken as if signing accounts; a map had been given and the disc had been pressed to her skin. She kept the council's hush out of it—none of that needed retelling as wonder—only what the ledger said and what was now pinned to her.

Laila's mouth shaped the beats Amina had learned to read in a dozen ports: sharp surprise, the hard snap of betrayal, and then the colder edge of worry for the crew. She walked a short measure, counting ropes under her breath to keep her hands from striking. "You should've told me, Mina. You never tell me. If they can reach you, they can reach what rides with you. We'll have to move the manifests, burn what we do not want found—"

Amina cut her off with a look; she could hear the old words in Laila's mouth but she could not let the plan be simpler than it was. "They did not press it for sport," she said. She let her fingers rest on the compass at her breast, the brass warm from being kept close. The ragged chart Azad had folded into her palm was still in her shirt; when she eased it out the paper smelled faintly of salt and something like old ink. Lines on the map tugged at her thumb as if unwilling to be followed straightforwardly—currents that promised direction and tricks in the same breath.

Laila shoved a coil of rope against the rail and counted again because counting helped make future regular. "So what's the play? Run? Sail away from their reach? Hide the map?" Her voice had the hurry of someone who wanted the answer now because answers made danger tangible.

"Destroying the token won't unmake the ledger," Amina said. The token had been pressed to her throat with a ritual that smelled faintly of feathers and ledgers; she felt its arithmetic inside her. She ran through the thought like one reads a bad tide: the Peri's enforcement came in degrees. First, subtle measurement and warnings; then, if the account demanded it, a private thing might be taken—something tucked in the mind; and, finally, a bond might be unhooked from the person who loved you. The idea hollowed her for a breath.

The ghost tugged at that hollow with a phrase she'd kept since childhood. Hold the line, Mina, it said, not counsel so much as an old seam in the hull that took pressure without splitting. The few words steadied her the way a reef steadies a mast.

Laila's anger folded into a sharp, practical fear. "Qarrah," she said, the name a raw sound. "They sent you to Qarrah? You know what they say. That island eats the way a thing remembers itself. None of us come back whole, if we come back at all."

Amina's hand went to the map; Azad's touch when he handed it over was in her memory—gentle as a bridle, precise. The chart's ink suggested paths, but she had felt the map's two-facedness in the council: it pointed, but it could not be trusted to mean only what it said. She let the possibilities press together in her mind—return the spindle and be loosed, or refuse and risk the council taking something harder than coin.

She thought of the Simorgh in detail: Nasir muttering curses into the binnacle as he wrestled a leeching block, Ibrahim's narrow shoulders bent to the wheel while he learned how the ship asked to be ridden, the galley woman's small, particular stoop when she pared lemons. These images made a ledger of their own; they were the things she would not trade lightly.

Laila's voice was quieter when she spoke next. "If you go, you'll be taking their bargain. You could be gone from us—changed or taken. Are you sure that's what you want?"

Amina braced her thumb on the small compass and let the needle's tiny certainty answer a question that had no neat ledger. "I will take it," she said finally. "Not for the promise of freedom they offer—I've no faith in their mercy—but because I'll take the terms myself before they take the crew or the ship. For the Simorgh, for the people that live under her, I'll carry their accounting."

The admission was a small thing, heavy as the rope she wound through her fingers. Laila allowed herself one long, steadying count of the ropes—two by two—and then, without welcoming the bargain, folded into it the practical work. "We will hide the map, we'll move the ledgers, we'll lie to anyone who asks. We'll make it so they have to take you and not us."

Amina's jaw set. She slid the map back under her shirt so its warmth pressed against her ribs and felt the token's quiet arithmetic at her throat. The counting was private and patient; it had not yet demanded its larger prices. She pocketed the compass and rolled her braid about her wrist, a small knot set for keeping.

She took the short companionway and stood on the quarterdeck where sunlight found the tarred planks. The smells of the ship—hot tar, rope, the sour of sweat and a faint thread of cumin—rose up like a promise that some things remained the same. She did not make vows aloud; she only made the decision and let it sit like ballast.

Laila set the plan in motion with precision rather than sentiment. "Start with the stores," she said. "No new hands. Tell no one in port. We'll sail on odd tides."

Amina nodded once. The hum at her throat kept counting, patient as a ledger. She straightened, felt the small, steady tug of the ghost's phrase at the seam of her resolve, and stepped into the day's work.

She would go to Qarrah. She would take what they demanded. She would hold the line.
