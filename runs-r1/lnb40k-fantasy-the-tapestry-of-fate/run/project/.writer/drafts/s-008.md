They saw it first as a shape in the fog, a suggestion of land that could have been a mirage if not for the way the sea tightened its ribs around it. The Simorgh ran on a pale ribbon of current and the fog opened, not with a blink but with the slow, animal patience of something that had been doing this for a long time. Black, contorted trees crouched along the shore; pale roots clung to the bank like fingers, and a crown of low stonework rimmed the island's throat. The sight made the deck go quiet in a way the map could not account for.

The fog that wrapped Qarrah was thick and clinging, smelling of damp peat and iron; it clung to cloth like breath. It lifted from the water in a wet, woolly puff that left salt on a man's wrist and the taste of old moss in the mouth. When the Simorgh cut into it, the planks woke as if someone had taken a damp hand and smoothed them: everything nearer smelled of peat, everything farther away smelled of tar and cumin and the ship's own small fires. Amina felt the fog's weight on the back of her neck and remembered why sailors learned to trust the sea and fear the fog in equal measures.

Halfway to the island Laila moved along the rail and palmed a pinch of the ship's salt. She did it without fanfare—small hands were better at ceremonies than loud ones—and said the old name so low only the wood could hear it. "Salt-keep," she said, and the syllables were an intention as much as a charm. She threw the grains into the air and counted them aloud in two soft numbers: "One, two." The sound clattered on the deck the way a coin hits a table; it made a line. When she finished Amina felt the map under her palm answer like a second heartbeat.

It was not a superstition they offered only for comfort. Shoals lay around Qarrah like a jaw. They did not sit still; the sand breathed. The shoals breathed; sandbars lipped and shifted under the keel, and an eddy caught the Simorgh and shoved her sideways before Ibrahim could steady the wheel. Men gripped cleats, hung on boat-hooks, and cursed small curses that sounded like prayer. Nasir swore and shoved a hand along the rail to keep himself upright; the small, practical muscle of his face did not look surprised, only annoyed. "She moves her teeth," he muttered, and that was all the map gave them for an answer.

Ibrahim's compass trembled; its needle wavered and seemed to point at something inland, and the map's lines went grey beneath Amina's thumb as if soot had been rubbed across them. The inked ribbon she had been trusting—Eshra's Thread—pulled faintly, a complaint rather than a command. The Simorgh nosed in toward a lee that the map promised and found the lee folded away. Where the chart had marked a shallow channel the water curled into a small, hungry whirl and pushed them one boat-length toward a rocky lip. For a moment, the ship answered the island's rearranging as though it were a living thing testing a visitor's teeth.

From the crown of stones a green, petal-thin smoke drifted up—a green smoke-petal that unfurled like a moth and hovered over the tide. It did not bloom like fire or fill the air like incense; it was a single, bell-shaped breath of colour that made the men on deck still as a rod. Amina saw the thing and knew it for what it was: a shore-signal. The witches marked their presences the way birds mark territory; the green petal was both greeting and warning. Laila's fingers tightened on the rail; she said, quiet and taut, "They have seen us."

The landing was not a landing but an argument. They fought the shoals, they bled speed into a careful turn, and at the last they trusted to small things: a brace of oars, a boat-hook thrust like a pike, Nasir's hand on a warp. A bowman dropped a warpsman into the water to test the bottom and came back with silt that smelled of iron and years. The Simorgh put her nose to the bank; men scrambled to shove a skiff over and down, and every felt line that was meant to hold pulled tight as if the island were answering their touch. Amina could have ordered them to stay; she did not. There was a business here and a bargain: the Peri had sent her to take what she could and return.

The ghost kept to her as such things do—near, intimate, and utterly private. Only she heard the voice that had loved and lost and left her with a set of orders carved like a habit. "Hold the line, Mina," the voice said, and in the words the old night returned: the lemon peel, the wash of seawater, the slow breath of someone who had been patient with dying. No one else on deck saw the breath of it. Laila's mouth worked on counting numbers; Nasir's hands were a small storm of practical work. The ghost sat against Amina like a coin in a closed fist and gave her a measure.

They marked the crossing in other ways too: Ibrahim hummed under his breath until the wheel slowed its nervous song; Tariq leaned over the map and traced Eshra's Thread like a promise on a palm; Nasir measured the pitch of the mast with a practiced eye and nodded. When the boat went free of the Simorgh and the men lowered themselves over the small water the island did not answer with a door but with the slow rearrangement of its parts. The sand beneath the bow moved an outcrop's breadth to starboard; a tuft of sea-campion that had been visible at one tide had slid out of reach like a thing embarrassed. Their landing was accepted on a transaction of tiny mercies: Laila's salt, the boat-hooks, a precise measure of courage.

Amina kept hold of the map until the last man was over the rail. The ink was paler now, the lines like a memory of ink rather than ink itself. She had felt the map warm at the beginning of the voyage; now it cooled and dimmed where her thumb had rested. She pressed her fingers until the paper did not feel like paper but like a thin skin stretched over a thought. If the island unmade exits by unmaking the names of the exits, then she would go keeping her name in her mouth and in her pocket and in the map that still made a faint, stubborn tug toward the interior.

When the skiff broke the thin green petal of smoke and slid into the shallows, the island watched in a way the sea never did. A single, bone-white gull rose like a question over the crown of stones and the green petal drifted after it like a child's reply. The men climbed and the ropes slipped; Nasir's hand left the rail and came away smelling faintly of peat and metal. Amina folded the ghost and the token under her chin and took one last, slow breath of cumin and iron. Then she stepped off the Simorgh and the island took her in.
