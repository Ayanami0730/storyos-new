They had no business calling it weather. The lights that braided the low horizon were not storm but memory made sea—an eddy of things that were and were not, folding the ship back on to the past like a palm closing on a coin. Amina felt the beginning of it in the marrow of her teeth: a thin pressure that did not belong to wind, a smell that slipped between the tar and the cumin on the deck.

The map was warm under her shirt and tugged with the same patient insistence it had since Port Rahim. Eshra's Thread lay pale beneath her thumb; the paper's ink seemed to pulse like a lantern. Around her the crew moved with the small, trained economy of hands that had a habit of saving lives—a knot here, a shout there—each motion steadying the ship against things ropes could not hold.

The first of the memory-storms arrived as fog-phantoms. They rose from the sea along the rail like laundry come ashore: translucent shirts and skirts, a child's faded scarf, a shawl that smelled of onion and sun. They made no wet on the planks; where they brushed, the wood remembered a shape and then forgot it as if embarrassed. The smell came next: stale bread, a woman's hair oil, a coal-iron smoke that tugged at a year and a man and a child's name.

The crew's reactions were small and honest. Laila counted the lashings of a stay by twos because that was how she always calmed a restless hand—two, four, six—each pair a tiny anchor. Ibrahim tightened his thumb at the wheel and hummed the little tune that steadied him. Nasir's one, blunt answer to wonder was work: he kept his palms busy, as if fingers moving could stitch a mind together.

For a moment, Nasir's hands did not know they worked for a living. They smoothed a place on the fore-rail and paused, as if folding a sheet. For a blink the fore-rail filled with his mother's washing, white squares flapping in a wind that had no origin. He blinked and the sight slid away; his knuckles went white where he had gripped the rope.

Amina felt the same tug—memory like a hand at the collarbone—only it carried a voice she had known since a night that smelled of seawater and lemon peel. The ghost was not wind and not light; it settled into her like a tucked letter. It was for her alone.

"Hold the line, Mina," the voice said, calm as a plank under foot.

The words fit into her like a practiced order. She saw, with the flat, terrible clarity of remembering, the face that had held them both in the dark—her sibling's face as it had been into the slow dying—and the mouth shaping those words as if they had been a weathering instinct.

No one else on deck saw the ghost; Laila's jaw set around nothing and Nasir kept his hands working the rope.

That private seam—her ghost, the half-thing that visited in mirrors and in dreams—pressed at the dress of her memory and did not ask permission. It had the old phrase, the family cadence, and it folded around her like a weight she had learned to carry. The token at her throat hummed a register she could not mistake; it matched the ghost's timing with its own slow arithmetic and counted the seconds like a ledger.

Around them, other voices rose—some the ship's, some the sea's. The memory-storm took many forms the map had warned about: fog-phantoms, sudden scents, echoed voices, music that drags at a name.

The music came like a reed played under the hull, a single note that found the corner of a thought and would not leave. It tugged at something inside Amina that was not a plan and not a fear but a hinge.

She did not let it move the hinge. There were ways, she knew, to keep a name from sliding in a place like Qarrah: simple practices sailors had learned from port-voices and gossip. Anchors were plain things—counting, named ritual, physical token—and any one of them could hold a name against the island's unpicking. Laila's counting was one of them; the token against the skin at Amina's throat was another; a named ritual was Amina's memory of a phrase a witch had once hissed in a coastal tavern as if it were an apology.

"Keep counting," she said to Laila, and the order was smaller than a command and larger than a prayer. Laila's mouth went tight; she began aloud, a low tally that set the deck like teeth in a jaw: two, four, six. The sound gathered and made a line that the fog could not pass. It was small and ridiculous and true.

The memory-phantoms did not all behave like laundry. Sometimes they took the shape of sounds—a child's voice running a verse the crew knew in half-syllables, a chord of a music box—or they came as scents that made men look away. For a moment Ibrahim flinched, seeing a boat he had once boarded with a stranger who had given him a coin; for a moment Tariq's lips formed the words of a book he'd not meant to say aloud. Each was private, intimate, and leaking into the world as if the sea had grown thin.

From somewhere off the bow, a thin, clean music threaded the air like a needle. It wrapped names in a half-remembered cadence and for a breath tugged at Amina's—then found only rope where name should be. The map burned under her palm like a second heart, Eshra's Thread a single pale promise.

A voice she would have met in a court or a market whispered once, half-remembered among the motes: witches did not take lightly to anchors being plucked. The coven preserved memory with named rituals; they feared removing anchors. If the island unmade a thing, it did not do so because it liked the work; it did it because unmaking was the island's grammar. There was care and a kind of cruelty in that care.

Amina let the thought sit where it could do no harm. She had come to this—not by love of the council's ledger nor by liking for danger—but because the terms set in a high hall had left her with fewer choices than a knot. The token at her throat measured; the map suggested; the sea tested.

If the island was a place that rearranged exits by making people forget the purpose of walking, then she would step inside it with a string in her fist. She pressed her hand flat against the chart and felt Eshra's Thread steady under skin. "We keep to the Thread," she told herself, and the sentence was a thing she could hand to a crew.

Laila's counting rolled on like a drum. Nasir blinked and breathed and took his hands off the fore-rail as if nothing had happened, but Amina had seen the way his shoulder tightened where memory had squeezed. Ibrahim's hum settled back to the rhythm of the wheel. The fog-phantoms thinned at the edge where the map pulled—they might be tests, or the sea making polite inquiry. Whatever they were, they had not broken the Simorgh.

She looked toward the point the map drew for them—the pale ink that would lead them to Qarrah—and set her face to it. The ghost's words lingered; they were ballast when ballast was scarce. "Hold the line, Mina," her sibling had said, and she folded that speech into her hands like a rope.

The Simorgh ran on. Amina let the map warm against her as if to borrow courage from it. She kept her throat steady against the token's hum and steered toward the island that promised unmaking and—perhaps, if the spindle could be taken—mending. She would go into Qarrah knowing the price might be exact and necessary.

She braced herself the way sailors braced for a squall: not with hope but with practice. If the island unpicked names, she would go armed with three anchors—the count in Laila's mouth, the token at her throat, and the Thread under her palm. And if that were not enough, she would make the beginning of a ritual in the small space between two breaths and hold her name with her two hands.

The sea closed behind a shrug of light. Ahead the pale ribbon unspooled like a promise and the island waited in fog and rumor. Amina tightened the braid at her wrist until the skin puckered and took a breath that tasted of cumin and iron and the old slow sorrow.

She would go.
