### previous-scene  <novel/chapters/ch-02/scenes/s-008.md>

They left the cottage as if they were two people practising civility. The high street took them in with its usual small, reliable noises: the bell above the teashop door, the newsboy folding papers at the corner, the cinema poster rolled like a promise against a shutter. Summer light flattened the paving stones and tourists moved in slow, curious clusters, but the town itself kept its distance, able to look without knowing names.

The actress walked with the economy of someone who had rehearsed being seen. Her gloved hand rested on the leather of her bag; her hat sat at a precise, practised angle. She did not move to meet anyone’s eye. She wanted, more than anything, the small anonymous pleasures of a town that did not know her name: a cup of tea at a window, a pastry bought without ceremony, the way a grocer would lift his head and say nothing.

The confidante matched her step for step, a shadow with a method. She watched the street not for scenery but for opportunity and hazard: where a pressman might place himself, which shutter might conceal a lens, which alley gave a view of the Long Walk. Her voice was dry by habit, the sort of voice that had learned to turn intimacy into fact.

"Keep your hat low," she said without looking at her. "Lenses find their way." 

The actress smiled along a thin line. "Is that what you tell yourself when you sleep?"

"I tell myself to keep the ledger straight," the confidante said, and when the actress looked to find the joke she saw the small notebook in the confidante’s palm—pages thumbed as if to where a dozen names had been set down and folded into columns. "Names, times. Where they stood. It helps if one day we have to show this was nothing more than a crossing."

The confidante did not make the catalogue into a spectacle. She tucked the book back into her coat as if tucking in a fault. She spoke of it as a tool: not paranoia, she said, but business. If a single flash could make a night, a single list could unmake a story.

They moved past the teashop window where the aroma of scalded milk and lemon polish clung to the morning. A man at his usual table lifted his head. The neighbor, the town’s small chronicler, recognised the shape of the actress the way one recognises an old poster: at once familiar and out of place. He rose, a polite interruption, and came across with the modest speed of someone who had nothing to gain and everything to offer.

He tipped his hat. "Good morning, ladies—fine day for the castle," he said, and his voice was all small-town largesse. He nodded once at the actress as if passing a compliment and nothing more.

The actress inclined her head. It cost her nothing. The neighbor lingered a breath, then smiled at the confidante, who gave him the briefest, practised smile of her own that said Thank you, keep it closed.

They walked on. A newsboy called the hour by the papers he held; a postman bent to a name on a pile of letters; a bicycle bell tinkled somewhere between the grocer and the florist. The confidante kept watching. Once, she paused at a shop window and turned the paper over in her hands—an old habit that reappeared as reflex. "If someone wants to make a story," she said, "they'll do it from the edges. We'll need the edges catalogued."

"You believe a list can settle a thing," the actress said. There was a softness in her voice that was almost confession. "When I was in California, they kept lists of who you kissed on screen and who you didn't. They measured you by inches."

"It's the same trade," the confidante said. "Here the editors keep a longer memory and the readers keep a shorter one. But names stick. A ledger tells a story that never needed invention."

They fell into a rhythm of memory as they passed a shop that sold postcards—snatches of fame for a shilling. The actress spoke without wanting pity; she spoke because someone had asked. She told the confidante about an evening at a studio party when everyone had applauded a joke she had not made, and how the applause had felt like a draft that let the heat out of her. The confidante listened the way someone listens when the account is both personal and commercial: attentive, but with an eye to the cost.

"Loneliness has the shape of a theatre," the actress said. "People sit in it and offer applause. They ask for entrance and they never ask after the ticket."

"Then give yourself a day without the theatre," the confidante said. "I'll mark it down. We'll call it a walk and not a meeting. If anyone asks, it was never more than that."

The actress laughed at that small invention. For a slow, private second the street looked like one of those ordinary towns in which a person could disappear into the next doorway. The confidante's catalogue became, in that thought, a kind of insurance for disappearing.

They had nearly reached the end of the high street when the confidante's gaze snagged on a shadow inside an alley two doors down—a shape of a man partly folded into darkness as if he had been folded into the day's composition. She stopped them by the gentlest pull at the actress's sleeve.

"Don’t look yet," she said. "Let me see if he moves." 

She studied the alley, measured the angles by a practised subtraction, and then let her shoulders relax. The man stepped out, carried a basket of gloves, and walked the other way. The confidante breathed as if a small weight had been taken from her chest and let them pass.

They crossed the road under the cinema poster. The actress felt the town's ordinary gazes—an economy of looks, measured, uncomplicated—and it steadied her. The confidante kept the notebook in her hand until they reached the corner; then she closed it and slid it into the inner pocket of her coat, a movement that made the catalogue private again.

"I'll be back in an hour," she said, noting the time on a small, sensible wristwatch. "I need to check on a thing at the cottage—calls, nothing more. Keep to the window table for a while; don't let anyone in."

The actress watched her go and, when the confidante had turned the corner, allowed herself one small, unperformed exhalation. For that hour, the town could be what she wanted it to be: ordinary, the way bread and tea are ordinary, and not a stage that measured her every breath.
