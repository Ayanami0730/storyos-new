### previous-scene  <novel/chapters/ch-02/scenes/s-005.md>

The sitting room in the cottage had the small, borrowed luxury of rooms that meant to be kind and not show it. A lamp with a soft, honeyed shade stood behind an armchair and found little pockets of warmth across the rug; a silk shawl—pale, not yet folded—lay over the back of the other chair as if someone had just paused in the act of leaving it. A low table was set between them with a book and a small bowl of shelled nuts; beyond, a narrow sideboard held a decanter, a pair of short glasses and a metal bucket of crushed ice, the sight of which tasted like improbability and courtesy in the heat of June.

She had arrived with a smile that was practiced into the doorway and left it there. In private she was not the placard of Hollywood; she was someone who could sigh and turn the page of a script like any other reader. The paper waited on her lap in its single fold—three loose leaves, corners softened—edges thumbed, the margins pencilled with a few neat carets and one underlining at the bottom of the first page. She smoothed it once, then again, as if the paper were not the thing the world knew her by.

Her hand moved like a rehearsal even after the cameras had gone: she traced a line with the pad of her finger, felt its length in her mouth, practiced a pause the way an actor practices a bow. When she reached a sentence she liked she said it aloud, very quietly, and then listened to it as if someone in the room might have to be persuaded. The habit of the public life lived in these small domestic muscles; she polished a phrase the way a woman might polish a shoe—until she could do it properly without thinking.

A glass stood at her elbow, sweating a neat map of beads onto the tablecloth. She did not drink for show. The gin and tonic was cold enough to make a faint sound when the ice settled; the glass gave away the summer in a smell of citrus peel and juniper. She lifted it only when a pause in the lines allowed her the small pleasure of a sip, and when she did her face softened in a way public light never permitted.

The confidante hovered in the doorway and then drew up an easy chair as if to keep the map of herself visible but small. She had the business of publicity written in her posture—shoulders set to the task of keeping callers away—but there was tenderness braided into the practicality. She folded the telegrams into a drawer, looked at the actress with an impatience that was almost fondness, and arranged a small slip of paper on the mantel with two neat orders: No calls; do not admit; bring a glass if wished.

"I told the cottagekeeper to keep us private," she said, in the flat, usable voice years of studio fights had practiced. "If anyone comes, he refuses them. I'll be in the hall. I won't be far." She measured the sentence the way one measures an arrangement of furniture, or an argument in a contract—no more, and no less.

She did not vanish. Twice, in the hour that followed, she crossed the room on small errands—a kettle to check, the angle of a lamp to correct—and once she left the door ajar to listen down the lane. Each time she came back she carried the warmth of a person who had gone out to see that nothing had happened; each time she leaned on the back of an armchair and waited until the actress told her it was all right.

Sometimes the actress read aloud so softly the words were almost a texture at the edge of the room. Sometimes she read to the glass in her hand, speaking to it as if the liquid might carry off the line and keep it honest. Once she pronounced a name—slow, with a kind of private astonishment—and let it sit. The confidante's fingers tightened on the telegrams in the drawer and then relaxed. The room altered the way a small boat alters its wake.

Outside, the lane made a dull, late-summer noise: a bicycle bell, a distant cart; nothing impeached the cottage's claim to being ordinary. Inside, silk and paper and ice held a private weather. The actress folded the script back into its flap, put it in the neat leather bag at her feet, and for a moment the two women sat with the same small, unsent things between them: the glass, the folded paper, the knowledge that some performances belonged to no audience.

When the confidante finally rose it was not the departure of a manager going back to duty; it was a concession sutured to affection. "I'll leave you an hour," she said, quick as a small trade. "Then I'll come back and I'll want to hear the end." The actress gave a half-smile that moved differently in the lamplight. The public smile could have been a curtain; this one was a hinge.
