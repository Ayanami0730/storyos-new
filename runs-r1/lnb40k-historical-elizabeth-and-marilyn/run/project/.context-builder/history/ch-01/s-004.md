### previous-scene  <novel/chapters/ch-01/scenes/s-003.md>

The high street of Windsor lay in a late-summer softness, heat going out of the stones as if the town were taking a deep, polite breath. Early shutters were open: a grocer’s awning striped white and green, a tear in a poster at the noticeboard for a repertory season, the tea-room’s windows steamed with fresh pots. A milkman in a white smock shouldered a crate, a varnisher knelt over a banister with a tin of gloss, and a boy pushed a tray of lemonade between the tourists’ legs like a steady, sunburned metronome. Hats and collars and the small commerce of mornings gave the street its rhythm.

Tom Harris had the best place to see it. He stood on the step of Harris & Sons, towel at his waist, one hand on the brass rail he’d polished that morning. He was a man who had learned to read the town in half a dozen gestures: the scuff of a brogue that suggested whether a man paid his rent on Friday or fancied himself a gentleman, the ink-stained thumb of the printer’s apprentice, the way a vicar removed his hat in the shop and set it on the counter like an offering. Tom had kept rooms above the shop for fifty years and could place a new arrival by the knock.

“Good morning,” he said to no one and everyone, sweeping the last grit off the door-mat. His voice was the mild, slightly ironic voice of someone who had watched generations of summer suits come and go. He nodded at the noticeboard where a poster for the theatre had curled; he flicked his finger at the newsagent’s window where a neat pile of cuttings had been set behind glass. The file of press cuttings—already familiar to the palace man—showed in the window now, and Tom noted the neat pile as proof the town read reputation like a ledger.

Inside Langley’s Newsagents a publicist—small, efficient, orange silk handkerchief to the cheek—moved with the precise economy of someone used to making stories behave. She stood long enough to make a small sign; the newsman slid a paper behind the counter without a show of it. That was no secret here: the pile in the window did the talking for them. Tom watched the exchange like a man who had learned that reputation was traded in margins and margins were read aloud.

The actress’s driver steered his car down past the Crown Tea Rooms with the calm of someone who had learned to make grand entrances feel incidental. His uniform was neat, his hands steady on the wheel, and he carried a case of luggage like a man who had done this for years. Almost at the same moment the royal chauffeur came the other way, cap pulled low, limousines idling behind him in the castle yard like patient beasts. The two men passed in the street—two professional courtesies, a half-nod, a measuring look—and Tom paused with his broom, blade of it resting on the step, to watch them go.

Tourists clustered at the quay end of the street, cameras slung like talismans over their shoulders. A woman with a straw hat angled against the sun laughed at something a companion said; a child thrust a hand into the pocket of a coat that was too big and kept hold of a cotton reel of candy. Windsor regulars moved through the scene: men in dark suits with trilby hats, shopgirls with neat aprons, children with short trousers and handkerchiefs knotted at their collars. Tom found small confirmations of his reading in the pause and the ways people set their hats or tucked a glove into a purse. A clerk with ink at his cuff watched from the doorway of the solicitor’s office, a theatre programme folded in his breast-pocket like something worth saving.

The publicist moved out, a bright slash among the shopfronts, and the newsman slipped a paper behind the counter with a practiced hand. The whole business of the cuttings—the same sort the palace man kept in a folder labelled Windsor—had become visible to anyone who cared to look. Tom harrumphed softly, more to fill the silence than from surprise. He had seen stars before, actresses who believed every passerby was their audience and gentlemen who disliked being noticed at all. The town made distinctions that were not unkind: celebrity could be enjoyed from a distance, whereas the royal household was something to be particularly careful about. “We clap for the pictures, sir,” he told the boy sweeping leaves, “but we take off our hats for the house.”

When the actress’s driver stopped for a moment to let a bellboy wheel a case across the pavement, Tom tipped his hat with the old courtesy, not foolish but observational. He watched the glove as if it were the last word of a sentence. “Looks the part, don’t she,” he said in a tone that was not envy but appraisal; the woman at the tea-room smiled and nodded, then turned back to her pots.

The day had the pleasant, particular air of a town that expected to be admired and to ignore the admiration in measured turns. Windsor, Tom knew, had always been a place of carefully held attention: you noticed what mattered, you kept your ledger of whom you watched and why, and you kept most of your talk for the evening when the lids came down and the shutters closed. For now the high street kept to its rhythm—hovers of tourists, the precise commerce of shopkeepers, two chauffeurs passing on business the town would speak of over lemon tarts—and the noticeboard, with its curling poster, looked on like a patient neighbour.
