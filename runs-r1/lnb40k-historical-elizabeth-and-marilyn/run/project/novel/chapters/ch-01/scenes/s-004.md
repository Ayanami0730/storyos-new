The press office at Windsor was no more than a small, high-windowed room off the servant’s corridor, but it had the same neat gravity as the household: a strip of oak desk, a pigeonhole for wire copy, a brass bell fixed to one corner, and a pile of newspapers tied with brown string. The royal butler showed the palace press liaison in with a hand that did not linger; his face was the house’s ledger—polite, economical, not in the habit of giving more than the day demanded.

The palace press liaison set the bundle on the desk and smoothed the top clipping with a forefinger as if straightening a thought. He worked in the neat language of small decisions: make an introduction, refuse a photograph, time a paragraph. The newspapers smelled faintly of ink and lemon peel; under the string the file was labelled Windsor, the handwriting familiar enough to be a signature of the household’s attention.

"It came in before breakfast," the butler said. "Miss Langley passed this on."

He left them with the door half-ajar. The palace press liaison made a sound that was nearly a laugh and untied the string. He spread the clippings like a patient physician, pointing at a gossip column, a photograph with a thumbed corner, a wire story that had picked up a remark from a provincial correspondent. He set a photograph to one side and let the paper rustle; the sound suited the day.

When the lady-in-waiting arrived she closed the door with the exacting politeness of someone who kept her house in order. Her voice in the little room had the same careful cadence it had at the Queen’s shoulder: measured, private, and immediately protective.

"Good morning," she said. She did not sit. "You will have seen about our visitor."

He nodded and laid a clipping in front of her. "Miss Cavendish is staying at the Castle View. Her publicist has been busy in town. I thought it best to bring you the file and to ask how we should proceed."

They worked with the same objects and different priorities. The liaison’s fingers found the index card where he kept the names of newsreel men and the number of the provincial office in Reading. "There are a few options," he said. "We ask her publicist for a controlled photograph—two or three frames at most—agree a short caption, and issue a single wire. It gives the papers something that can’t be misread in a gossip column. My worry is the columns: the Daily Mirror and The Times will print what they can, the small gossip sheets will make more than the facts. The newsreels will pick up a neat frame if we hand it to them properly."

The lady-in-waiting touched the clipping as if it were something that might bruise. "And it places her in the path of cameras rather than away from them. The Queen’s mornings are for the Queen. We preserve that, if we can. Do you propose an introduction?"

"A formal introduction could be arranged," he said. "It would need the Queen’s agreement, and one of us would have to set the precise terms. If you prefer, we keep any meeting private and notify no cameras; but where there is a vacuum of fact, gossip fills it. We can control a photograph; we cannot control imagination."

"The household has rhythms," she replied. "There are breakfasts to be kept, dispatches to be read. The sovereign’s private time is not an accessory to someone’s publicity. If we must have the meeting—if the Queen wishes it—then it should be as private as possible, and only by her leave should it be made public."

He did not argue the principle; he argued the arithmetic. "Allowing an uncontrolled scrum will give gossip columns more to invent than the facts would justify. I recommend we supply a single, short caption and one photograph if necessary, and keep any further comment for Buckingham. Locally I will coordinate; anything billed as official should be cleared by the Private Secretary."

She considered the photograph the way one might consider an offered brooch—an item that could bind or betray. "Who signs off?" she asked.

"Ultimately, the Queen. Her Private Secretary would, in practice, clear anything billed as official. Locally I would coordinate; anything material goes up the chain." He tapped the index card to make the chain visible between his finger and the edge of the desk.

There was a pause as the two of them accounted for the household and the town. Outside, the castle’s chiming softened the morning into business as usual. Inside, their trade was nouns: caption, wire, newsreel; and their counter-trade was time: private, staged, public.

The lady-in-waiting kept the private consequence in view. "If we set a photograph," she said, "we must set its bounds. No balcony shots, no evening promenades in costume—only the rooms she would be welcome in. If the Queen prefers not to receive her publicly, the household will make that case."

He wrote the options on a small memo and folded it to her. "I will call Miss Cavendish’s publicist and put forward a controlled frame. I will also make an inquiry to Buckingham about whether any formal line should be issued from there. If the Queen wishes a private interview, we shall wait for her word."

"Then you will let me know before anything is given to the press," she said. "We do not speak for the sovereign without her leave."

"Of course." He stamped the memo with the soft pressure of a man who issued statements and lived to revise them. "I will also have the file of cuttings kept under lock until we decide."

She took the clipping, folded it once, and held it like a petition. "There is kindness in discretion," she said. "And harm in display."

He replaced the newspapers in the file labelled 'Windsor' and retied the brown string. "Then we shall keep the story honest and keep the morning honest as well. I will speak to the publicist in town first."

Outside the small room the house continued its ordered day; the butler’s steps went on in the corridor. They left the meeting with a decision deferred: a call to be made, a line to be drafted, and, if the Queen inclined, a very private question to be put to the sovereign. There was, between them, the peculiar unease that comes when private rhythms must negotiate with a public appetite for frames.

The palace press liaison took the file under his arm and left. At the threshold he hesitated, then turned back. "We had this once, briefly, at an investiture," he said. "It was not detailed—professional acquaintance."

She inclined her head in acknowledgement. The door closed behind him and the house took its breath again, holding, for the moment, what it had chosen not to give.
