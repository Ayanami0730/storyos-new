The Queen began the morning in the small domestic orbit she had kept for herself at Windsor: a single room of pale curtains, a brass kettle, a row of shoes lined as if for inspection. She did not open the heavy doors that looked over the park until the servants had finished their first round; habit held the day together. She made tea with the same motion each time—the tea bag pinched and counted by the same invisible metronome—and sat with her hands warmed by the cup while the house set about its more public rhythms.

The butler arrived with the day's ledger and a quietled face. He moved as he always did through the apartment: a practised economy of gait, a carriage of small ceremonies. He laid the ledger on the table and, without being asked, set out the note of luncheon possibilities in his careful hand. The kettle hissed and the Queen watched the thin steam as though it were a thing that answered her questions.

"One thing," she said, when the ledger had been read and the butler's voice had returned to its neutral cadence. "I should like a walk. The Long Walk, if you please."

The butler inclined his head with the simple fidelity of his kind. "Very good, Ma'am. Shall I tell the kitchen to prepare a basket?"

She shook her head. "No basket. Keep the house to hand. A small cold tray will do if I'm back by lunch."

He made a small note. "We will make provisions. I will keep the household flexible. The kitchen will put together something—sliced cold chicken, open sandwiches and fruit—should you return hungry. I will also inform the senior staff of your absence."

He spoke as if from duty; she heard his duty as a shield rather than an instruction. The choice he offered—flexibility rather than fanfare—fit the small private world she wanted to hold. It was not a refusal of the world's rules so much as a shrinking of ceremony until it could be carried at arm's length.

The lady-in-waiting arrived shortly, efficient and luminous in the way of those who practiced stillness. She stood at the door and folded her hands in the old way; there was a steadiness to her expression that the Queen had learned to lean on. When the Queen said, plainly, "I would like a walk," the lady's fingers tightened for the briefest instant and then relaxed into her answer.

"Of course, Ma'am. When shall we go?"

They walked out together with no fanfare. There was no footman in livery at the gate; there was no motorcar waiting to hum them somewhere else. The butler had been informed, the kitchen had been told to keep a small tray ready, and the household would fold the moment into the day. The Queen liked it that way: the world politely unmade so she could be small and otherwise.

The Long Walk lay precisely as it always did in summer—an avenue of light and green that seemed at once public and private. People took photographs from its edges and picnicked at its shoulder; yet there were places along it where two women could walk without feeling watched. They kept to one of those places: the avenue near the garden pavilion, where a line of elms made a ceiling and the gravel kept their steps honest.

A gardener was there at the pavilion, sleeves rolled and straw hat low, attending to the beds of tea-roses. He lifted a hand by way of greeting; his presence was a piece of the castle's machinery, as much a part of the place as the stones. He did not come close enough to overhear. The Queen appreciated that distance the way one appreciated the margin between stage and wings.

They walked with the particular rhythm of two people who had practiced not to disturb the other: silence tightened into a comfortable pattern and then opened. The Queen said small things at first—about the light, about the way the elms kept the sun from sinking into the park. The lady-in-waiting answered in the neutral, luminous way that had carried her through state dinners and private confidences.

There was a moment—no more than a pause—when the Queen let the thought that usually lived folded in her notebook press at the corner of a sentence. "It is odd," she said, looking straight ahead, "how public everything feels even when one is alone."

The lady-in-waiting did not attempt to soften the statement. She only matched it with a small truth of her own: "One learns to be watched for the sake of it. One also learns to keep the rest of oneself out of sight, because it is safer."

They spoke of loneliness then, not as a complaint but as the fact beneath the trappings. The Queen's voice did not rise; it collected and set down. She spoke of evenings when the lights went out and the house seemed larger than it should, of days that were supposed to be full of meaning but which felt, in private, like a series of obligations. The lady-in-waiting listened as a person who kept confidences for a living, with the kind of attention that was not a question.

"You do what is required of you," the lady said finally. "But you do not have to carry it as though there were only one shoulder convenient for it. There are hands that will hold it if you ask."

The Queen smiled in the way she sometimes did when a practical sentence landed where sentiment could not reach. "I am grateful for that. It is not a thing to say aloud often."

They turned back the way they had come. The gravel kept its small music. The gardener bent again to his roses and the long light made a last soft pass between the trunks. When the Queen reached the apartments again she paused in the doorway, taking in the sight of the kettle on the hob and the ledger folded as if nothing had happened.

The butler was waiting inside, not at her elbow but present; he offered his usual report of messages and dressing plans and then allowed a small, private courtesy as she closed the door behind her.

"Everything in the house was kept to allow your absence, Ma'am," he said. "The kitchen prepared a cold tray, as requested."

She let out a breath that might once have been called a laugh. "Very good. Bring it to the table. I will write for a moment—only a note."

She opened the journal, though she did not write long. The day had already changed shape: the walk had cost her nothing public but it had also given her, quietly, an altered view of the day to come. Outside, the park moved with its usual small dramas; inside, the household resumed its choreography. A private thing had been done in a public place and the world had not noticed.
