The press table was a low, scrubbed surface of oak under the sash window, and on it lay the nervous machinery of the day: stacks of pencilled telegrams, the tight, folded slips from the station, a single waste bin half full of crossed‑out drafts. The telegrams made a sort of small geography—agency bundles tied by string, a blue note marked "URGENT" bent at one corner, loose messages gone to the margins where somebody had abandoned them for later. The press liaison moved around that map the way a man moves around a small, complicated machine: hands precise, eyes quick, a mind used to patching noise into something that looked like order.

He kept his hat on out of inertia, though the room was warm. He read the first strip aloud, because speech fixed the words and made them easier to refuse or accept. "URGENT: sighting reported, details to follow."

The phrase itself was harmless; it would be harmless for hours, or until a photographer put a lens on the wrong hedge. The liaison felt the harmlessness like a weight. He had learned to answer in a sentence, not a story—one line that would stop a rumor without feeding it. He made a first attempt and wrote it down: "Holding line: No comment on private movements."

When the butler arrived he had the same slow certainty he brought to the charge of a table—no fuss, no apology for his authority. He stood in the doorway and let the room set itself around him. The lady‑in‑waiting followed, pencil tucked behind her ear, the posture of someone trained to make small dramas disappear. She did not come as a sensation but as a measured presence. Together they made a small audience for the liaison's rehearsal.

"Do you have to be told?" the butler asked, and the question was not an accusation so much as a tally of responsibility.

"We get what slips through," the liaison said. "From the station, sometimes, or a photographer. This one came wrapped in urgency." He tapped the blue corner with a forefinger. "If one of the papers gets wind, they will run with it before breakfast."

The lady‑in‑waiting sat at the table, not to read the telegrams but to watch the process of containment. She had spent a life learning how sentences moved people; she would not invent any that had to be kept. "What is the event to be acknowledged?" she asked.

"Nothing public yet. A sighting on the Long Walk earlier—the actress. The messenger said 'possible sighting.'"

"Then we give them the frame we want them to see now," she said. She drew a pencil line in the margin of a scrap of paper as if arranging the weight of a sentence. "Something that offers warmth without private fact. Something that cannot be misread."

The liaison tried a tone she suggested and heard its faults. He spoke stiffly, then softer. He tried to be informative and found himself offering a floor of unwanted detail. The lady corrected his cadences—the small hesitations to cut, the pivot words that sealed off an invitation to speculate. The butler, who rarely offered language, kept on the watch for anything that would commit.

They ran three lines aloud, one after the other, as if the voice could prove the phrase's safety by surviving it.

"Her Majesty is pleased to learn of a visit and looks forward to welcoming the actress at an appropriately arranged time," the liaison read the first, and the syllables sagged under their own weight. It suggested arrangement to come—a progress of events that reporters would count as a fact.

"We will not confirm private movements," he read the second, and this was lean and effective; it left the public nothing to hold and the gossip only its own hunger.

"It would be inappropriate to comment on personal calls or private hours," the last tried to be polite and final, but it gave a taste of something secret and therefore of interest.

"Give them the second," the lady said. "Firm, short. 'We will not confirm private movements.' That closes it."

The liaison rewrote the sentence into the clipped form he had known to use in the heat of a deadline: "Holding statement: We will not confirm private movements." He read it aloud as if testing a lock.

They practised the delivery—no wistful softening, no qualifying half‑phrase. The butler watched the way cadence could turn a denial into a dare; the lady checked that the phrase was not an admission and did not pretend to be a welcome note.

Outside the window, the castle's stone held the day's light, indifferent to the small dramas of phrasing beneath it. But within the oak walls there was the nervous choreography of a household that knew the sound a leak made before it wet the paper. The liaison, who had once chased headlines and learned the appetite of editors, felt his craft go brittle when it met the butler's command: press last. Someone, higher than the butler, had already written that instruction into the house—only those who must be told would be told—and that thought made his palms tighten.

"You give them that line and nothing more," the butler said finally, not as command but as law. "You do not add the time or the circumstances. We do not make a public event of a private hour."

"Understood," the liaison said. He tucked the pencil behind his ear the way the lady had, as if that could steady the hand.

He did what he always did next: he turned the containment into a form that could move. He put the words in the shape the wires expected—short, neutral, a single sentence with no ornament—and set them beside the telegrams. He wrote the heading 'Holding statement' and beneath it the sentence they had tested. He made a note for the station messenger: hold for clearance.

There was an old economy to the way they worked: rehearse the interior lines until they were seamless, then send the hollow shape into the noisy world and watch to see if it collapsed. The liaison folded the scrap and pushed it toward the butler. For a second he imagined the scrap travelling: to the station, to a telephone exchange, to a desk in Fleet Street that would decide what it was worth. That image made him uneasy in a new, precise way. He could not unsee how a grammar of privacy would be measured by an editor as a story.

The lady looked at him as if he were a small, necessary machine. "Make it clear it is a holding line. If they press, you do not answer beyond that. We will let facts be facts until then."

He nodded. He had no more time. A porter from the station came in with a new parcel of slips and the blue urgency folded into the top. The room's small choreography resumed: words arranged, hems tucked, a public surface rehearsed until it would not wrinkle under scrutiny. When the door closed behind the butler and the lady, the liaison sat with his scrap of paper and the telegrams and practised the exact phrasing until the words lost their temptation to expand. It was a private kind of performance—small, secret, and meant to be invisible when it worked.
