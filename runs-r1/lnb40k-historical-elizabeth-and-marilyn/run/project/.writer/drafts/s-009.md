The afternoon light came through the pale curtains as if it too were careful of privacy—filtered, slow, and without ceremony. The Queen had the small room arranged as if to put a polite face on solitude: a brass kettle warming beside a chipped cup, a line of shoes like silent sentries, a low chair by the window. On the narrow writing table, half in shadow, lay a book closed like a promise.

She had been at it for ten minutes when the lady entered: the soft, known tread of someone made for houses that are polite and implacable in equal measure. The lady carried a folded paper in one hand, the household’s new habit—an economy of words for an economy of dangers. She stopped by the chair as she always did, hands folded in the old way, practised to steadiness.

The Queen did not look up immediately. She closed the book with a single movement, slow and private, sliding a single finger under the ribbon to level the page. The book was small, bound in soft brown leather, its edges browned by use; a single ribbon marked where she had paused. When she let it rest against the skin of her knee it seemed less a thing than a shape of habit.

“You are later than you said,” the lady observed, not as reproach but as accounting. Her voice held the muscle of procedure; her eyes flicked once to the table where the folded paper lay. “There have been calls. They ask—”

She unfolded the paper and tested a line aloud, not exactly the voice of any reporter but the voice the household had practised in rooms such as this: “We will not confirm private movements.” The sentence wanted no softening. It had been taught to stop curiosity at the border of performance.

The Queen watched her say it and closed the small distance between speaking and not speaking with a look that was almost attention. There was no lightness in the face, only a measurement of the room and of the woman she had learned to trust.

“No,” the Queen said. It was the single quiet that had the weight of a decision. “No. Tell them I was in the garden.”

The lady paused, paper between her fingers. The rehearsed line, the economy of a deflection designed to shut a thousand mouths, hung in the air as if it were a thing with its own gravity. She had practised it until the cadence was painless; she had practised it because painless cadences keep history from getting noisy. She knew how those sentences worked—how they built a wall.

“Ma’am—” she began, then checked herself against something older than the line; she folded the paper back deliberately and let it lie on the table. “Very well,” she said. Her voice rearranged itself into a smaller, practical shape. “I will tell them you were in the garden.”

It was a small rebellion, not dramatic. The Queen did not speak for liberty or for doctrine; she closed the book again and smoothed the cover with the heel of her hand as if to make the decision less visible. “They do not need the precise choreography of my morning,” she said. “They will not be better for it.”

The lady watched the book as if it were a thing both precious and private. She did not reach for it; she did not, in the space between them, ever reach for such a thing. Instead she folded her hands and moved the paper so that it lay between them like a flat, harmless island. “If there is any comment you would prefer—”

“No comment,” the Queen said, and smiled once in a way that had the small, private geography of a person who knew how little the public could hold. “Just the garden.”

They sat with the sentence between them and the rustle of leaves beyond the glass. On a side table a sheaf of loose pages lay marked in pencil—small, impatient carets and underlinings that did not belong to the room but took up its margins like a foreign language. It was a film script, the sort of thing that felt casual and loud in a way the house itself never allowed. The presence of those pages was a quiet contrivance of contrast: the Queen’s bound book beside the scatter of another life’s paper.

When the lady stood, she moved to the writing table and, because the movement had a private choreography of its own, opened the narrow top drawer. The Queen watched but did not ask; it was not necessary. The drawer was where she had always kept the book when she was not using it—neat and near the window so that the light touched it first in the morning.

She slid the journal into the drawer herself, hands small and efficient, and left the ribbon’s end to brush the wood as if to mark that something had been laid to rest. The lines within had been hers—unequivocally; the slanted, patient script of her own hand. She wrote like this, in longhand, the way someone who had been taught to keep a slow, retrievable record might write: deliberately and without flourish.

The lady closed the drawer with the soft click of domestic containment. She did not read the book; she would not read it. There was a long, unspoken etiquette to how households kept a person’s private things: they preserved them, they did not repurpose them, they made them into things to be honoured rather than instruments to be used. Whatever formal authority the household had over papers in a crisis existed only by instruction; in the ordinary room between two women it translated to respect.

Outside the window the garden held its own light, washed and unimpressing, as if it could be summoned in place of a better story. “Tell them the garden,” the Queen repeated, as if the phrase might be a hinge. The lady inclined her head and tucked the paper into the inside of her glove. There was a small economy in that gesture too—things put away to make room for a shape the day would need.

When the lady turned to go she hesitated in the doorway, saw the quiet book in its drawer, and said, not as an apology but as a vow, “Your privacy will be kept.”

“And I will keep the garden,” the Queen answered.

They left the room as if they had done nothing other than what was necessary—that is to say, as if they had done everything.
