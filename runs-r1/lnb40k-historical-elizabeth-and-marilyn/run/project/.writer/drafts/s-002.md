The limousine eased up under a shallow canopy of the Hotel Castle View and then stopped, long black like a beetle drawn close to the hotel's pale brick. The chauffeur opened the rear door with a motion practiced enough to look casual. Porters came forward in a small choreography—gloves, trunks, a hand to a shoulder—while the summer light folded itself across polished badges and brass handrails. The smell of petrol and a too-bright perfume threaded the warm air; someone had forgotten a hat on the pavement and it lay like an unclaimed promise.

She stepped out as if she had been practising it all her life: hat low enough to shade the face, gloves pale against her day dress, sunglasses lifted from her hair for the brief courtesy of the doorway. The movement turned the hotel into its stage. A camera-reflex would have loved her, but there were only a few late-morning strollers and a boy sweeping leaves; the presence around her was not a crowd but a sheen—an expectation that some spectacle might arrive if one only waited long enough.

Her driver, a steady man with white hair at the temples, took the suitcases with the half-manner of someone who had once been a valet. He did not loiter. The publicist touched the actress’s wrist once—a small, efficient adjustment of cuff and astringency—and then stepped back to the newsagent’s window where she bought today’s paper with the same quick attention she gave a contract. She did not read it in public. Instead she slid it to the newsagent with a folded finger, offered a tight smile, and whispered something that made the man tuck the paper into his counter drawer rather than display it on the rack. A small, private arrangement: where the paper might speak loudly, she had the power to ask it to be quiet.

The hotel’s ground floor breathed of bottled shampoo, a polish for furniture and the faint iron of boiled eggs on the chef’s silver trolley. A porter escorted them under the tawny sweep of the stairs to the lift; the lift rose with an obedient hum. The suite opened into a room that seemed to be built to take the act of being seen apart from the act of being alone. Lacquered furniture caught the light in thin, glossy stripes; a mirrored dressing table stood beneath a row of concealed bulbs that would, later tonight, make faces look as if lit from within. Script pages lay at an angle on a small writing desk, their corners weighted by a make-up case and a silver cigarette case that had been carried too often to be new.

She set her valise down and let her fingers travel, once, over the cigarette case as if expecting the same steadiness from the metal. It was cool and marked with a hairline scratch near the clasp. She opened it not to smoke but to order the small private ritual of checking what belonged to her. Inside, a lined card had been slipped between the cigarettes—an arrival note from the publicist and a pressing list, neat as a ledger. She did not read it aloud. The gesture was private; the objects kept their authority by staying quiet.

The publicist had what looked like a folder of clippings tucked under her elbow, a small, sensible stack that smelled faintly of citrus from a handkerchief. She spoke in low, pointed phrases: no interviews for the morning, meet the mayor after tea, no photographs on the balcony. The actress listened; the listening was a posture she knew better than the words. The confidante stood by the window, elbows on the sill, and offered an observation that availed itself of both wit and weariness. “You look at that roofline and tell me which of us has the better costume,” she said, voice dry. “They’ve saved the fancy for the stonework.”

The actress laughed, a short sound, and went to the window. Windsor folded itself in miniature beneath her—a scatter of slate roofs, a distant parapet, the river like a ribbon of pewter threading through the town. The castle’s roofline broke the horizon; she found herself looking at it with the same attention she gave a crew-chief’s instruction. It made a private map in her head: where a hat might catch a breeze, where a line of stone might throw a shadow across a camera. For a moment the landscape and the publicity were the same thing—a surface to negotiate and to be made to look inevitable.

Down on the high street the publicist’s small arrangement at the newsagent had been another action in a longer choreography. She had the habit of carrying clippings—what she called the ledger of sentiment—folded like a note, and she showed them to the actress as if they were talismans to be consulted. “See that?” she said, tapping a paragraph with a neat thumbnail. “They’ll paint you as the season’s ornament if we let them. Keep the balcony until after tea and we tell the right paper first.”

The confidante watched the actress with an economy that had the shape of long acquaintance. She was the kind of friend who had learned to count the time of a public face: how long it could hold a smile before fatigue cracked it, how many autographs were practical before an elbowed hand became a story. “If anyone asks,” she said, “we came for the river and stayed for the scenery.” She meant both. The actress, without looking back, slid the cigarette case closed and set it beside the make-up brushes as if the act completed something in the room.

A bell tinkled in the corridor as a footman announced—politely but with an authority concealed in the syllables—that lunch service would begin in the garden-dining room at half past. The publicist checked her watch, then folded the clippings and slid them into an inner pocket. The sheen of publicity, which had been only suggestion and appetite when the car had drawn up, settled into a plan: times, places, what would be shown and what would be kept out of sight. It was a map everyone understood now, even if they would not say it.

She turned back once more to the window. The river caught the light like a strip of quicksilver, and someone far below laughed—an ordinary sound, not for the cameras. For all its practiced glamour, she felt unexpectedly small, as if the glass between the hotel and the palace could not protect her from being seen in ways she had not practised. She lifted the sunglasses back into place and tilted her face to meet the room’s bulbs, the small light that would make her spectacles useless and her face legible instead.

The day was not yet a scene; it was a list of possibilities. She folded her hands on the dressing table and, when the footman’s polite knock came again—this time with a card—she put a finger to the small name-plate and read the single word it bore. It was not a public announcement. It was, simply, a beginning.
