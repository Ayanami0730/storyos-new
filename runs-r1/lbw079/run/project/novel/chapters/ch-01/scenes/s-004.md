I had the match in my fingers because I had a habit of making ritual out of measurement — a small, deliberate flare I could control when everything else refused to be neat. The lamp had guttered while I was in the attic; the wick had gone faint and the hearth was cool when I came back down. I struck the match and brought it to the wick. The flame settled into the cone I'd measured earlier: six feet from the mantel, eighteen inches above the hearth, seven inches left of the mirror's centre. The attic slat cut the lamp's beam into a thin, pale line that fell straight to the seam.

I read the last page aloud because the diary had always been strict about saying things twice. The new entry I found tucked behind the others was dated March 25, 1972 and said, in Hannah's small, tight hand: "March 25, 1972: A leaving asks a taking; the seam will not be satisfied without an offered place." I read it exactly as written: "A leaving asks a taking; the seam will not be satisfied without an offered place."

The seam caught the light and the glass answered the way the diary promised — not with whim but with geometry. The shimmer concentrated like a held breath along the hairline; the reflected stair folded wrong again, and the blue cold in the mirror sharpened to an edge. For a second my hand and its reflection were two different clocks: mine a fraction ahead, hers a fraction late. Then the seam was a midplane and the world tightened around it.

I spoke her name because the paper told me to, because names with measures seemed to be the language this house understood. "Hannah Hale," I said, and then the line from March 24: "Speak her name and give the leaving."

Something moved in the glass not as an image but as a will. My reflection — my face, my hands — stopped matching mine and began to obey the other side. Where I lifted my hand she did not lift hers in time; instead she loosened her fingers and let them fall to her side, the small domestic motion of someone who was not astonished. The mirror's Hannah did not mouth 'light' this time; she inhaled, and the sound I heard was not from the room but from inside my own skull.

A pressure slid down my spine like a cord being pulled. I felt my center detach — not an ache, but the certainty of moving away from a place I had occupied. There was a flutter, a tilt in the lungs, and then I was looking at the parlor from the wrong side of everything: from behind the glass where the light was colder and the stair went the other way. My hands were pale, pressed to the inside of the mirror; they were me and not-me, reaching out across a surface that would not yield.

On the room-side my body — my hands, my coat, my face — moved without the insistence of my will. Mrs. Haverford, who had come up the path and was in the doorway with her knitting basket, gave a small, sensible sound — a gasp the way a sensible woman gasps when a kettle boils over. The woman in my body smiled, buttoned the cuff of my coat the way someone checking a sleeve does, and said, in my voice but not mine in intent, "Thank you, dear. I can walk now."

The logic of the diary and the light was a clean thing; it had not lied. The seam had wanted symmetry, a place offered to receive what it would let go. It asked for a taking and the house, obeying its angles, provided one.

I pressed my forehead to the cool, unhelpful glass and watched the only proof I had of a bargain keep to its terms: Hannah, alive with my body, turned toward the street and took a step. Behind the glass my breath fogged the reflected pane; beyond it a woman moved off the mat toward a life that had cost me mine.
