I set the torn page, the locket and the close-up of the stain on the kitchen table and waited for the house to say its piece. Mrs. Hollis sat opposite me with a chipped mug between her palms, small movements that kept her calm. Above us the ceiling answered with the familiar two thuds, a pause, two more — the child's cadence like a watch that would not stop.

"Read it again," I said. The paper felt thin and conciliatory between my fingers. I read, careful not to smudge the ragged edge: "—spilt tea under the boards. Don't tell."

Something whispered it back in a voice that had the size and cadence of a child: "Don't tell."

Mrs. Hollis folded her hands and did not look away. The civility on her face thinned; the practiced patience rearranged itself into something brittle. When she spoke, she chose simple shapes instead of a speech.

"Her name was Elsie L.," she said. "The photograph's date — October the twelfth, 1913." She set her jaw and her fingers went to the hem of her skirt. "I wasn't thinking straight. I went across the kitchen for a jar of sugar; while I was gone she had an accident. I hid her and I told people she had been sent away."

I tilted the locket under the lamp. The shallow letters on the silver caught the light: E.L., rubbed and honest. Beside it the sepia's smear resolved into the same date I had coaxed from the back of the photograph. The pieces fitted with the cold logic of things that do not need imagination.

I said nothing more. Evidence does the work better than an accusation, so I took her by the sleeve and we climbed the narrow ladder together.

The plank gave with a soft, greyed sigh. Beneath the bundle of child's papers the subfloor showed a dark, circular mark: tannin-brown, the colour of over‑steeped tea, its edges specked with dust and a tacky, glue-like ring around the rim where something had been bound fast. I scraped at that rim with my thumbnail; a powder came away like an apology.

I held up the porcelain doll. The ribbon braided at its wrist matched the loop the diary's folded page suggested: a child had tied something on purpose. The diary itself mentioned fastening a ribbon to the doll so it would remember. The alignment of that sentence and the physical braid was too clean to shrug off.

When I set the doll on the joist it moved, not with the theatrics of a story but with a single, practicable push. It slid sideways, nudged at the air and came to rest in my palm as if a small hand had placed it there.

On the attic hatch, fresh white strokes formed the single, wobbly letter of a child's name: an E. I hadn't made it. Chalk dust feathered the strokes. The voice that followed did its small, circling thing: "Don't tell."

The measured footfalls that had occupied the house stopped as if someone had wound down a watch. The silence was immediate and the house seemed to sit back, listening.

Mrs. Hollis's shoulders finally let go of their posture. "I was frightened," she said, voice cracked and ordinary. "I sealed that space myself the same day. I thought saying she was sent away would keep the questions away. I thought I was saving the house from scandal, and I wound up keeping a child from being spoken for." Her hands trembled where they had rested on the ladder.

In the cracked mirror on the landing my reflection showed me holding the dented locket; the dent caught the light like a pinprick. Beside my face the mirror held a thin absence — not a shape but the memory of one — where a child's voice had been. I felt the house exhale. For the first time since I moved in, the small, clockwork sounds that answered me stopped moving at all.
