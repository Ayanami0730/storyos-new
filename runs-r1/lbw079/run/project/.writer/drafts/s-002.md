I went to the archive because paper keeps its promises longer than people do. Mr. Calder worked with gloves and a lamp and the patience of someone who has watched handwriting fade into rumor. He pushed a small folder across his desk and said, as if reading from the edge of a map, "Holloway Gazette, mid‑March 1972 — photograph of Holloway House. Box 4, Folder 12. Accession HA‑1972‑03." He let me lift the brittle clipping. The caption beneath the grainy photograph named the day: March 14, 1972.

The picture showed the mantel and the mirror in the same shape I'd learned by touch: the hairline seam across the silvering, the tall east windows throwing a wedge of light onto the floor. Mr. Calder tapped the margin with a clean finger. "We don't have much more than this and the report," he said. "If you want recollection, ask the neighbours."

Mrs. Haverford brewed the kettle as if she were pouring memory into cups. She watched me over her spectacles, and when I told her about the clipping she pursed her lips and offered what a town keeps in pockets: small certainties and larger warnings. "Hannah fussed with her glass," she said. "She used to warn people about the seam — said you could feed a thing into it if you weren't careful. Mind the seam, love — and don't light it at noon." She bisected the words like a housewife folding sheet linen and set them before me.

Back at the parlor I spread the diary on the mantel and read until the ink began to look like instructions rather than memory. Two entries repeated the same odd, technical image and gave it dates in March. One, headed March 9, 1972, said, "At the seam the glass receives what the room offers; mark the joint and do not let the light strike the wrong edge." Another, dated March 21, 1972, read, "Note the angle at the sill; when the light cuts the sill aright the seam answers and will not be denied." The book had also once advised, in the language of someone who trusted geometry over timepieces, that the midday light mattered more than the clock on the wall.

Those three notes—archive, neighbour, diary—snapped into a working shape. The photograph fixed a date and showed the seam; Mrs. Haverford supplied the folk phrasing; the diary insisted that the seam and an "angle at the sill" were recurring, intentional observations. If the silvering included a seam that functioned like a channel, then a beam arriving at a particular declination could be the activating condition. That activation would obey optics, not omen: an aligned ray traveling to the seam, unbroken, might produce the reflection effects I had already noticed.

I made the hypothesis explicit and wrote down two simple, falsifiable predictions. Prediction one: reproduce the March sun's angle with my oil lamp and the mirror will show the lagged echo again and an intensified shimmer at the seam. I set a tentative placement on the sketch — lamp six feet from the mantel, flame roughly eighteen inches above the hearth, and aligned seven inches left of the mirror's center mark. Prediction two: interrupting the reflective path by covering the seam with an opaque strip or placing a board across the sill will stop the lag and the shimmer, proving the effect needs an unobstructed reflective route.

I measured the six feet with the cheap tape and made the mark seven inches left of centre in pencil on the envelope I kept with the diary. The protractor from my toolbox was ready; the oil lamp was already ordered online. Mrs. Haverford's string of warning — "Mind the seam, love — and don't light it at noon" — sounded less like superstition than like a neighbourly inversion of caution: a note to be observed and also tested. The next clear morning I planned to set the lamp at my pencilled spots, measure the angle across the sill, and watch what the glass did when the light cut it true.
