I had written both times down the way I always did, as if ink could fix an angle of the world. The footsteps came like a small clock: a measured pair of thuds, pause, another pair. They had woken me twice in the week since I moved in, and each time I had checked my watch and put the minute beside the note in my notebook. There was nothing cinematic about them; they carried through the house the way the hall sounds had in the morning — thin joists, hollow cavities, a precise resonance that translated attic wood into something you felt under the pillow.

The next morning I climbed up the narrow ladder to the attic with a torch and my notebook in my breast pocket. 9:14 a.m. — attic. Cold light came through the gable and the whole place smelled of dust and old varnish. The trapdoor had a crescent of white on the hinge: damp chalk smudges, the chalk itself soft to the touch as if it had been pressed and rubbed minutes, not years, ago. A jar on a low crate wore pale handprints around its rim where someone had held it; the powder on the prints still felt faintly tacky when I brushed at it with a fingertip.

I moved a tea tin that had been balanced like an afterthought on the central joist and there, under its dented lid, the doll lay on its side. 9:21 a.m. — porcelain doll. It had one glass eye gone; the socket stared with an empty glaze. A braided ribbon was knotted to its wrist and a smear of dried mud darkened the hem of its dress. The doll smelled like attic dust and something faintly of garden earth. I set it on my palm and its small porcelain head swung as if someone had just breathed near it.

Along the northern eaves I found the diary wedged tight under a beam where the rafters met the floor. 9:26 a.m. — diary and drawing. The cloth cover was frayed and the handwriting was small, earnest and a little slanted. Short entries, dates in a child’s hand; one page had a drawing of a toy with a bent wheel that looked, at a glance, like the broken toy in the photograph I had opened in the locket. At the back a page had been torn out and the torn corner folded back into the lining like a secret.

Three boards from the trapdoor the floor was slightly springy. I levered the loose plank and the edge came up with a greyed sigh. 9:33 a.m. — floorboard plank. The cavity beneath held a narrow bundle of papers and envelopes the size of a child's palm; beneath them the subfloor bore a dark, stubborn stain. The stain was dry when I touched it — a dark patch that smelled faintly of iron and old cloth, as if something had bled and then been left to the dust. I photographed it quickly with my phone and wrote the time down.

Everything in that attic behaved like evidence. The chalk at the trapdoor hinge was damp; the jar had thumbprints in white powder; the joists carried the exact cadence of footfalls into the bedroom below. Nothing looked staged, nothing supernatural in the theatrical sense — only small, repeatable marks that a child might have made and, for reasons that did not yet make sense, continued to make.

I sat on the floor with the doll in one hand and the diary open on my knees, the locket warm against my palm where I had taken it out to compare the bent wheel in the drawing to the photograph inside it. The bent toy in the picture and the child's scrawl in the diary matched close enough that I could not dismiss the likeness. And the smear along the locket's rim stayed unexplained, a dark comma I had not yet read.

9:48 a.m. — I wrote the time and the feeling: a small presence, patient and precise, and an anxiety that had the shape of a clock hand moving steadily toward something I could not yet name.
