I set the oil lamp six feet from the mantel and propped the wick so the flame rode roughly eighteen inches above the hearth. I measured the six feet again because measuring was the part of this that felt like control, then I pencilled a small cross on the floor seven inches to the left of the mirror’s centre and taped a strip of envelope paper there to hold the mark.

When everything was where the sketch said it should be I struck a match. The lamp threw a softer, more yellow cone than an electric bulb; it felt as if I had lit an old photograph. The seam in the silvering caught it first and, for a heartbeat that was both longer and too short, the glass answered with an intensified shimmer along that hairline joint. My hand in front of the glass registered a fraction of a second sooner than its reflection — the lag I'd written down as a prediction — and where the seam ran the reflected surface wavered like a skin being plucked.

The reflection was not the room I was sitting in. In the glass the narrow stair descended on the left while, behind me, the real stair rose on the right. Light in the mirror had a colder edge, a blue that did not belong to my lamp; the reflected banister cast a shadow where the real banister made none. The impossible geometry sat there like an answer to something I had only started to ask.

I followed the diary's careful language because the diary had been precise where people were not. Tucked into the margin, dated March 24, 1972, were three short directives I read aloud, as if hearing them aloud would make them obey me as well as the paper:

"Mark the seam."

"Place the flame where the sill is cut."

"Speak her name and give the leaving."

I took the brass key from the mantel—still dull where my thumb had warmed it earlier—and turned it in the attic hatch's rim lock. The catch resisted with an old sigh and then gave. The stair to the attic was narrow and pitched; I climbed into a low, cedar-dark space where dust made the air taste like dry paper. The attic ceiling brushed my forearms; the roof slants made the room feel smaller than the house below.

A single slat on the north rafter was split from edge to edge and left a vertical gap of about two inches. It sat two feet from the hatch and ten feet from the east-facing window, and when I knelt on the hatch lip a thin shaft of lamp-light threaded through the gap and struck the attic floor in a line that aimed back toward the mantel. The diary's phrase about the angle at the sill stopped being an abstraction when I saw that line: the house already gave the ray the path the paper described.

Back in the parlor I pressed a fingertip to the glass where the seam ran. The skin of dust under my fingertip was unmoved but, in the mirror's other side, something matched my touch. She lifted a finger and dragged it along the glass. In the mirror's other side the dust seemed to part where her finger slid; in that reflected seam the darkened trail spelled a single word: "free." The dust on my side of the frame did not move.

Hannah came then — not a storm but a sequence: she tapped once, twice, three times along the inside of the frame; she did it in precise, patient counts, as if enumerating steps. Her mouth formed one slow word. I read it because there is no other polite thing to do with an apparition that mouths a syllable: "light."

My fingers closed around the match. I struck it, lifted the flame to the wick, and said, plainly, "Hannah Hale." Then I read the diary line and spoke it aloud to the room, slow and deliberate: "Speak her name and give the leaving." The wick took; the lamp settled into its cone of light where I had measured it. The mirror watched, and whatever answered to names and measures waited without hurrying.
