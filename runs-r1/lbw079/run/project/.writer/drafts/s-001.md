10:12 a.m., March 3 — moved in.

I had parked the last box under the threadbare wardrobe and stood for a long minute in the doorway because 67 Fairfax folded around me like an old hand: the constant, faint smell of boiled tea and old paper, the stairs that complained in a different key with each step, and the thin walls that let the hall sound through as if it belonged to my room too. Mrs. Hollis met me on the landing with a key on a neat ribbon looped over one finger, small and exact as the woman who owned it.

She was exactly as you'd expect from her photographs in other people's mouths: small, precise, habit-bound. She inclined her head once, clipped the name from my lips, and ticked off rules in sentences that behaved like lists.

"No smoking in the house, please. No late visitors up past eleven. And don't be troubled by the hall clock — it's been stopped since before my time; there's nothing to be done about it."

It was the way she said it — the civility with an edge of practised reluctance — that made me ask, before I could be polite, whether we'd met before. "No," she said, with a quick, patient smile that folded thought away. "This is the first proper time."

11:03 a.m. — I wrote the hour in my notebook as habit demanded, because writing things down made them smaller, manageable. Mrs. Hollis showed me where I could put the kettle and pointed at the hallway with the same clipped hand: "Don't worry about it; the clock's stopped at 4:22, it likes that time."

I noted the stopped face of the broken clock as she mentioned it and then shut the door on the landing's soft light. My room was smaller than I expected: a single bed, a narrow wardrobe, a wall-hung mirror with a hairline crack low on its glass, and a strip of floral wallpaper behind the wardrobe where someone had made a patch.

The patch was fresher than the rest of the wall: its edge had the clean, un-aged line of a recent glue job. Behind the wardrobe, in the right-hand corner where the paper had been tucked and folded by a careful thumb, there was a narrow gap beneath the lining.

I shifted the wardrobe and my fingers went into that seam without thinking. It gave like a mouth and something small and silver slid against my knuckle — cold with dust.

11:07 a.m. — found silver locket.

The locket was dented on one side, its chain run through with the almost-absence of a loop. A smear ran along the rim, red-brown in the way rust leans and dark like dried mud, so I turned it over on the palm of my hand as if it might explain itself. The silver and the way it had been dented looked almost a century old — early twentieth century; the metal was worn thin at the edges in a way the chain, barely there, did not match.

When I prised it open the photograph inside was a faded sepia: a child with a broken toy held in both hands, the edges curled and soft. On the reverse, two letters had been scratched clumsily into the silver — E.L. — shallow enough that dust sat in the grooves. The whole piece smelled faintly of old soap and a hint of lavender; a scatter of powder dusted the hinge.

I held the locket up to the mirror. The hairline crack distorted the lower third of the reflection so the dented edge of the locket seemed doubled, as if two small mouths were making a secret of the photograph between them.

I slipped the locket into my pocket and, without thinking, wrote a quick line in my notebook: "11:07 — silver locket. E.L."
