### world-rules  <world/rules.yaml>

These govern what is TRUE. They do not say what anyone KNOWS.
A character knows a rule only where canon says so; otherwise they may be ignorant of it, wrong about it, or in the middle of working it out — and if the story is about someone discovering one of these, writing it into their thoughts early is how that story gets destroyed.

- Supernatural effects are bounded and leave physical, reproducible traces: scratches, displaced objects, marks on surfaces, temperature changes measurable by normal means.
- Ghosts in this story are anchored to people, objects, or places; they cannot teleport away from their anchor or create new anchors ex nihilo.
- No retroactive changes to recorded facts or documents: handwriting, photographs and stains found in the house were placed there in the past and cannot be altered by the ghost once discovered (the ghost can only reveal or reproduce marks consistent with existing materials).
- The narrator's perceptions are essentially reliable (senses, timestamps, recordings are trustworthy) though psychological tension can bias interpretation; contradictions must be resolvable by later, physical evidence.
- No time travel, no resurrection of the dead as living persons, and no physical impossibilities (e.g., objects do not pass through solid matter unless a consistent mechanism is supplied).
- Causality holds: every observed effect has a discoverable proximate cause within the world (possibly including the ghost's agency), and the reader should be able to follow the chain of reasoning from clue to conclusion.

### previous-scene  <novel/chapters/ch-01/scenes/s-002.md>

11:07 a.m. — silver locket. E.L.

2:00 a.m. — footsteps above.
3:12 a.m. — footsteps above.

I had written both times down the way I always did, as if ink could fix an angle of the world. The footsteps came like a small clock: a measured pair of thuds, pause, another pair. They had woken me twice in the week since I moved in, and each time I had checked my watch and put the minute beside the note in my notebook. There was nothing cinematic about them; they carried through the house the way the hall sounds had in the morning — thin joists, hollow cavities, a precise resonance that translated attic wood into something you felt under the pillow.

The next morning I climbed up the narrow ladder to the attic with a torch and my notebook in my breast pocket. 9:14 a.m. — attic. Cold light came through the gable and the whole place smelled of dust and old varnish. The trapdoor had a crescent of white on the hinge: damp chalk smudges, the chalk itself soft to the touch as if it had been pressed and rubbed minutes, not years, ago. A jar on a low crate wore pale handprints around its rim where someone had held it; the powder on the prints still felt faintly tacky when I brushed at it with a fingertip.

I moved a tea tin that had been balanced like an afterthought on the central joist and there, under its dented lid, the doll lay on its side. 9:21 a.m. — porcelain doll. It had one glass eye gone; the socket stared with an empty glaze. A braided ribbon was knotted to its wrist and a smear of dried mud darkened the hem of its dress. The doll smelled like attic dust and something faintly of garden earth. I set it on my palm and its small porcelain head swung as if someone had just breathed near it.

Along the northern eaves I found the diary wedged tight under a beam where the rafters met the floor. 9:26 a.m. — diary and drawing. The cloth cover was frayed and the handwriting was small, earnest and a little slanted. Short entries, dates in a child’s hand; one page had a drawing of a toy with a bent wheel that looked, at a glance, like the broken toy in the photograph I had opened in the locket. At the back a page had been torn out and the torn corner folded back into the lining like a secret.

Three boards from the trapdoor the floor was slightly springy. I levered the loose plank and the edge came up with a greyed sigh. 9:33 a.m. — floorboard plank. The cavity beneath held a narrow bundle of papers and envelopes the size of a child's palm; beneath them the subfloor bore a dark, stubborn stain. The stain was dry when I touched it — a dark patch that smelled faintly of iron and old cloth, as if something had bled and then been left to the dust. I photographed it quickly with my phone and wrote the time down.

Everything in that attic behaved like evidence. The chalk at the trapdoor hinge was damp; the jar had thumbprints in white powder; the joists carried the exact cadence of footfalls into the bedroom below. Nothing looked staged, nothing supernatural in the theatrical sense — only small, repeatable marks that a child might have made and, for reasons that did not yet make sense, continued to make.

I sat on the floor with the doll in one hand and the diary open on my knees, the locket warm against my palm where I had taken it out to compare the bent wheel in the drawing to the photograph inside it. The bent toy in the picture and the child's scrawl in the diary matched close enough that I could not dismiss the likeness. And the smear along the locket's rim stayed unexplained, a dark comma I had not yet read.

9:48 a.m. — I wrote the time and the feeling: a small presence, patient and precise, and an anxiety that had the shape of a clock hand moving steadily toward something I could not yet name.

### recent-scene-s-001  <novel/chapters/ch-01/scenes/s-001.md>

10:12 a.m., March 3 — moved in.

I had parked the last box under the threadbare wardrobe and stood for a long minute in the doorway because 67 Fairfax folded around me like an old hand: the constant, faint smell of boiled tea and old paper, the stairs that complained in a different key with each step, and the thin walls that let the hall sound through as if it belonged to my room too. Mrs. Hollis met me on the landing with a key on a neat ribbon looped over one finger, small and exact as the woman who owned it.

She was exactly as you'd expect from her photographs in other people's mouths: small, precise, habit-bound. She inclined her head once, clipped the name from my lips, and ticked off rules in sentences that behaved like lists.

"No smoking in the house, please. No late visitors up past eleven. And don't be troubled by the hall clock — it's been stopped since before my time; there's nothing to be done about it."

It was the way she said it — the civility with an edge of practised reluctance — that made me ask, before I could be polite, whether we'd met before. "No," she said, with a quick, patient smile that folded thought away. "This is the first proper time."

11:03 a.m. — I wrote the hour in my notebook as habit demanded, because writing things down made them smaller, manageable. Mrs. Hollis showed me where I could put the kettle and pointed at the hallway with the same clipped hand: "Don't worry about it; the clock's stopped at 4:22, it likes that time."

I noted the stopped face of the broken clock as she mentioned it and then shut the door on the landing's soft light. My room was smaller than I expected: a single bed, a narrow wardrobe, a wall-hung mirror with a hairline crack low on its glass, and a strip of floral wallpaper behind the wardrobe where someone had made a patch.

The patch was fresher than the rest of the wall: its edge had the clean, un-aged line of a recent glue job. Behind the wardrobe, in the right-hand corner where the paper had been tucked and folded by a careful thumb, there was a narrow gap beneath the lining.

I shifted the wardrobe and my fingers went into that seam without thinking. It gave like a mouth and something small and silver slid against my knuckle — cold with dust.

11:07 a.m. — found silver locket.

The locket was dented on one side, its chain run through with the almost-absence of a loop. A smear ran along the rim, red-brown in the way rust leans and dark like dried mud, so I turned it over on the palm of my hand as if it might explain itself. The silver and the way it had been dented looked almost a century old — early twentieth century; the metal was worn thin at the edges in a way the chain, barely there, did not match.

When I prised it open the photograph inside was a faded sepia: a child with a broken toy held in both hands, the edges curled and soft. On the reverse, two letters had been scratched clumsily into the silver — E.L. — shallow enough that dust sat in the grooves. The whole piece smelled faintly of old soap and a hint of lavender; a scatter of powder dusted the hinge.

I held the locket up to the mirror. The hairline crack distorted the lower third of the reflection so the dented edge of the locket seemed doubled, as if two small mouths were making a secret of the photograph between them.

I slipped the locket into my pocket and, without thinking, wrote a quick line in my notebook: "11:07 — silver locket. E.L."
