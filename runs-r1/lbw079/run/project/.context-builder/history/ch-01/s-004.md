### logline  <novel/outline/logline.md>

When Lena inherits an old house, an antique Venetian mirror and a hidden diary draw her into a precise, daylight-angled ritual that promises to free the woman who vanished there — but the mirror obeys geometry not mercy, and what is freed is not what she expects.

### world-rules  <world/rules.yaml>

These govern what is TRUE. They do not say what anyone KNOWS.
A character knows a rule only where canon says so; otherwise they may be ignorant of it, wrong about it, or in the middle of working it out — and if the story is about someone discovering one of these, writing it into their thoughts early is how that story gets destroyed.

- 1) The only supernatural mechanism in this story is reflection-conduction: certain mirrors, under specific geometric and lighting conditions, can act as conduits or repositories for a presence. No other ghost powers (telekinesis, omniscience) are allowed.
- 2) The Venetian mirror (obj-venetian-mirror) is special because of its silvering pattern; only it can hold Hannah's presence. Ordinary mirrors do not replicate the phenomena unless explicitly noted.
- 3) Light and angle matter. A precise alignment of light (time-of-day or a lamp placed at a marked position) must occur to 'activate' the conduit. The activation obeys ordinary geometric optics (lines of sight, reflection), and the narrator can calculate and reproduce it.
- 4) Physical objects obey normal physics. Breaking, moving or covering objects has immediate, ordinary consequences (shards, blocked sightlines). Breaking the mirror disperses the stored image/presence unpredictably but does not 'destroy' the metaphysical trace; the narrative must not show impossible physical outcomes.
- 5) Ritual language in the diary is ambiguous and fallible. It describes procedures that will produce an effect consistent with rules 1–4; it may omit moral cautions. Any consequences that follow must be deducible from the diary plus the mirror/light rules, not from arbitrary supernatural fiat.
- 6) Identity transfer is possible only through the mirror-conduit under the activation conditions. Such transfers are constrained: the conduit swaps or binds the reflected and reflecting subject according to the mirror’s geometry and cannot create or annihilate independent souls.

### previous-scene  <novel/chapters/ch-01/scenes/s-003.md>

I set the oil lamp six feet from the mantel and propped the wick so the flame rode roughly eighteen inches above the hearth. I measured the six feet again because measuring was the part of this that felt like control, then I pencilled a small cross on the floor seven inches to the left of the mirror’s centre and taped a strip of envelope paper there to hold the mark.

When everything was where the sketch said it should be I struck a match. The lamp threw a softer, more yellow cone than an electric bulb; it felt as if I had lit an old photograph. The seam in the silvering caught it first and, for a heartbeat that was both longer and too short, the glass answered with an intensified shimmer along that hairline joint. My hand in front of the glass registered a fraction of a second sooner than its reflection — the lag I'd written down as a prediction — and where the seam ran the reflected surface wavered like a skin being plucked.

The reflection was not the room I was sitting in. In the glass the narrow stair descended on the left while, behind me, the real stair rose on the right. Light in the mirror had a colder edge, a blue that did not belong to my lamp; the reflected banister cast a shadow where the real banister made none. The impossible geometry sat there like an answer to something I had only started to ask.

I followed the diary's careful language because the diary had been precise where people were not. Tucked into the margin, dated March 24, 1972, were three short directives I read aloud, as if hearing them aloud would make them obey me as well as the paper:

"Mark the seam."

"Place the flame where the sill is cut."

"Speak her name and give the leaving."

I took the brass key from the mantel—still dull where my thumb had warmed it earlier—and turned it in the attic hatch's rim lock. The catch resisted with an old sigh and then gave. The stair to the attic was narrow and pitched; I climbed into a low, cedar-dark space where dust made the air taste like dry paper. The attic ceiling brushed my forearms; the roof slants made the room feel smaller than the house below.

A single slat on the north rafter was split from edge to edge and left a vertical gap of about two inches. It sat two feet from the hatch and ten feet from the east-facing window, and when I knelt on the hatch lip a thin shaft of lamp-light threaded through the gap and struck the attic floor in a line that aimed back toward the mantel. The diary's phrase about the angle at the sill stopped being an abstraction when I saw that line: the house already gave the ray the path the paper described.

Back in the parlor I pressed a fingertip to the glass where the seam ran. The skin of dust under my fingertip was unmoved but, in the mirror's other side, something matched my touch. She lifted a finger and dragged it along the glass. In the mirror's other side the dust seemed to part where her finger slid; in that reflected seam the darkened trail spelled a single word: "free." The dust on my side of the frame did not move.

Hannah came then — not a storm but a sequence: she tapped once, twice, three times along the inside of the frame; she did it in precise, patient counts, as if enumerating steps. Her mouth formed one slow word. I read it because there is no other polite thing to do with an apparition that mouths a syllable: "light."

My fingers closed around the match. I struck it, lifted the flame to the wick, and said, plainly, "Hannah Hale." Then I read the diary line and spoke it aloud to the room, slow and deliberate: "Speak her name and give the leaving." The wick took; the lamp settled into its cone of light where I had measured it. The mirror watched, and whatever answered to names and measures waited without hurrying.

I went to the archive because paper keeps its promises longer than people do. Mr. Calder worked with gloves and a lamp and the patience of someone who has watched handwriting fade into rumor. He pushed a small folder across his desk and said, as if reading from the edge of a map, "Holloway Gazette, mid‑March 1972 — photograph of Holloway House. Box 4, Folder 12. Accession HA‑1972‑03." He let me lift the brittle clipping. The caption beneath the grainy photograph named the day: March 14, 1972.

The picture showed the mantel and the mirror in the same shape I'd learned by touch: the hairline seam across the silvering, the tall east windows throwing a wedge of light onto the floor. Mr. Calder tapped the margin with a clean finger. "We don't have much more than this and the report," he said. "If you want recollection, ask the neighbours."

Mrs. Haverford brewed the kettle as if she were pouring memory into cups. She watched me over her spectacles, and when I told her about the clipping she pursed her lips and offered what a town keeps in pockets: small certainties and larger warnings. "Hannah fussed with her glass," she said. "She used to warn people about the seam — said you could feed a thing into it if you weren't careful. Mind the seam, love — and don't light it at noon." She bisected the words like a housewife folding sheet linen and set them before me.

Back at the parlor I spread the diary on the mantel and read until the ink began to look like instructions rather than memory. Two entries repeated the same odd, technical image and gave it dates in March. One, headed March 9, 1972, said, "At the seam the glass receives what the room offers; mark the joint and do not let the light strike the wrong edge." Another, dated March 21, 1972, read, "Note the angle at the sill; when the light cuts the sill aright the seam answers and will not be denied." The book had also once advised, in the language of someone who trusted geometry over timepieces, that the midday light mattered more than the clock on the wall.

Those three notes—archive, neighbour, diary—snapped into a working shape. The photograph fixed a date and showed the seam; Mrs. Haverford supplied the folk phrasing; the diary insisted that the seam and an "angle at the sill" were recurring, intentional observations. If the silvering included a seam that functioned like a channel, then a beam arriving at a particular declination could be the activating condition. That activation would obey optics, not omen: an aligned ray traveling to the seam, unbroken, might produce the reflection effects I had already noticed.

I made the hypothesis explicit and wrote down two simple, falsifiable predictions. Prediction one: reproduce the March sun's angle with my oil lamp and the mirror will show the lagged echo again and an intensified shimmer at the seam. I set a tentative placement on the sketch — lamp six feet from the mantel, flame roughly eighteen inches above the hearth, and aligned seven inches left of the mirror's center mark. Prediction two: interrupting the reflective path by covering the seam with an opaque strip or placing a board across the sill will stop the lag and the shimmer, proving the effect needs an unobstructed reflective route.

I measured the six feet with the cheap tape and made the mark seven inches left of centre in pencil on the envelope I kept with the diary. The protractor from my toolbox was ready; the oil lamp was already ordered online. Mrs. Haverford's string of warning — "Mind the seam, love — and don't light it at noon" — sounded less like superstition than like a neighbourly inversion of caution: a note to be observed and also tested. The next clear morning I planned to set the lamp at my pencilled spots, measure the angle across the sill, and watch what the glass did when the light cut it true.

I arrived with the van half-empty and the house already choosing its sounds. The parlor took my boxes with the same measured indifference: a long room with tall windows, faded wallpaper, and a mantel that sat four feet off the floor. The Venetian mirror above it was six feet wide by four feet tall; its lower edge began three feet above the mantel, so the glass started seven feet from the floor. The windows faced east; from the mantel to the nearest window opening it was thirteen feet, and the mantel’s right edge lay three feet from the east wall where the narrow stair rose.

I set down the last box, untied my tape, and drew the room on the back of an envelope — a rectangle for the mirror, a line for the windows, a dot where the hearth still smelled of old coal. In my sketch I wrote the numbers in a neat column: mantel 48 in., mirror 72×48 in., mirror bottom +36 in. I measured that by eye and with the cheap tape from my toolbox because these are the sorts of things that matter later.

The mirror was nailed to the mantel with old iron brads. I could hear the brads scrape faintly when I rocked the frame forward; the sound made me think of a hinge and a breath. I slid a putty knife under the bottom edge between the brads and eased the glass forward until the frame scraped the plaster. Behind it, folded flat against the wall, was a small diary: neat, narrow, the sort of book that had been written in with a very small, very steady hand.

I palmed the diary and felt the grain of its cover while the mirror returned to its place with a soft, rustling click. When I opened the book the handwriting ran on tight lines about angles and lamps and the way a slice of light would travel across the floor in March. I read one line aloud without meaning to: "Measure the angle at noon; do not trust the hour by the clock." The writing sat there like an instruction and a warning at once.

I wanted to check the floorboards because a house that keeps secrets often hides keys where a person might kneel to look for them. Two feet east of the hearth I found a plank that rode up under the heel when I stepped on it. A screwdriver pried the board loose and under it lay a small brass key, dull with age but warm from my hand the moment I picked it up.

There was a tapping then, very faint and regular, as if someone were turning a page behind the other side of the wall. It was not the settling that I’d expected; it had a rhythm — one-two, pause, one-two — and when I put my palm flat on the plaster I could feel the tiny modulation through the wood.

Back in front of the mantel I did a small experiment: I held my hand at a point I had marked on the sketch — seven inches left of center, at roughly the height of the mirror’s lower edge — and lifted it slowly. The reflection followed, but not perfectly. For a fraction of a second the glass showed my hand a beat after my hand had moved, as if the mirror were watching my gesture through a slow feed.

I closed the diary and let the brass key sit on top of it. The house hummed around me like an engine idling. I decided then to test the optics properly: precise measures, lamp angles, a repeatable note on where the lag happened. I made a note in the margin of the envelope and, as I packed the putty knife back into the toolbox, I felt the first, clean obligation of a problem I could solve.
