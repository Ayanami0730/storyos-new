I went back into the study with Lane's warning still in my pocket like a coin I couldn't spend. She had told me not to touch anything until forensics arrived; I kept my hands in my coat and let my eyes do the work. The longcase clock took the far wall and swallowed the light. The access panel at the back was a hair ajar; when I peered in the brass looked thumbed and raw where varnish had been rubbed away. There were fine, fresh scores around the latch—hairline marks that caught the light as if someone had used a slender tool to lever it open.

At the base the black thread hung where I'd seen it: waxed, stiff, caught on a small screw. This time I followed its line with my gaze. It wasn't a loose scrap that had wandered into the case; the thread looped and ran up toward the movement, vanishing behind the plates. If you wanted a thing to move at a chosen minute, a thread laid like that would be the sort of modest, patient device you'd use.

I didn't say any of that aloud. I turned instead to Thomas Reed when he came in from the workshop, his hands smelling faintly of oil. He had that thumb-and-forefinger rub that men in his trade do when they steady themselves. "You were in here recently?" I asked.

He met my eyes and his mouth went flat. He said, "I was in three days ago to wind it and I stripped the weight-gear to clean the pivots—it's delicate work, Ellis." The sentence landed without flourish; it was an admission and a small, professional apology all at once.

"You left the panel ajar?" I asked.

He looked past me at the frame of the clock. "No," he said. "I always close it. I wouldn't leave the case open. I closed it when I left." He rubbed his thumb harder, like a man trying to work a stubborn tooth loose. "I didn't kill him, if that's what you're getting at. I serviced it—winding, cleaning the pivots—but I was out of the shop by ten. Ask anyone." His voice tightened on the last clause.

There was a thing in the dead man's hand that didn't fit with Thomas's timeline. Lucius's fingers were curled around a torn scrap; the ragged edge lay on top of the knuckles, the torn fibres splayed against his skin rather than pressed into it. The blotter beneath showed the neat ring of a round stain, the dark halo that told you where blood had soaked through. The scrap, though, sat on that halo without a smear. If he'd clutched the paper in his last moments it would have been different—the fibres would have been pressed, ink and blood mingled into the edge. This looked staged.

"The scrap's been put there after the fact," I said, and I kept my tone light because accusation is heavy and Lane was watching. "Its fibres lie on top of his fingers. It hasn't been pressed into the skin, and there's no transfer where you'd expect it."

Thomas's cheeks hollowed. "Why would someone do that?" he asked. "To make it look—" He didn't finish.

That was when Lane walked over with the constable and a man from forensics, although they were careful and quick and didn't disturb anything more than a single gloved hand to lift a sample. The forensics man paused at the desk and tapped his latexed finger at the envelope's diagonal crease. "We lifted a partial print from the flap's crease," he told me without flourish. "Preliminary lift consistent with Iris Harben."

Iris appeared then, as if the naming had summoned her. She was composed; the movement of her hand to her wedding band was the small private motion I had seen before. When I asked her about the sale and the note she said, with a bluntness that might have been armour, "If he sells it, we have nothing left but his arrogance."

Her words settled over the room like another clock tuning itself. For a moment everything in the study felt arranged to be observed: the stopped hands at eleven-seventeen, the access panel that shouldn't have been raw with tool-marks, the thread running toward the gears, a torn note that read like stagecraft, and an envelope that now carried a print.

I kept my hands where Lane could see them and stepped to the clock's case. I did not touch the latch; forensics had the gloves and the reason. But the thing I was thinking was plain and dangerous: this wasn't just a prized object anymore. If someone's clever—or cruel—hand had used the train as a timer, the clock could be the mechanism that killed Lucius.
