- obj-antique-clock: An ornate mantel clock on the library fireplace; its hands were stopped at the moment the investigators estimate the fatal wound was inflicted.
- obj-letter-opener: A heavy brass letter opener from Lena's desk, found embedded in her chest but with none of the obvious prints and with an oil smear on its haft.
- obj-bloodied-handkerchief: A small linen handkerchief with a monogram; found clutched in Lena's hand. The monogram does not match her own.
- obj-secret-pulley: An improvised pulley anchor and short length of cord attached to the outer window sill and hidden in the ivy — a possible device for manipulating the window latch or a line from outside.
- obj-harrow-folio: Lena's folio: a ledger of household accounts and a private notebook in which she annotated names and times. A page is torn out and missing.
- obj-ashtray: A kitchen-style ashtray found on the library sill with cigar ash; one cigar brand was favoured by Henry Harrow.

### narrative-voice  <novel/style/voice.md>

Narration: first person, char-august-crowe, past tense. This was decided for the whole book before any of it was written and does not change.
Check your own sentences against it before you stage them. Mixing a collective "we" into a third-person narration, or slipping into present tense for a scene that feels immediate, is scored as a defect and is the most common one this system has produced: seven of nine consistency errors in a measured 18,000-word manuscript were exactly this.
If you think the story genuinely needs a different voice, propose the deviation — do not simply write it.

### world-rules  <world/rules.yaml>

These govern what is TRUE. They do not say what anyone KNOWS.
A character knows a rule only where canon says so; otherwise they may be ignorant of it, wrong about it, or in the middle of working it out — and if the story is about someone discovering one of these, writing it into their thoughts early is how that story gets destroyed.

- No supernatural explanations; every effect has a physical or human cause.
- Physical actions (mechanical triggers, pulleys, climbs) must obey normal physics and the constraints of a late-20th/early-21st-century English manor house.
- Forensic facts (stoppage of clock, blood spatter, fingerprints, cigar ash, monogram identification) are admissible and treated realistically — i.e., they can be partial and ambiguous but cannot be ignored without reason.
- The narrator (Crowe) is fallible and does not have knowledge beyond what can be reasonably observed, deduced, or learned by questioning witnesses and examining physical evidence.
