The cab left me on a wet flagstone, the driver already counting the seconds until he could leave. Harben House sat back from the lane under a low sky; rain freckled the leaded windows and made the oak panels look younger than they were. Inspector Lane stood in the small foyer with her collar up, a constable behind her, the hall already marked off with a strip of tape that had more dignity than utility. “Don’t touch anything until forensics are here, Ellis,” she said when she saw me. Her voice was polite; her eyes were not.

Anna Rowe met me at the study door. She had been the one to find him. “I found him at his desk, Mr. Fletcher. He still had something in his hand,” she told me before she could stop herself. Her hands were steady but her knuckles were white where she gripped a shawl.

The study door was shut. A heavy brass key sat in its lock, the metal dulled by years of handling and scored along one edge. Lane pointed at it without looking: “Locked from the inside,” she said. She did not ask me whether I wanted to see; that would have been an invitation I would have taken if she had given it.

The room beyond smelled of oil and paper and a faint, sharp tang of metal. It was smaller than my head had made it; bookcases leaned in as if gossiping. The longcase clock took up the far wall, an upright thing of walnut and gilt that pulled the light toward it. Its hands were frozen at eleven seventeen; the pendulum hung motionless, a dark tongue. Whatever had stopped it had stopped it cleanly—the access panel at the rear of the case was a hair ajar, a sliver of inner brass visible where the latch sat crooked.

Opposite the clock, a glass-fronted case displayed a row of mounts on velvet. One mount was empty. A little brass label under the gap read IVORY REVOLVER in neat type; the mounting beneath it showed the ghost of a shape, the crushed velvet where the grip had rested. The case held everything else in its place, all the gilded mouths and ivory handles lined up, and then that absence like a missing tooth.

Lucius Harben sat at his desk with his shoulders folded forward as if in sleep. The back of his head caught the light; his fingers curled over a torn scrap. I stepped closer the way you do when you mean to offend nothing, and Lane let me. His chest was still. A neat, round mark on the wood, the kind a single shot makes, stained the blotter beside his hand.

In his palm was a torn note, no larger than my thumb. The fragment I could see read, simply, do not wind. I read the words twice, because a sentence like that felt like a trap or an instruction and I prefer to be polite in the face of either.

At the base of the clock, tangled in the carved scrollwork, a black thread hung, waxed and stiff. It caught my nail when I checked it; it looked absurdly ordinary to be part of anything clever, and for that reason I did not dismiss it.

The windows were latched and the glass unbroken. No sign of forced entry showed on the doorframe; the brass key remained in the lock on the study side. Anna hovered near the threshold, glancing from the body to the clock as if she were still trying to reconcile two different days. Lane watched the room the way a surgeon watches an exposed artery.

I said nothing at first. There was a set of small, precise things here: a stopped clock, a brass key in the lock, an empty space where an ivory revolver should have been, a torn note clutched in a dead man’s hand. The facts were polite and stubborn; they would have to be asked their questions in order. For now there was only the noise of the rain, the smell of oil, and the way the house seemed to be holding its breath.

I went back into the study with Lane’s caution still hanging between us. She closed the door behind her and said, in the very words she’d used when I first arrived, “Don’t touch anything until forensics are here, Ellis.” The sentence landed like a rule; her eyes supplied everything she didn’t say.

The longcase clock took the far wall and held the light. Its hands were frozen at eleven seventeen; the pendulum hung motionless. The rear access panel was a hair ajar and the latch sat crooked where it should have been flush. At the base, tucked behind the inner brass and snagged on a small screw, a length of black thread hung, waxed and stiff. A thin line of dust lay above it; below, the wood was cleaner, as if someone had brushed the area in haste.

Opposite the clock the weapons case kept its quiet. One mount was empty; the brass plaque beneath read IVORY REVOLVER. The velvet where a grip had lain was pressed down, an absence that made the rest of the row look oddly whole by comparison.

On Lucius Harben’s desk a sealed envelope sat with a shallow diagonal crease across the flap. When I tilted it the typed line showed through the thin paper, blunt and plain: “DO NOT SELL THE IVORY REVOLVER. THIS IS YOUR LAST WARNING.” I did not open it; forensics would want their say.

Anna Rowe stood in the doorway, practical and steady. She told me she had seen Mr. Harben at breakfast, just after eight, that Mrs. Harben had left for town a little after nine, and that Mr. Reed had called by later and left before eleven. She reminded me she’d been the one to find him at his desk and that he’d still been holding something in his hand when she did.

Iris Harben spoke to me in the small parlour off the foyer. She gave a neat account — she’d been in town that morning to discuss payments connected with the collection — and kept her voice level while she smoothed the edge of her hand against her wedding band. The motion was small and automatic, a private thing trying to steady public calm. She would not be drawn on the envelope; she said only that talk of sales had made her anxious for the house’s finances.

Thomas Reed was in the service room, his hands carrying the faint grains of his work. He said he’d been in the workshop across the square until just before eleven and that a neighbour could confirm it. He spoke quickly, with an impatience that shortened his answers, and kept rubbing the pad of his thumb and forefinger together in that restless way of men who handle small gears. When I asked about the disputed sale he gave a clipped account and shut the subject down.

Lane watched from the doorway as I closed matters with Thomas and then returned to the study. She repeated, without irony, the warning she’d given me at the start: “Don’t touch anything until forensics are here, Ellis.” Her look after the words said I could look and ask questions, but I must not be a clumsy hand. I told her I understood. That would have to be enough for now.

I went back into the study with Lane's warning folded into the hollow of a sentence I couldn't spend. She'd told me not to touch anything until forensics arrived; I left my hands in my coat and let my eyes do the work. The longcase clock took the far wall and held the light. The access panel at the back was a hair ajar; the brass inside looked thumbed, varnish rubbed away where skin or a tool had rested. Fine, fresh scores ringed the latch—hairline marks that flashed when the light caught them, as if a slender implement had been used to lever it.

At the base the black thread hung where I'd seen it: waxed, stiff, snagged on a screw. I followed its line: it looped and ran up toward the movement and vanished behind the plates. That was no stray sewing fibre; laid like that it could be a patient little mechanism, a thing that might nudge a wheel at an appointed minute.

Thomas Reed came in from the workshop, bringing the faint smell of oil with him. He had the thumb-and-forefinger rub that tells you a man is working through a thought. "You were in here recently?" I asked.

He met my gaze. "I was in three days ago to wind it and I stripped the weight-gear to clean the pivots—it's delicate work, Ellis." He paused, then added, "I was out of the shop just before eleven. Ask anyone." The admission sat in the room like a small brass cog: useful, precise, and easily checked.

"You left the panel closed?" I said.

"I always close it when I'm done," he said. He rubbed his thumb as if to work a stubborn splinter free. "I didn't kill him, if that's what you're getting at. I serviced it—winding, cleaning the pivots—but I was gone by then." His voice tightened on the last word.

Lucius's fingers curled around a torn scrap. The ragged edge lay on top of the knuckles; the torn fibres splayed against the skin rather than pressed into it. The blotter beneath showed a neat, round stain—the dark halo of a single shot. The scrap sat on that halo without a smear. If he'd clutched the paper in his last moments the fibres would have been pressed, ink and blood rubbed into the edge. This looked arranged.

"The scrap was put there after the fact," I said, careful to keep my tone even. Accusation is heavy; Lane was watching.

Thomas's face went slack. "Why would someone do that? To make it look—" He stopped.

Forensics arrived just then. They moved with the efficiency of people who practise restraint: a gloved hand to lift, a small vacuum for trace, a careful photograph. The tech at the desk tapped the envelope's diagonal crease with a latexed fingertip. "We lifted a partial print from the flap's crease," he said. "Preliminary lift consistent with Iris Harben."

Iris came in as if the name had called her. She kept her composure; the small motion of smoothing her wedding band was the private thing I'd noticed before. When I asked about the sale and the note she answered with a bluntness that might be armour. "If he sells it, we have nothing left but his arrogance."

For a moment the study felt like a display cabinet for evidence: the stopped hands at eleven-seventeen, the access panel scored with fresh marks, the black thread threading toward the gears, a torn note that read like stagecraft, and an envelope that now carried a lifted print. I kept my hands where Lane could see them and stepped to the clock's case without touching the latch. Forensics had the gloves and the reasons.

What I thought, and was careful not to say out loud, was simple and dangerous: this clock had become more than a piece on a mantel. If someone clever—or cruel—had used the train as a timer, a clock could do a very private kind of violence.

I said it out slow, because the room needed slow sentences. Lane let my voice do the work while the techs kept their gloves on and their gloved hands where they belonged.

Forensics pried free a false rear shelf and photographed it; a technician eased a gloved finger into the gap and, after a careful shot, showed me the pictures. The images, and what the tech described, made the rest plain: a shallow cavity sat behind the shelf, the exact dimensions a small revolver's grip would have occupied. "There was a false shelf inside the case, a shallow cavity exactly the size the ivory's grip would have filled," I said, because saying it made it a thing we could look at instead of a story I was telling myself.

Photographs showed the revolver seated on a thin brass shim, its gilding marked with clock oil, a neat curled shaving of brass beside it where the shim had been trimmed. The waxed black thread that had been threaded into the movement ran into that cavity and was fastened to a small bent bracket on the gun's trigger-guard. The scores and hairline scrapes around the access panel suggested the bracket had been fitted and the work concealed.

Putting those pieces together suggested the mechanism. The clock's strike train would deliver a reliable tug at a set minute; the thread, fixed to the bracket, would pull the trigger. The revolver, bedded on the trimmed shim, could discharge toward the blotter. The shim's trimmed edge and the cavity's profile, as seen in the photographs, were consistent with a rebound that allowed the barrel to slip partly back into the hollow; a small notch or catch beneath the barrel could then take a rim as the case settled, leaving the access panel to sit crooked as if latched from inside. Those were not miracles — the photographs and the bracket's thread attachment suggested a plain, mechanical series of events.

I kept to the evidence because Lane's warning was still a thing I'd agreed to: don't touch until forensics. I did not touch. I described what their hands had revealed.

Thomas, who'd serviced the clock, rubbed his thumb and forefinger the way he always did and said the thing I'd already been given to expect: "I didn't kill him, Ellis. I was there days ago to service the clock, but I left before eleven." His admission of recent access was a cog in the machine of motive and opportunity, but his words were the same ones he'd given me before.

I set out the paper trail. The typed envelope on the desk bore the blunt warning about the ivory; forensics had lifted a partial print from the flap's diagonal crease that they described as consistent with Iris Harben. The torn scrap in Lucius's hand, on inspection, had the look of being placed afterward — fibres lying on top of the knuckles, no transfer staining. The pattern of those facts together suggested staging: a threat that tied to a sale, a missing ivory, a note put in place to mislead.

Iris's hand found her wedding band as if by habit. There was no theatrical refusal in her face; there was the small, unbearable plainness of someone calculating loss and risk. I said, without flourish, what the collection and the clock and the threat all meant together.

She answered in a voice that did not bother to be theatrical: "I couldn't let him sell it. I knew where he kept it. I did what I thought would save us." It wasn't the clean, legal confession a court prefers; it was a thing said to stop me explaining.

Lane stepped forward then, with the economy of someone who has to close an investigation without theatrics. "Mrs. Harben, you'll be formally held pending further inquiries," she said. Her words did what they were meant to do: they made a legal incision where the night had been a gape.

We had paid off some of the counting that had been owed this case: the revolver had been found in the clock's cavity on a trimmed shim; the thread, the bracket and the tool-marks all suggested a single plan; Thomas's statement left him not cleared but not pinned, for now. One thing remained off the table — a small cameo watch from Mrs. Harben's mother's display, missing when I asked about it. It wasn't where she'd said it would be.

I folded that absence into a pocket like a question I was not yet prepared to answer. "I'll find it," I told them. Lane's look said she neither endorsed nor objected. Some things the law collects; some things it leaves you to pry out by hand.
