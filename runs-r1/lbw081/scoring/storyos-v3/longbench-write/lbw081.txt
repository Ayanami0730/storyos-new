Inspector Milne had his hand on the bolt when I came through the doorway, as if the iron itself were an answer he could produce. He held it up between us and the room like a placard: closed, finished, uncomplicated.

The library smelled of old paper, lemon polish and the faint, metallic sourness that clings to dried blood. The oak door behind me was a blunt thing of good timber; Milne had shown me the bolt and said it had been turned from the inside. He wanted the scene to be tidy — a single line from problem to solution — and his impatience made the room feel smaller.

From where I stood the mantel dominated the west wall, a ribbon of carved wood and stone with the mantel clock perched like a small, solemn thing. The clock's face presented no work of time: its hands were rigid, arrested mid-gesture. I read later that the mechanism had stopped at 10:17; the note was dry and precise, the sort of entry made when someone decides it is better to have a measurement than a sense. The clock's brass was bright at the edges and dull where dust had settled; nothing about it suggested violence, only interruption.

Between the fireplace and the only exterior window a wide desk took the light. Lena Harrow had been at that desk. She sat now with her upper body pitched forward as if in the middle of writing a sentence that never finished. One arm lay across the blotter; the other clutched a small square of linen whose corners were stained a dark, thirsty red. The hand under it had closed until the fabric puckered.

Set deep into Lena's chest was a heavy brass letter-opener — the blunt, workmanlike kind kept for opening estate envelopes rather than as ornament. It had been driven in with force enough to bend the ribs faintly around it. The haft carried no neat fingerprint to place an ordinary hand; there was, however, a thin smear of oil along the wood, the kind left by someone whose hands had been working on machinery rather than on paper. The smear caught the light at the edge, almost apologetic.

The window was the library's only break to the outside, a single-pane sash with a heavy latch that closed against the frame. On the outer sill, tucked into the ivy so it did not show unless you leaned, I found a short length of cord and a little loop of metal serving as a makeshift anchor. The housekeeper's note in the bag read that it was an improvised pulley anchor, hidden in the ivy; it looked exactly the sort of small, practical modification you make when you have a task to do and do not wish to be observed.

Milne kept returning, in his mind, to the bolt. "We lock it, we call it something neat," he said at one point, rubbing his jaw. He wanted a verdict on the case as if paper and procedure could smooth away questions. He spoke of drafting the file by teatime; his timetable was a pressure in the room.

I did not want neat. I wanted trouble. The misfits are the thing that opens a locked house: an oil smear where a hand should carry prints, a pulley anchored where no respectable customer would fit it, a handkerchief with a monogram that did not belong to the dead woman. The handkerchief's initials were not listed on the slip — they were simply noted as present — and that absence kept the name inside the cloth like a secret. Whoever had clenched it had made certain of being remembered, or of not being recognised at all.

It was small things that made me lean forward and look twice: the tack of polishing wax on the desk lip, the faint crescent of cigar ash on the sill that did not match Lena's habits, the particular way the clock's hands burned at 10:17 on a brass face now still. Milne saw disorder where I saw opportunity; he wanted to pin the scene with his bolt and leave. I wanted to find the thread that would unravel the neatness.

I ran a fingertip along the desk edge without touching where the opener had been, feeling for the ghost of oil under the varnish. Outside, the ivy moved with a dry, patient sound. Inside, a clock kept the exact wrong time. Milne asked if I had a theory. I told him not yet — that is the moment when an open case becomes one worth solving.

I began with Henry because that is what happens in houses where the oldest grievance sits in the family name. He was polished enough to look like a verdict handed down — collar neat, gloves folded in his lap — and he had the kind of impatience that becomes a public virtue in a man who thinks time will put things right for him.

"I was in the smoking-room all evening," he said, short as a docket. "Mrs Harrow and I argued, certainly. We argued about money and the will. But I did not go near the library after nine." His fingers found the rim of the armchair as if they needed the furniture for a witness.

I watched him while Milne made a note and pretended not to. Crowe is not a name I used in that room; I am the man who asks how firmly someone remembers the furniture. Henry's answer was neat and partial: an alibi without an attendant clock. He had motive, which is not the same as guilt, and he had the look of a man who expected that to be sufficient.

Mary was smaller in stature and larger in everything else — loyalty, constancy, the quiet kind of fury that has kept a house running longer than any carpenter. Her hands smelled of lemon polish when she offered them; she set herself between Henry and the desk the way a human screen sets itself between a fire and the furniture.

"I was downstairs, laying out the supper," she said. "I heard nothing until Mr Dane came in shouting."

She did not look at Henry when she said it; she did not need to. Her first impulse was to protect the dead woman she had served for thirty years. When I showed her the folio she stiffened, like someone touching a private letter.

"Lena kept lists," Mary said. "Names, times, the sort of thing that keeps a house honest."

Henry's mouth tightened. "Names? Times?" he said. "What would she have been scribbling about me for?"

That was the moment the argument over the folio rose, small and contained, like steam from a kettle. Henry insisted the book was proof — proof that Lena had kept a tally against him. Mary said the book was a ledger of groceries and coal and the petty irritations of an elderly woman who wanted order. Oliver, who had been standing with his cap in his hands as if it were the only thing he could offer, said nothing at first and then, in a voice that sounded like a gate unlatched, "She writes down when people come, sir. She notes times."

Oliver's hands were work-roughened; he did not argue so much as catalogue his resentments. "We had words about pay the week before," he admitted when pressed. "But I was at the greenhouse most of the day — ask the men. I don't spend my nights in the library."

The dust and voices of the room were handy witnesses while I fed them small attention. I moved to the desk and opened the folio. The book sat like any other object that has been allowed to carry a life: corners softened, a leather thumb-worn where Lena's fingers had turned pages. One page had been torn cleanly away.

A clean tear is a message. It suggests intention rather than accident. I ran a fingertip along the paper where the missing sheet had once lain and found, near the sill, a fresh smear of oil that caught the light the way a small lie catches the interlocutor's eye. It ran to the folio's edge and stopped as if whoever had placed it there had checked with their hand and then withdrawn.

On the sill sat the kitchen ashtray, a neat crescent of cigar ash in it. The inventory noted cigar ash on the sill; the catalogue made a suggestion elsewhere that a brand was favoured by Henry, but here, in the room and in the record before me, there was no tidy stamp that would pin the ash to him. Milne rubbed his jaw; his timetable wanted that neatness.

"We can finish this, Mr Crowe," he said, too loudly. "There are enough reasons to call this tidy."

I answered him the way I always do: no. There were misfits here still — a torn page, an oil smear, an ash-stained sill and three people whose alibis fit only as far as their own mouths would carry them. I wanted more than reason; I wanted a pattern. Milne wanted a tidy explanation. The difference was not polite.

Oliver left to speak to the men. Mary hovered by the door like someone who guards secrets the way others guard jewels. Henry lit nothing but impatience and left in a direction that suggested a study. I closed the folio and put it back where it belonged; the book had given me a question, not an answer. The locked-room explanation, whatever it would be, remained unopened.

It was close to midnight when I went back to the library. Milne had locked the house and left his notes on the dresser; the house smelled of lemon polish and the cold, metallic memory of blood, and under the moon the ivy at the outer sill looked like a dark hand pressed to the brick.

I took the mantel clock's face into my hands first, because the clock keeps promises even when people do not. The hands were frozen where they had been frozen earlier — that detail had been recorded — and I did not need to invent anything more of it. What I wanted was not what time the clock stopped, but how quickly a small, deliberate action could be finished and the room restored to its apparent loneliness.

Outside the window the little loop of metal and the short cord lay where Mary had said they were, tucked low in the ivy so you missed them if you were not looking for them. The loop caught the beam of my torch and glinted like a nail-head. There were no measured dimensions, no forensic photographs I could consult; there was only what the hand could feel and the eye could test. That absence matters. It was worth saying plainly: there were no recorded dimensions or scuff photographs of the sill for me to work from.

I threaded a spare cord through the loop and tied it to the heft of the opener. The letter-opener was the sort of blunt brass thing Lena kept for estate envelopes — heavy in a way that made it obedient to a short swing, as long as my palm and thick enough that the haft filled the hand. It carried the same thin smear of oil along the haft that we had noted in the room; the oil caught the light like a small confession.

Balancing the opener on a short tag of cord, I pushed the sash just enough to take the tiniest of gaps. The glass did not need to be thrown open; a thumb's breadth and a practiced hand are all the operator needs. With the opener lashed to the line, I could thrust it through and withdraw cleanly; with a single, smooth pull the sash came down and settled as though no hand had been inside it at all. The cord ran back to the loop and took up easily on a single-handed draw.

I took out my watch and counted the moves. From sash to thrust to withdrawal and the final pull that set the glass neat again: less than a minute. The mantel clock had been stopped at the time recorded in the report; my trial showed that the whole intervention could be made in the space of a short tick — quick enough to be consistent with a single, sudden interruption. I do not pretend that measured precision lives in words like "less than a minute," but it was the sort of shortness that makes an alibi brittle.

There were loose things I could not fix that night. The loop's exact attachment — whether it had been tied to a screw, hooked, or simply tucked in — was not written in the file. I could feel it take a load; I could not, there in the dark, say how it had been fixed into the stone. Likewise, there was no measured note of scuff marks on the outer sill and no previous record that Oliver had ever used that route. Those absences are a different kind of evidence: not of what was, but of what has yet to be asked.

I ran the test twice, and on the second try I let the opener bite into a pad of cloth I had tucked against the sash to simulate flesh. It drove home with the blunt, mechanical authority of the instrument we had found in Lena's chest; the oil on the haft smeared on the cloth in a way that looked right. When I pulled the cord back the opener came free and the sash fell. Watching the movement, I thought of the torn page in the folio and the smear that had run to its edge — hands that knew cords and clocks more than they knew people.

That was when the sill moved beside me. Someone's weight found the outer ledge; a booted shape threw its shadow into the window's thinner light. I had not heard the foot come. A hand closed on my wrist with a pressure that was not quite friendly; it was the kind of quick, practical grip that says do not waste breath.

"You shouldn't be here," Oliver said, hard enough that the name would have been the sort of thing you bark when you meant a threat. He smelled of cold earth and tobacco. His other hand went for the loop and began to wind the cord away as if he meant to make the thing vanish while I still had fingers on it.

I let him keep the grip. I remember the exactness of the moment — the way his thumb caught the cord and the small flash of oil against the brass — because it proved something I had only been able to show in demonstration: someone had wanted the mechanism unfound.

"I'm not here to make trouble," I said. "I'm trying to see if it's possible." I felt him evaluate the truth of that, like a man weighing coins. He did not answer. He released my wrist but did not step back.

When I came away from the sill the house seemed colder for it. The experiment had given me a plausible mechanism and a clock within which it could have been executed; Oliver's interruption had given me a motive for silence. I would bring Milne back before daylight and stage the thing properly. I meant to make them watch the same movements by daylight, with a note-book and a camera. The locked-room was not yet explained; it was something to be proved.

I asked them to come back in the morning. Daylight makes liars smaller and mechanisms larger; Milne arrived with his notebook like a blunt instrument, Mary with her hands folded as if she might lay them over the truth, Oliver with his face the colour of peat, and Henry as composed as a man who expects a room to agree with him.

I set the window as I had the night before: a short loop of cord through the little metal eye in the ivy, the letter-opener lashed to it so the haft hung true. I placed a cloth against the sash to take the bite; I put the clock where the hands could no longer be trusted and told Milne to time me with his watch. They watched where people watch at trials — not at the instrument, but at the person who uses it.

My hands are better at small betrayals than at speeches. I threaded the cord through the loop again, felt the loop take the weight with a little give, and let the opener rest in my palm. The brass was cold; it filled my hand the way a small animal fills a glove. I thumbed the gap on the sash — a thumb's breadth, just enough; the glass yielded like skin. I pushed the opener through, feeling the resistance of the cloth as it closed about metal. The haft slipped, the oil on it catching the light like a dry promise.

There are sounds no one notices until you make them matter: the shush of cord through ivy, the faint drag as metal meets seal, the cloth's brief, surprised surrender. I drew the opener free, wound the cord a single, sure turn, and with a small pull the sash settled as if it had never been moved. Milne's watch ran the seconds out loud; when I let my fingers go he said the number into the air: "Less than a minute." It was the sort of shortness that unmakes alibis.

I did not let the demonstration speak for the whole case. I laid the things out on the desk: the folio with the ragged gap where a page had been torn cleanly away; the smear of oil that ran to the folio's edge and stopped; the handkerchief from Lena's palm, its corners blood-darkened. Under daylight the monogram was still smudged and unreadable — no tidy initials caught the eye. I did not pretend the cloth named him; its letters were, for all the room's clear light, illegible.

I placed the ashtray where it had been; the household recognised the brand by habit even if the laboratory had not stamped it for the file. The oil on the opener matched the smear by the folio in the way that a fingerprint matches a face: not absolute proof, but a joining of small things. Whoever could make the sash open from outside with a loop in the ivy and an obedient weight could thrust a blunt instrument through the gap and withdraw it without leaving the window visibly disturbed. Whoever could do that would know to smudge oil on the haft and on a page to make the trace look like toil rather than malice.

It was Henry who answered the chain I had laid. He had kept his face like a ledger's cover for too long. Milne asked the question I wanted him to: "Where were you between ten and half past?"

Henry's gloves came off in a small, quick gesture. "I went to her window," he said. The words came out flat, like a coin on a table. "I couldn't stand her tallying me — the things she had written. She loved that folio more than she loved me. I meant to go, to frighten her, to make her stop — I didn't think it would be like that." His hand shook, as if he had only just felt how heavy the opener had been.

Henry admitted, on the record, that he feared the ledger's entries about him and that fear had driven him to act. He told them how he'd fixed the loop in the ivy, knotted the cord, made the opener a weight and a blade for the same motion, and how he'd pulled the sash neat again. He admitted the torn page. "I took the sheet out. I could not have it in her book. It would have ruined me." In his words the quarrel over money and the will took a sharper shape: not only dispute, but dread of a ledger that would read him bankrupt in reputation.

Milne's notebook closed with a dull, practiced noise. He did not find the case tidy; he found it finished. He stepped forward when Henry's anger ran to a thin, legal thing and took him by the wrist. Mary said nothing at first; Oliver's hands were empty of tools but full of relief. I thought then of small proofs and larger truths: the demonstration had made the how visible; a man's admission had made the why indisputable.

Milne wanted neat words for the file. I wanted the sort of certainty that does not fit neatly on a form. I let him have the paperwork. For once, paper and blunt instruments agreed.
