Harrow met me at the study door, clipboard at an angle that said he was not entirely sure whether I was help or hindrance. Evelyn stood in the hall like a photograph you could not quite pick up; gloves folded in her hands, expression composed to the point of polish. Hale lingered at the corridor’s edge, fingers working at his jacket as if there were something there they would not let go of. Marta stood near the hearth, apron neat, a faint smudge at the cuff. No one shouted. There was no immediate need for theatre.

From the hall the deadbolt showed as engaged; the metal sat flush in its mortice and the door did not betray even the faintest give. The sash window’s catch sat flush in its slot; the glass threw back the garden in a steady, dull rectangle with no light slipping beneath the frame. "Latched," I said, because facts demand a ledger before hands do anything with them.

Lionel Ashby was at his desk as if someone had closed a book over him—head tipped, shoulders slack, the kind of neat ruin a man makes when he keeps things orderly even in the dark. The oak desk still held the impression of his weight. On the mantel, a small brass clock had stopped: its hands fixed at 11:17, stubborn and precise like an accusation.

The desk drawer that should have carried his brass dagger-shaped opener was open and light. The felt groove where the letter-opener belonged gaped empty.

The hearth held two peculiarities. One was a scrap of paper, burned along one edge and crumpled into a hesitant confession; the only legible mark was a single underlined word: 'trust'. The other was a single long match lodged at an angle among the coal—too deliberately placed to be mere detritus. The matchbox, a thin thing made for long striking matches, lay on the mantel with its cover half-askew.

The inner ledge of the study window bore a smear of fresh mud across its lip, and the painted inner sill showed a pale scuff where wood had briefly protested contact. No glass was broken. No hinges were bent. The house showed no sign of forced entry.

All that made a tidy paradox: the deadbolt drawn, the sash latched, Lionel dead at his desk, the small brass opener gone, a half-burnt scrap with 'trust' underlined, a long match set oddly in the hearth, fresh mud on the garden-sill and a scuff on the inner window—details that belonged to separate crimes if you were greedy for simplicity.

I do not like tidy paradoxes. I like them because they force you to choose between being clever and being careful.

Harrow watched me with the polite suspicion of a man who mistrusts improvisation. "We have the house sealed," he said. "No one in or out since you arrived."

"For now," I answered. I moved through the room slowly, letting my eyes do what fingers must not yet. The clock’s face, the empty felt groove, the underlined 'trust', the long match, the smear of mud and the scuff—those were the pieces. They made a sentence I could not finish yet. For the moment they were simply contradictions pressed close together.

When I looked back, the three people in the hall looked, respectively, like the problem in its three useful states: Evelyn, still and careful; Hale, a man who looked surprised at his own hands; Marta, tidy, watchful. The problem had been set. It was now my job to find who had tried to tidy the answers and who had merely rearranged the chairs.
