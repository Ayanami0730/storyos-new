### previous-scene  <novel/chapters/ch-01/scenes/s-001.md>

I arrived at Hale Mansion close to nine and found a party that had been turned polite and small by a corpse. The house kept its old shape — steep roof, stone steps, a lantern guttering at the porch — and inside the air smelled of tea gone cold and varnish. I tell you this because detail is the only thing that steadies you in a room where someone has just stopped being anything at all.

Victor Hale lay in his study as if his last conversation had been with the fabric of his own chair. He was slumped in the heavy upholstered seat by the mantel, the chair itself looking as if it had held him for decades: "Victor's heavy upholstered study chair — the site where he was found slumped. It bears old repairs and a faint circular wear spot on the left rear seam." That line is not mine; it is the chair. It was where he had stopped.

The study's door was locked from the inside, and every window in that room was latched. I saw the latch on the high window and a hand had banged the lock closed. The household had not forced anything; the scene was intact in the way a watch is intact when it simply will not run. There was, on inspection, no conventional weapon in the room. Whoever had done this had left no knife on the carpet, no blunt instrument on the desk: only the body and its last arrangements.

On his person lay his watch — gold, small, ruined in one graceful way: "Victor's gold pocket watch found stopped on his person; the minute hand bent and the watch stopped at a time relevant to establishing the minute of death." The watch had indeed stopped. The minute hand had been bent; the watch read 9:14 p.m. I note the time because I have to. The mantel clock, which had been Victor's pride, gave me the measure of the house: "The mantel clock, a gilt 19th-century piece, chimes every quarter-hour; its mechanism was Victor's pride." It chimes in the old pattern — one at the quarter, two at the half, three at the three-quarters, four on the hour — and in the hall the chime had already marked the evening for us once.

In the foyer the household and the night's guests had been gathered. Lucy Hale stood by the newel, hands clasped so tight the knuckles showed white; she was plainspoken in her grief and in her fear — inheritance anxiety, you could call it, though that is a small thing when a man is dead. Alfred Broome, the butler, kept his face nearly blank; the stoic, old-school air of a man who has spent his life managing other people's exactness. I had the sense — and I say this as a writer's choice of what to stage, not as evidence yet in someone's testimony — that Alfred's fingers were oil-stained, that the old railway machinist had the calm of someone familiar with springs and clockwork.

Owen Myers hovered, aggrieved and quick to snap a remark; he is a clock repairman and collector, skilled with small mechanisms and apt to quarrel with Victor over the worth of a thing. Evelyn Ross smiled with the thin courtesy of business; she was the collector who wanted what Victor kept, every polite syllable counting toward acquisition. Between them the motive map was plain: the draft of a new will had been found on Victor's desk and it suggested he intended to cut someone out — "A draft copy of Victor's new will, suggesting he intended to cut someone out; its existence supplies motive." That document sat in the study like a promise and a threat both.

The study itself offered a handful of physical cues that I filed in the order my hands could reach them: a faint abrasion at the chair frame, dust pressed and tufted where someone had shifted, a bent minute hand on a dead watch that read 9:14, and the worn, circular spot on the left rear seam of the chair where a pocket or a shoulder might have rubbed for years. The mantel clock's mechanism — Victor's pride — would be the measure by which we might place a minute to a sound.

A locked door, latched windows, a stopped watch, a worn patch on a chair, a draft will and a company of people with reasons to want a change. That is what the house offered me when it opened its door. I stood in the foyer, the mantle chiming, and promised myself I would make the room speak.
