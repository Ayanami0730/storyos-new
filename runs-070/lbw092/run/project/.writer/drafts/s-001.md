Elisande was early to the terrace because she had practiced patience like a small art. The stone bench was already warm from the afternoon sun; the clipped roses smelled faintly of copper and dust. She kept her hands folded in her lap, the way a lady hid the readiness of her fingers, and watched the path where the servants crossed below the hedges.

Rowan came up the gravel with the long, sure stride of a man who had measured every foot of Garrick grounds. Soil freckled his jacket cuff. He carried nothing but a small twig of hawthorn—stolen from a hedge, trimmed, and left with the plain intention of a gift. When he saw her he hesitated, as if the sight of her could unseat him.

"You are early," he said, and his voice settled across the terrace like a well-worn blanket.

"The afternoon promises little else but light," Elisande answered. Her voice was trained to be airy, but it held an edge: a courtly cadence that named how she had been taught to speak even when she did not mean the thing she said.

Ana was already there, kneeling like a small conspirator by the herb box, smoothing a pleat in the hem of her mistress's gown. She looked up and met Elisande's eyes with the plain, frank expression that made confession easier than a sermon.

"He came an hour ago and watered by the north border," Ana said, not as news but as a favour returned.

Rowan set the hawthorn on the bench between them and bowed his head in a private, clumsy way. He had a habit of making things small and certain: a carved horse hidden in a stable, the inside of a pocket stitched with a neat, straight line. Tonight there was a new certainty on his palm.

Elisande loosened the small chain at her throat. The onyx locket slipped from under her gown like a secret surfacing; the stone was cool and black, dull where fingers had worn it. Inside, she kept the hair she had wrapped from her mother's braid and a pressed scrap of blue blossom. She carried tradition like an ache.

She set the locket in Rowan's open hand the way one sets a consecrated thing into another's palm. For a moment his fingers closed around it and the two of them looked like two halves of an old promise.

"Not a priest, never that, not here," Elisande said quickly, and the words were not a denial so much as a careful map. "But I give you this, if you will have it. Keep it when I am foolish or when the household is stern. Keep it when your hands are full of hay and mud and when my dresses make me forget how to be small. Keep it because it is mine." 

Rowan tilted the locket toward the light and glanced at the tiny blossom pressed inside. He touched it with the tip of a finger—his gesture was practical, as if testing whether the thing would survive. "I will keep it," he said. "I will keep it and I will bring it back when you ask. I am not a man with papers and oaths, Lady Elisande, but I will guard what you give me." His voice did not call her lady so much as name the distance he accepted.

She leaned forward and laid a single rose petal—folded small until it fit beneath the onyx lid—inside the hollow with deliberate fingers. It was the small, wilting white of a stall-grown blossom, not the polished roses of the parterres. "A thing that will go back and forth, then," she said. "Not law, not a contract. We cannot be married by the household, Rowan. This is ours, and only ours." 

Ana watched with a loosed breath. She had watched the two of them meet like this twice a week for months, slipping into the terrace as if into the margins of a book. She knew the small practises that steadied Elisande—a certain poem murmured when the sun dipped—or the knot Rowan tied in the end of a thread when he was thinking of leaving. She had learned to fold the knowledge into a pocket and walk on with the dutiful tread of a maid who kept confidences.

When the locket was closed, Rowan looped the chain over his neck with a tenderness that made Ana's hand go to her mouth. Elisande, suddenly smaller in the world and therefore more herself, brushed a smudge of soil from his cheek with the inside of her wrist, as if such a domesticity could stand between them and ruin.

They sat for a while with their silence full of small customs: the rustle of clipped hedges, a boat churning far on the river, a distant clang from the kitchens where footboys ran messages. The terrace was a stepped fold of garden—tiers of clipped yew that rose to a low stone balustrade along the river, a narrow outer path where servants passed between stables and kitchens, and the bench set near the eastern corner beneath a trellis. From the manor's windows you could look down into the terrace if you leaned and looked with purpose; but the hedges and balustrade broke sightlines enough to make private things possible.

As dusk paled, Ana plucked at her apron. She walked the path back toward the steps and paused, frowning. Near the lower gravel, where footboys often slipped notes between the kitchens and the steward, a folded scrap of paper lay half-shaded. The seal of red wax was whole.

She did not break it. She bent, scooped the letter into her palm, and pressed it to her chest as if to hide the small heat of it. Her face had a new smallness in it—one that had less to do with duty and more with fear.

Across the bench, Rowan adjusted the locket against his collar. Elisande smiled, a quick, private thing, and reached for his hand. They squeezed each other's fingers once, the motion neat and decisive, then let go.

None of them saw the letter's meaning yet. None of them guessed the way a folded sentence could kick a stair loose beneath the household's order. Ana tucked the word into the pocket of her apron and turned back up the steps, keeping the wax unbroken and her trouble held tight.
