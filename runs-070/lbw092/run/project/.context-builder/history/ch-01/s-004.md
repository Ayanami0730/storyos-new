### world-rules  <world/rules.yaml>

These govern what is TRUE. They do not say what anyone KNOWS.
A character knows a rule only where canon says so; otherwise they may be ignorant of it, wrong about it, or in the middle of working it out — and if the story is about someone discovering one of these, writing it into their thoughts early is how that story gets destroyed.

- No supernatural elements or anachronistic technology — the story is grounded in a quasi-medieval household world.
- Household law: the lord has the social and legal authority within his household to arrange his heir's marriage and to punish servants for crimes (including conspiracy or aiding escape). Secret tokens or promises carry emotional meaning only; they confer no legal standing.
- Marriage is a legal/sacral act that requires the lord's approval (or a priest with jurisdiction). A clandestine private promise between a noble and a servant does not create a recognized marriage in this world.
- Social mobility is limited: servants cannot inherit land or titles; they can be freed, bound, or punished, but any change to status must be explicitly recorded if it is to be a fact later in the manuscript.
- No deus ex machina: once a scene sets a fact (a letter intercepted, an order given), later scenes must carry its consequences — events are causal and traceable.
- Objects introduced (hairpin, sealed letter) are physical evidence: their existence, who possesses them, and any inscriptions or marks must be recorded in the index if later referenced.

The great hall smelled of rushes and roasted meat and the bright scraping of forks on wood. At the high dais Lord Harrow sat like a careful sentence, and when the hall quieted he let it be known in the way that made conversation fold into obedience.

"Aldric brings Westbrook's levies and a fine marriage with no scandal," he announced, and the words rolled across the benches like a struck bell. Aldric stood by the dais—neat-booted, practiced in the art of polite distance—accepting congratulations with a measured bow. Around the hall faces bloomed into smiles that were both sincere and required; Elys felt the room press at her shoulders as if a cloak had been tightened.

Applause clapped like trapped birds. Elys's hands were clamshells in her lap; she did not look at Aldric. Marta watched from the servants' aisle, her mouth a fold of something that did not quite fit the expression called for above her station.

Outside, beneath the eaves, Garr moved with the quiet efficiency of a man packing what must be carried and leaving what cannot. He had the sealed scrap beneath his tunic—warm against his chest, the wax pressed to a corner—where he'd tucked it for safe keeping. He ran a hand over the leather as if the paper itself might be soothed.

A whisperless stir came from the doorway: Captain Rowan, with two men at his back, had been sent to make a routine check of the yard. Stage direction: Lord Harrow's steward, having noticed hurried movement among the stables after the announcement, sent Rowan to the stables to ensure everything was in order. Rowan's duty, and the household's unease, collided in the doorway.

Rowan's voice was low when he stepped forward to Garr. "We are checking the yard," he said. His eyes flicked to Marta, who had been tending a tray nearby. "Marta—fetch Garr's tunic. Bring it here."

She looked as if someone had wound the air too tight around her ribs. In a voice that was both a confession and a plea, she answered, "I kept their hour like a prayer; they have asked me to unmake that prayer." Her hand trembled as she moved toward the tack-room. The line was a thing dropped between them; it named both what she had protected and the small treason she now performed.

Marta returned with the tunic and handed it over with fingers that would not meet Rowan's face. One of the men ripped at a seam, and a scrap of paper tumbled into his palm—folded, stained at the corner with wax. Rowan took it as if it were a verdict.

He broke the seal with his thumb and read. The hall's din seemed to shrink; the paper's meaning made a private road between him and the dais. He turned, the words gone from his mouth but the command ready.

Lord Harrow read what was shown to him. The lord's face did not flame; it set. He lifted the scrap and the wax between his fingers as evidence, and his voice when he spoke carried the weight of household law.

"This is enough," he said. "Captain Rowan—see that Garr is brought here. Take him from the yard to the courtyard. Let none hinder you."

Rowan folded and held the order like a thing he would answer. For a single beat, his hand hovered at the pommel of the door as if he could choose a different path. Then he exhaled, the sound small and private. "Orders are weights I carry, Lord; they do not make the carrying easy," he said, and his words were apology and oath braided into one.

Two guards moved quickly. Garr, still near the stables, heard the call and braced, but he did not flee—there was nowhere to go without dragging more ruin. Marta watched him with an expression that mixed rescue and remorse; her hands were empty where they had once smoothed a cuff.

The men took Garr by the arms with a practiced surety and threaded him through the servants' passages into the courtyard. The household watched as if watching a lesson. Elys, who had been told of nothing but fate, felt the world narrow to the sight of Garr's back as he was led away.

In the courtyard the air tasted of cold stone and the horse-heat that lingered in the stable doors. Garr's wrists were not yet bound, but the distance he had imagined between himself and the future had been shortened to a measured step toward the dais. The sealed scrap had been placed on the steward's table; a guard kept watch like a small, bright accusation.

Marta stood where she had been made to stand and pressed her palms together as if to still them. She had kept their hour; she had unmade it. The hall resumed its long appetite and Lord Harrow's voice—the voice that rearranged names and marriages—went on with the business of congratulations and duty. Garr was taken toward judgment.

Marta's eyes flicked to the corridor and then back; when a serving girl passed, Marta tripped her with a smile and a question; the sweep was delayed, the corridor quieted.

"Take it, Garr," Elys said, the line as private as a coin counted in the dark, and he closed his hand around the ivory as if she had lent him a piece of herself.

They spoke in the geography of what was possible and what was not. "They mean to call him Aldric," Elys said, watching his face. "They say Lord Harrow is pleased. He said at table—'Aldric brings Westbrook's levies and a fine marriage with no scandal.'"

Garr's mouth thinned. "Westbrook's levies won't carry my name anywhere but a ledger. I know the back lanes, the river and the mill. I can take a horse and hide a head behind a plough. But I am not the sort to promise more than bread."

Marta leaned nearer, voice dropped to a fretted practicalness. "You have a narrow hour after vespers when the house is noisy and a good fence in the east hedge where the ground dips. I can leave the west shutters unlatched and tell Jory the pantry is missing a tray. I will not be an accomplice in a sermon, but I will put you outside the notice of men who count names." Her hands smoothed the hem of her apron as she spoke; the action was small comfort and small treason.

Later, when the corridor had quieted and the serving girls had been shepherded back, Elys climbed the narrow stairs and stood on the west balcony for a quieter breath. The wood smelled faintly of cut herbs and the far smoke of the kitchens; below, Garr waited where the hedge made its pocket of dark.

"Your hands are cold," Garr said when she leaned on the rail. He lifted his palm and let her fingers rest against the willow's carved leaves.

They did not speak of routes as if hope were a map to be measured—only of what Garr could do, plain as a working man's chores. "Then if we go," he said, steadying the word, "we go on what I can promise. I'll write. I'll fold a note and seal it honest with wax. If I put it where only I can find it, you'll know I mean it."

He drew a scrap of paper from his pouch and with a coin pressed a warm blob of wax to the corner. The fold closed like a small decision. He slipped the sealed scrap beneath his tunic and laid a hand over it.

"I'll write it tonight," he said. The sentence felt like a ladder rung offered to a hand.

Elys stood a moment longer on the balcony watching him below, tracing the willow's tiny carved leaves with her fingertip. Then she turned and went down the stairs again, the wood beneath her feet a practiced sound, and returned to the bench where Garr waited. The garden folded them both back into its hush: Marta to her shadow by the hedge, Garr with the willow-pin like a private law in his palm, the letter warm against his chest. The house settled; the east hedge kept its dark. In the pocket under his tunic the sealed scrap waited, patient as a thing that must be kept alive until needed.
