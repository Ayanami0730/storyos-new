### logline  <novel/outline/logline.md>

In the austere halls of Harrowhold, Lord Harrow's daughter Elys falls for Garr, a stable-hand; their tender, clandestine love collides with duty and status when a marriage is arranged for Elys. A betrayed plan and an intercepted letter force a cruel choice: order will be upheld at the expense of the two who loved across the divide.

- loc-harrowhold: Harrowhold: the lord's manor — stone house with a great hall, a high balcony over the gardens, a walled garden, stables and a paved courtyard. It is both a home and a small, ordered world.
- loc-walled-garden: The walled garden of Harrowhold: a secluded patch of fruit trees, clipped hedges and an old stone bench. The garden is where unequal people can meet unseen if they dare.
- loc-balcony: The west balcony of Elys's chamber: overlooking the gardens, a narrow, sheltered ledge where conversations can be lowered into the night.
- loc-great-hall: The great hall: the center of household life, where proclamations are made, alliances announced and the social choreography of the estate is performed.
- loc-courtyard: The paved courtyard before the servants' door and stables. A gateway between worlds and, in this story's end, the place where order will be publicly enforced.
- obj-hairpin-ivory: An ivory hairpin, carved with a tiny willow motif. Elys's keepsake; it becomes the lovers' token and the object that fuses intimacy to consequence.
- obj-letter-sealed: A folded and sealed note: an earnest, simple message from Garr that will be intercepted. The letter is a practical attempt at arranging escape that becomes evidence.

### world-rules  <world/rules.yaml>

These govern what is TRUE. They do not say what anyone KNOWS.
A character knows a rule only where canon says so; otherwise they may be ignorant of it, wrong about it, or in the middle of working it out — and if the story is about someone discovering one of these, writing it into their thoughts early is how that story gets destroyed.

- No supernatural elements or anachronistic technology — the story is grounded in a quasi-medieval household world.
- Household law: the lord has the social and legal authority within his household to arrange his heir's marriage and to punish servants for crimes (including conspiracy or aiding escape). Secret tokens or promises carry emotional meaning only; they confer no legal standing.
- Marriage is a legal/sacral act that requires the lord's approval (or a priest with jurisdiction). A clandestine private promise between a noble and a servant does not create a recognized marriage in this world.
- Social mobility is limited: servants cannot inherit land or titles; they can be freed, bound, or punished, but any change to status must be explicitly recorded if it is to be a fact later in the manuscript.
- No deus ex machina: once a scene sets a fact (a letter intercepted, an order given), later scenes must carry its consequences — events are causal and traceable.
- Objects introduced (hairpin, sealed letter) are physical evidence: their existence, who possesses them, and any inscriptions or marks must be recorded in the index if later referenced.
