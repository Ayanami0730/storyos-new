### world-rules  <world/rules.yaml>

These govern what is TRUE. They do not say what anyone KNOWS.
A character knows a rule only where canon says so; otherwise they may be ignorant of it, wrong about it, or in the middle of working it out — and if the story is about someone discovering one of these, writing it into their thoughts early is how that story gets destroyed.

- No supernatural elements or anachronistic technology — the story is grounded in a quasi-medieval household world.
- Household law: the lord has the social and legal authority within his household to arrange his heir's marriage and to punish servants for crimes (including conspiracy or aiding escape). Secret tokens or promises carry emotional meaning only; they confer no legal standing.
- Marriage is a legal/sacral act that requires the lord's approval (or a priest with jurisdiction). A clandestine private promise between a noble and a servant does not create a recognized marriage in this world.
- Social mobility is limited: servants cannot inherit land or titles; they can be freed, bound, or punished, but any change to status must be explicitly recorded if it is to be a fact later in the manuscript.
- No deus ex machina: once a scene sets a fact (a letter intercepted, an order given), later scenes must carry its consequences — events are causal and traceable.
- Objects introduced (hairpin, sealed letter) are physical evidence: their existence, who possesses them, and any inscriptions or marks must be recorded in the index if later referenced.

### previous-scene  <novel/chapters/ch-01/scenes/s-002.md>

They met first in the garden as they had the week before and the week before that—Elys slipping down the narrow stair from her chamber in the hour after vespers to sit on the old stone bench, Marta folding herself into the hedge like a knot of shadow while Garr lingered where the yew made a dark pocket. The house gave them its noise: chopping in the kitchens, laughter that was not theirs, the great hall settling into its long appetite.

The willow-pin was in Garr's palm from the last time they'd touched it, small and warm. He watched Elys across the bench as if the space between them were a measured thing that could be kept tidy. Marta's eyes flicked to the corridor and then back; when a serving girl passed, Marta tripped her with a smile and a question; the sweep was delayed, the corridor quieted.

"Take it, Garr," Elys said, the line as private as a coin counted in the dark, and he closed his hand around the ivory as if she had lent him a piece of herself.

They spoke in the geography of what was possible and what was not. "They mean to call him Aldric," Elys said, watching his face. "They say Lord Harrow is pleased. He said at table—'Aldric brings Westbrook's levies and a fine marriage with no scandal.'"

Garr's mouth thinned. "Westbrook's levies won't carry my name anywhere but a ledger. I know the back lanes, the river and the mill. I can take a horse and hide a head behind a plough. But I am not the sort to promise more than bread."

Marta leaned nearer, voice dropped to a fretted practicalness. "You have a narrow hour after vespers when the house is noisy and a good fence in the east hedge where the ground dips. I can leave the west shutters unlatched and tell Jory the pantry is missing a tray. I will not be an accomplice in a sermon, but I will put you outside the notice of men who count names." Her hands smoothed the hem of her apron as she spoke; the action was small comfort and small treason.

Later, when the corridor had quieted and the serving girls had been shepherded back, Elys climbed the narrow stairs and stood on the west balcony for a quieter breath. The wood smelled faintly of cut herbs and the far smoke of the kitchens; below, Garr waited where the hedge made its pocket of dark.

"Your hands are cold," Garr said when she leaned on the rail. He lifted his palm and let her fingers rest against the willow's carved leaves.

They did not speak of routes as if hope were a map to be measured—only of what Garr could do, plain as a working man's chores. "Then if we go," he said, steadying the word, "we go on what I can promise. I'll write. I'll fold a note and seal it honest with wax. If I put it where only I can find it, you'll know I mean it."

He drew a scrap of paper from his pouch and with a coin pressed a warm blob of wax to the corner. The fold closed like a small decision. He slipped the sealed scrap beneath his tunic and laid a hand over it.

"I'll write it tonight," he said. The sentence felt like a ladder rung offered to a hand.

Elys stood a moment longer on the balcony watching him below, tracing the willow's tiny carved leaves with her fingertip. Then she turned and went down the stairs again, the wood beneath her feet a practiced sound, and returned to the bench where Garr waited. The garden folded them both back into its hush: Marta to her shadow by the hedge, Garr with the willow-pin like a private law in his palm, the letter warm against his chest. The house settled; the east hedge kept its dark. In the pocket under his tunic the sealed scrap waited, patient as a thing that must be kept alive until needed.

The garden takes them like a secret: a pocket of late light pressed into stone and clipped hedges, apple-sweet air smelling faintly of the stables, the great house above them as solid and unmoving as a law. Marta stands a little back, half in shadow on the path where the hedges meet the wall. She is Elys's maid and confidante—pragmatic, watchful—and tonight she has the face of someone who has decided to be a sentinel rather than a snitch.

Elys sits, folding her hands over her skirts. The hairpin that lives with her, a thin thing of ivory, catches the last light—a willow carved on its length, slim as a reed. She has kept that pin on a low tray at her dressing table since she was a child; it is the sort of small thing a person names by habit and forgets is precious until someone else needs it.

Garr takes her hand without hurry. He watches her fingers, the careful way she lays out her words like stitches. For a moment the world narrows to the dip of her wrist and the pale gleam of the pin.

"Take it," she says, and the sound of her voice is less than a vow but more than a promise. "Take it, Garr."

He closes his hand around the thin ivory and tucks it into the rough of his palm as if expecting to hide something precious beneath skin used to work. The motion is small and final.

Marta's answer is a whisper that the leaves nearly steal. "I'll watch. I won't tell."

They sit with that—three people under a sky that forgets to judge them for the length of a breath. Above them, somewhere in the great hall, dinner is ending. At the head of the table Lord Harrow says little; his silence rearranges the room: the servants walk straighter, voices lower. His power is the architecture of the household, a constant that presses against the edges of gatherings and decisions.

Elys looks at Garr as if testing a truth against the feel of his face. "It is not law between us," she says, the sentence practical, like mending a seam. "We are not wed. We are two people who have found one another where the house said we could not."

He gives a laugh that is almost a breath. "No priest, no ring. I can't give you what the law gives, Elys. But I can give you this."

He stands and draws nearer, the grit of the yard in his boots. "I will come back," he says, and the words are plain as a pledge laid on wood.

"I will wait," she says, as plain and as absolute. The bench between them keeps their knees from touching, and still their bodies make a map of decisions—small, stubborn.

They do not speak of routes or times or foolish dreams. Garr's hands are rough around the ivory; Elys counts the willow's carved leaves the way other people count prayers. Marta lifts her chin, a concession and a guard.

When they part, it is by degrees: Garr slipping away to the stables' shadow, Elys to the narrow balcony that is more a promise than a door. The hairpin is in his palm; the garden keeps its hush. The household resumes its order above them, Lord Harrow's presence folded into the sound of footsteps and the closing of shutters.

On the stone bench the willow's tiny leaves throw a grin of shadow. The promise between them is not a binding; it is a private law they give one another, absolute in the small jurisdiction where they have authority: the hour after vespers, the old stone bench, the willow-pin warm in his hand.
