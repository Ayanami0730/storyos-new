The courtyard smelled of cold stone and horse-heat and the kind of silence that sits before something is done. The dais they built for punishment stood plain against the sky, a beam and a ladder and the small practiced platform where decisions were made visible. Guards ringed it like an obedience; a crowd that had been household and feast gathered now as if to learn what order looked like when it took breath.

Elys pushed through the press with Marta at her elbow, breath sharp as a bell. She had not been told—no summons had been given to her—but the sight of Garr in the centre, pale against the paving and held by two men, drew the whole of her into a single, tearless moment.

In the scuffle a guard's gloved hand brushed Garr's palm and something small and bright clinked free; Marta's fingers were there before anyone noticed, and without a sound she caught the ivory and slipped it into Elys's sleeve as the girl surged forward.

His hands were empty; the willow-pin was gone from his palm. He saw her and his face did something like a question.

Lord Harrow stood above them on the steps, his shape cut by the long line of the house's will. He did not shout. He made a small motion of the hand and the manager of the steward's household read the sentence as if from a dry ledger.

"For conspiracy and the aiding of escape," the steward said, "the sentence of this household is death by hanging."

Garr's shoulders did not shrink from the words. He had the slow steadiness of a man who had known his edges for years. Elys moved as if drawn by a thread and produced the little ivory willow-pin from her sleeve. She crossed the space to him with a child's quickness and pressed it into his palm.

"Take it, Garr," she said, the words clean and quiet as they had been nights ago. He closed his fingers around the pin, and for a heartbeat they held each other's hands the way people hold a thing that's all there is to hold.

Rowan stood nearest the platform, the order in his hand like a brand. When Lord Harrow nodded, Rowan gave the signal with the same professional calm that had become part of his skin. He turned, and for a second his jaw trembled as if with something like the memory of a kindness he had once given.

"Orders are weights I carry, Lord; they do not make the carrying easy," he said, aloud though it was meant as a small excuse to himself.

The execution was swift. The rope took Garr with a single, terrible explanation; he swung once on the beam and then was still. A curdle of sound went through the watching people—a sound that was not cheering and not mourning, only the acknowledgment of a thing concluded.

Elys bent as if to touch his boots and then, as if a plan made itself, climbed the ladder with the speed of someone who would not be denied a conclusion. She pressed the willow-pin to his lips and kissed it, and then, without a word, she looped the rope over her own head.

Marta's hand went to her mouth and she cried out—one small, animal sound—and tried to take her. Hands reached; guards hesitated as if even they felt the wrongness of catching a lord's daughter. Elys's feet left the platform. The air closed around her as it had closed around Garr, and she went where he had gone.

They were dead. The steward closed his book and the hush spread like a cloth over the courtyard. Lord Harrow's face did not fall into triumph; it was the flat, exhausted line of a man who had paid a bill he thought would settle favor and found only more accounts.

When the crowd had been pushed back Marta stooped and found the little willow-pin where it had fallen and folded it into her palm as if it were something that might still be kept from being broken. She pressed it to her breast, a gesture that was not comfort but accusation.

Rowan stood with his head lowered. Later he would say what he had said here in smaller voice: that he had answered an order; that the weight would remain. For now his stillness said what a single line could not.

The household dispersed with slow, mechanical care. Aldric watched from the house's shadow, expression set. Lord Harrow went back into the hall as if to close a ledger. His victory was a silence that filled up rooms: practical, uncelebratory. The courtyard kept the shape of two bodies on the paving and the small, stubborn presence of a willow carved in ivory on the stone where Marta still held it.

Marta folded her hands over the pin and whispered to herself the line she had already spoken in a corridor and could not take back. "I kept their hour like a prayer; they have asked me to unmake that prayer." 

What had been private was now evidence; what had been love was now a lesson. The house returned to its tasks, but many of its faces had been rearranged by what they had seen.
