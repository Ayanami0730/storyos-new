### world-rules  <world/rules.yaml>

These govern what is TRUE. They do not say what anyone KNOWS.
A character knows a rule only where canon says so; otherwise they may be ignorant of it, wrong about it, or in the middle of working it out — and if the story is about someone discovering one of these, writing it into their thoughts early is how that story gets destroyed.

- Setting is historically grounded: World War II–era Hong Kong (Japanese occupation). No anachronistic technology or modern institutions may appear.
- Spirits are formed from and fed by unresolved memory, trauma, and communal silence; the more contradictory and suppressed the memory, the stronger the spirit.
- Ritual efficacy depends on correct materials, words, and community context; exorcisms can bind, release, or transform spirit-anchors but always carry a measurable personal cost to the ritualist or participants (loss of memory, physical injury, diminished sight/hearing, temporary blindness to emotion, etc.).
- Mercy Chan’s craft follows a coherent, internally consistent folk system (incantations, offerings, archival testimony). She cannot perform miracles like raising the dead or reversing wartime events.
- Spirits can manifest physically (poltergeist activity, sickness) and psychically (visions, emotional contagion). Physical laws hold except where explicitly suspended by ritual; nobody gains supernatural invulnerability.
- Community consent matters: large-scale rites require the residents’ participation; deceptive or coercive rituals produce backlash that strengthens the spirit.
- The revenant is not a single person but a composite wound; it responds when specific tokens or places are disturbed and is weakened by acts of truthful remembrance and repair rather than lies or suppression.
- Time is linear; memory manifests as overlayed scenes but not literal time travel—characters can witness the past as vision, not rejoin it.
- No contradictions with later committed scenes: all new claims must be recorded with provenance in the index when written.
- The story must balance supernatural horror with historical/social realism; the haunting’s resolution requires both ritual action and community moral reckoning.

The central courtyard had been hushed and steadied into a theater of memory. Low lamps guttered; torches were kept tight at the rims of the tiers. The inner ring held witnesses—two from each household whose pebbles had been placed in the alley—faces set to a duty that tasted like salt. The Elder sat near the battered scroll, packet folded, his hands folded as if in prayer. Mercy stood by the low altar, the locket and the folded cloth wrapped and placed at the center on a clean cloth; her kit lay open with its talismans face-down.

Mercy moved through the list she had practised in a thousand small diagnostics: powdered incense in the dented censer, offerings of rice and tea in small bowls, talismans kept folded until names were corroborated. She spoke the rules aloud so the sound would be a public contract: witnesses repeat placements, each memory is given with two witnesses, the Elder reads a fragment to set the scope, offerings are placed for what cannot be named. The sound of her voice—steady, thin—was a cord that braided the crowd together.

The Elder pried a page and read the fragment that had drawn them here: a smudged notation that repeated in different hands—"moved to side-yard," "placed, not named,"—and a margin where ink had been scraped away until the paper was raw. He did not pronounce it as accusation; he let the fragments sit like stones between people.

One by one the households came forward. The ritual was slow and careful: a witness spoke what they had seen—sensory phrases, gestures—placed a pebble at the altar, repeated the placement aloud and initialled Mercy's ledger. No speaker named a living person; the point was not to accuse but to make absence visible.

When it was the refugee's turn he stepped to the inner ring and spoke, small and clear, of the east step and the child's hand he had held. The witness who had stood with him repeated his words. Mercy touched the closed talisman to the smoke and watched the way the censer's breath rose around the photograph and the pebble they'd brought. The spirit tightened in response: a chorus of layered voices rose, a gust of cold swept through the courtyard, and for a heartbeat the sky above seemed to swell with the city's old panic.

Voices overlapped until the air itself sounded like accusation. A woman who had been there the night read aloud a fragment she had kept—how lights had been put out, how doors had been braced. A neighbor who had hidden a blanket in a stairwell placed his pebble and said, "We covered them," his voice small enough that the act of saying it felt like unbuttoning a wound. Each declared fragment was witnessed and recorded.

Then the spirit struck. It was not a single blow but a pressure that made the stones sing: a sound like falling boards, a dozen small thuds that rolled across the courtyard; a wash of images—sepia flashes of hands and faces—flicked at the edges of the witnesses' sight. Whispers crowded into sharper syllables that seemed to name absence itself. A lantern blew and the bow of the altar rocked where the offerings had been laid.

The community trembled. Some stepped back, mouths clamped; others pressed forward because the act of stepping forward was now its own defense. Mercy felt the old accounting in her body—the sharp, private ache that had been the currency of her craft. She had warned them: naming would cost. It would be paid in a thing that could be counted and in a thing that could not.

She had prepared for the cost in the only way she could: by choosing to trade what the revenant loved to take—an absence—from her own chest. She drew from the kit a small folded scrap she'd kept for herself: a soft cloth wrapped around a token no one else had touched, a private relic she had meant never to show. She did not name the memory aloud; to name it would have been to remake it in public. Instead she took the token, held it to her palm, and said, so the crowd could hear but not the world beyond, "I give what I keep for myself. I give the small thing I hold to remember. Take it and be counted." She laid the token on the altar and let the folded talisman rest on it.

Then she spoke the memory into the air the way a midwife speaks the name of a child into a new life, but Mercy spoke to undo—short, sensory lines that refused proper nouns: a warm cup left by a bedside, a shadow on a low wall, the small pocket where a child kept a shoe. As she spoke, the token loosened in her mind like a thread being cut.

The cost took immediate form. Mercy felt a private detail—one she had often used to steady herself when bargaining with the city—slip like a coin from a palm. It sank away not like a lost object but like a small door closed: she remembered saying the memory aloud, but when she reached for the picture that had held it, there was a thin, sharp blank that had not been there before. Her spine clenched; the ringing she had carried since the rooftop rose. Her voice came back thin and altered.

The act was recorded: Mercy folded the empty cloth into the altar and the Elder noted the sacrifice aloud in the ritual's ledger. The communal witness protocol was followed—two witnesses at each name, pebble placements, offerings set to the side—and the scroll's fragment read again to tie the names into the paper's pattern of erasure.

The spirit's reaction was a single, changing sound. For a long moment the chorus screamed and the air filled with small reenacted fragments; then something in the howl thinned, as if the absence the spirit had fed upon had been matched by a voluntary absence. The fury did not vanish like a candle; it reorganised. Where there had been a seething chorus there was now a quieter presence: layered voices no longer seeking to erase but to be held—an uneasy mourner who would not feed on silence but would stay, named and tethered.

The courtyard exhaled. Some neighbors wept openly; others stood with faces like carved stone. A few shook their heads as if disbelieving the bargain they'd watched. The Elder closed the scroll and, with a voice that trembled, said, "It has changed. It is not gone, but it is held. We will keep witnesses and offerings. We will work to name what was left un-named."

Mercy felt the loss as a new hollow where a small private certainty had been. It would change how she worked—an instrument slightly altered. She had paid with a memory that had once been hers to steady a bargain for the many. The Young Refugee came to her side and put his hand over hers on the altar—solid, warm—and Mercy felt for the first time the trade's meaning: a memory lost was matched to a community's chance at not feeding the wound.

They left the courtyard exhausted. The spirit no longer roared but watched like someone seated in sorrow; its immediate threat had lessened, though the shape of loss remained. The Elder set the date for follow-up witnessings and offerings. Mercy closed her kit with hands that trembled not just from strain but because something she had relied on was no longer there. The cost had been paid, the ritual recorded, and the community would now begin the longer work of holding what the dead had been given back—a hard task with brittle hope at its center.

They returned to the east step at dusk with the scroll wrapped in the Elder's cloth and the pebble still set where Mercy had left it. The bombed yard held the hush of a place made private by absence; low walls muttered with the city around them. A small ring of witnesses—Neighbor Lei, the kitchen woman, and two others who had placed pebbles—stood with Mercy and the Elder near the marked tile. The air smelled of cold stone and the faint iron the locket had worn into it.

The Elder unwrapped the battered story scroll with hands that moved like ritual themselves. He pried a single page, careful to make no show of unrolling what the Circle had kept guarded. "Listen," he said. "I will read fragments; they are not full lines. They are what is left." He read, not in judgment but in the flatness that made clay take form:

"—moved to side-yard—"

He tapped the smudged margin. "Here: a note in a different hand—'placed, not named'."

Another paper peeled at him and his voice lowered. "—page torn—name rubbed—'—wan'—then: 'moved to side-yard'—" He let the sound of that double note sit in the yard like a small stone. The scroll showed half-formed sequences: names begun and then scratched, notes about 'moved' and 'placed' repeated in different inks, marginal hand that read like hurried accounting.

Mercy held the family photograph like a small compass against the tile and watched the way the Elder's reading made the yard shift. The fragments did not say who had made the choice; they showed the fact of choice itself: movement recorded and then erased, bodies accounted for only in shorthand, ledger columns stopped mid-line. The pattern was not a single confession but a social practice—leaving, moving, burying in haste—that the paper had absorbed.

A neighbor—an older man who had stayed on the margin of witness lists—finally spoke, his voice a rope of rough words. He would not give his name, only a household marker and a hand lifted like a shield against shame. "That night," he said, "we heard them calling from the stairwell. We... we knew if we opened our door the patrols might—" He stopped, throat thick. He looked at the pebble and then at the tile. "We put out lights. We hid what we could. We told our children to sleep. We did not... we did not carry them out." The sentence fell away into the dirt like a mouthful of ash.

The testimony was not theatrical; it was small and ragged and full of detail that could be checked: the timing, the way lights went out, the act of hiding bodies under blankets in corners. No one spoke of commanders or orders; the neighbor spoke of fear and calculation, of choosing a household's survival when rescue meant risking everything. Where the scroll had shown blanks, the witness's voice gave a shape: a practice of protection that also left others unrescued to be buried in silence.

Mercy listened with the weight of the map they'd made in her pockets. She felt the pebble at the east step like a heart under her hand; the photograph hummed against her ribs. The spirit—ever the sum of these silences—stirred. Voices layered into a single, thin howl that ran up from the stairwell and shook the tiles; a gust of cold moved through the yard and a laundry line snapped taut as if struck. The Elder's eyes narrowed.

"This is the pattern the scroll and the witnesses point to," he said, slow and blunt. "Names written and erased. Burials noted and then made anonymous. The page shows a practice—people moved, pages stop—how the living saved the living by closing their mouths."

Mercy's mediating instincts narrowed into a plan she had not thought to say aloud before. A placation that smoothed memory had failed; what the spirit wanted now was to be held to account by truth that did not accuse the living without care. "We will offer a different thing," she said. "We will name what we can with witnesses. The Elder reads the fragment; a witness says what they saw; we place a pebble; we offer rice and tea; an offering is made for what we cannot say aloud. We do not point fingers beyond what witnesses swear. We will hold names with offerings so the dead have witnesses, and the living are held for what follows." Her voice shook a little where the ritual strain touched her, but the words were precise.

The Elder folded the scroll back a pace and met Mercy's eyes. "Naming costs," he reminded them. "The ritualist pays and the living pay. But leaving a wound un-said lets it feed. If this is to be binding, we take witnesses, we place offerings, and we accept the danger. We do not do this lightly."

Mercy laid out the small procedure—powdered incense in the dented censer, closed talismans at the ready, small bowls of rice and tea as offerings, two witnesses for each memory, pebble placement repeated aloud, and the scroll's fragment read first to set context. She warned the community what the world rules had taught her: naming without witnesses could draw attention from patrols; naming could cost memory, and she might pay her own memory in the act of bearing witness. It was the hardest bargain she could offer.

There was a hush like the yard holding its breath. The spirit's howl thinned into a chorus and then a single sound that felt like attention. Mercy felt the tightness at her ribs—the residual strain from earlier tests—and the thin ringing behind her ears climb a note. She set her hand lightly on the scroll's cloth and said, "If you will do this—if each household will send two witnesses and place small offerings—then we name in sequence. We keep it public, recorded, and witnessed. We will not shout; we will hold."

Neighbor Lei swallowed and spoke before others could. "I will stand. I will bear witness to what I remember. I will place a pebble and repeat what I saw." His voice made a small ripple; others shifted and two more hands rose. The witness list began, not yet full, but no longer empty.

The spirit made a sound that could have been assent or fury—a high, brief note that left the yard colder. Mercy tightened her grip on the kit's strap and looked at the faces gathered: some resolute, some terrified, some already counting the cost. They would name, and they would bear the cost together. The Elder rested his hand on the scroll like a benediction and Mercy felt the work of the next days arrive like a new burden.

They set the date: at first light, with offerings, witnesses, the scroll read, and the pebble-marked east step presented as place to be named. Mercy closed her kit and tucked the photograph back into its pocket; the pebble would be the breadcrumb and the scroll would be the ledger that made absence a thing to be witnessed. The yard's wail sank into the day with a new, sharper focus—the beginning of a ritual that would ask the living to hold what they had left un-held.
