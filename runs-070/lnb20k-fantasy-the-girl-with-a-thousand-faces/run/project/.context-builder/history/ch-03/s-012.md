### world-rules  <world/rules.yaml>

These govern what is TRUE. They do not say what anyone KNOWS.
A character knows a rule only where canon says so; otherwise they may be ignorant of it, wrong about it, or in the middle of working it out — and if the story is about someone discovering one of these, writing it into their thoughts early is how that story gets destroyed.

- Setting is historically grounded: World War II–era Hong Kong (Japanese occupation). No anachronistic technology or modern institutions may appear.
- Spirits are formed from and fed by unresolved memory, trauma, and communal silence; the more contradictory and suppressed the memory, the stronger the spirit.
- Ritual efficacy depends on correct materials, words, and community context; exorcisms can bind, release, or transform spirit-anchors but always carry a measurable personal cost to the ritualist or participants (loss of memory, physical injury, diminished sight/hearing, temporary blindness to emotion, etc.).
- Mercy Chan’s craft follows a coherent, internally consistent folk system (incantations, offerings, archival testimony). She cannot perform miracles like raising the dead or reversing wartime events.
- Spirits can manifest physically (poltergeist activity, sickness) and psychically (visions, emotional contagion). Physical laws hold except where explicitly suspended by ritual; nobody gains supernatural invulnerability.
- Community consent matters: large-scale rites require the residents’ participation; deceptive or coercive rituals produce backlash that strengthens the spirit.
- The revenant is not a single person but a composite wound; it responds when specific tokens or places are disturbed and is weakened by acts of truthful remembrance and repair rather than lies or suppression.
- Time is linear; memory manifests as overlayed scenes but not literal time travel—characters can witness the past as vision, not rejoin it.
- No contradictions with later committed scenes: all new claims must be recorded with provenance in the index when written.
- The story must balance supernatural horror with historical/social realism; the haunting’s resolution requires both ritual action and community moral reckoning.

They climbed into the stairwell like people stepping into a wound. The treads had broken away to a void; rubble lay in a tumbled skein at the base, and Mercy picked her way down with the same care she used in the library or the shrine—small, tested steps, a hand out to find something that would not betray her. The refugee followed, his breath a small, uneven sound, and two neighbors stayed at the lip above as witnesses, their faces lit in the shallow dusk.

Mercy worked with hands that knew slow patience. She cleared loose stones by hand, set them aside in neat piles, brushed the grit away with an old cloth, and used a small wooden trowel to tease at a patch where the earth seemed pressed flat. The stairwell held shadow in its hollows but the spot he had shown her—just behind a collapsed tread—gave like soft paper. For a moment she thought it might be a pocket of cloth; she slipped her fingers under the edge and drew up to light a small, stiff rectangle.

It was smaller than she expected: a dented oval, dark with age, its hinge stubborn and its metal pitted where time had worked a skin of verdigris. Mercy turned it over in the light of a neighbor's lamp; the clasp complained like a throat when she eased it. Inside, folded tight and intimate, lay a scrap of paper, a scrap of cloth no larger than a coin, a tiny curl of hair the color of soot, and the faint suggestion of a studio stamp pressed into a torn photograph corner. The items were layered, pressed one into another as if different hands had tucked them there at different times.

The refugee made a small, involuntary sound—half grief, half relief—and touched the locket's rim with a fingertip as if to prove it was real. Mercy did not let him handle the pieces until she had secured them. She spoke in the low voice she used when asking someone to trust a painful thing. "We handle this careful. We mark it. Witnesses watch." She lifted a square of clean cloth from her kit and laid the locket on it, then folded the cloth over like a small shroud and placed it in an inner envelope of her case.

The bloodstained cloth, when she brushed aside a slab of compacted earth a pace away, lay half-hidden where a shallow bowl of soil had been tamped flat. It was the ragged linen they'd known—a metallic tang rose even in the stairwell air; the edges were frayed and stiff, the stain a browned map of something that had once been red. Mercy let the refugee touch the cloth for the smallest moment, then wrapped it in another clean cloth and tucked it beside the locket.

The moment the objects left their shallow grave, the stairwell spoke. Voices sharpened into syllables that were not any single language; the wail condensed into fragments and repeated a staccato that made the stones feel like they trembled. A gust of cold went through the narrow shaft and rattled down the corridor; dust fell in a thin rain. Mercy felt the pressure like a palm across her sternum and, at the same instant, the familiar sting behind her left eye and the tightening at the base of her throat—her rite's toll. She pressed a thumb there as if to hold herself together.

The neighbors above gave a small unified sound—an intake, a recognition—and one of them, the woman who kept the communal kitchen's keys, crossed herself in the small, private way the community had learned to do when names would not suffice. The refugee folded in on himself, a man shrinking to the size that grief required, and then lifted his face toward Mercy with a strange, raw look of someone both relieved and terrified that the thing had been found.

Mercy did not try to conjure explanation from the locket's scraps. The paper had slashes of different hands: a neat, cramped scrawl on one folded edge, a rougher, hurried ink on another. The hair curl was clipped small, tied with a rusted pin; the cloth matched the texture of the refugee's own, though Mercy did not assert identity—she only noted it. The effect was clear enough: the object was not a single voice but a compression where several had been pressed together. That compression made the spirit into a compound anger.

She checked the pebble mark and made a careful notation in her notebook: "Found small oval locket under collapsed tread; layered contents; cloth beside shallow pit; pebble marker placed. Witnesses: Lei, Kitchen woman." She initialled the line and closed the book, her fingers lingering on the page as if to leave an imprint of persistence.

But she did not leave. There were other pockets beneath the broken treads and the compacted earth shifted in places where small objects might have settled. Mercy set the wrapped locket and the folded cloth beside her on a clean cloth, then resumed the slow task: she brushed at seams of soil, pried at a flake of brick, and found a ragged wooden slat that had once marked a makeshift grave. She placed a pebble at each disturbed spot, drew small arrows on her notebook map, and asked the two witnesses to repeat the placements aloud so the marks were not only chalk but testimony.

Each new scrape tightened the stairwell's voice. A laundry line snapped taut across the yard; a tin can rolled as if coaxed over a stone; the wail, threaded now with sharper syllables, cut into single words that sounded like accusation without an address. Mercy's vision narrowed at the edges and the ringing in her ears climbed a notch; she steadied herself against a fragment of wall and spoke to the refugee as one human to another. "We finish here, then I will take these to the library at first light. We ask the Elder to look with witnesses. For now, we make this place speak with marks and witnesses." Her voice trembled where the ritual toll tugged at her, but the instruction held.

He nodded so hard his chin trembled. The relief that had crossed his face was not a healed thing; it was the small easing of a pressure when the buried has been named as buried. Mercy wrapped the locket and cloth again and placed them in separate inner envelopes in her kit; she photographed nothing but clipped a final note and sealed the map with a pebble and two witnesses' initials.

As they climbed back out of the stairwell, the neighborhood watched with a tight, careful attention. The discovery had given the spirit a new face to name and the community a new burden to carry. Mercy felt the ritual cost in the prick behind her eye and the tightening that pulled at her voice; still, the act of removal and the on-site mapping felt necessary. They would consult the Elder and the ruined library next day, but tonight the yard had to be made to hold its own memory.

The orderly's eyes closed for the smallest beat. "We buried who we could. Not always with tags. Not always with people to say their names. Sometimes—people moved them," he said, and then let the pause stand. "Sometimes they were buried where no ledger would look."

Mercy could feel how the ward's work had been to hold bodies while the city tried to survive. The ledger’s blanked names and missing pages were not only administrative failings; they were a social practice of survival—erasure as protection. That very erasure, she understood with a quiet dread, made the kind of wound that fed her enemy. A ledger that stopped was a memory that had been allowed to die in silence, and where memory dies a revenant grows.

She did not perform a rite in the ward; this was a place for testimony and careful reading, not drama. Instead she took out a small notebook and copied the ledger's visible fragments—a transcription of a smudged line, the stub of a burial slip, a note that a page had been torn. Her hand moved in the practiced slowness of someone preserving paper rather than summoning spirits. The orderly watched, not unguarded but trusting the motion of someone who read as she worked.

"If you ask people…" the orderly said as she wrote, his voice low and practical. "Don't ask them to remember in accused ways. Ask them to tell what they saw. Offer tea. Offer a cigarette. Don't tell them what you want. They protect themselves with silence." His counsel was the kind someone who had seen how questions break people would give: a protocol of care.

Mercy left with the taste of iron at the back of her throat and a narrow transcribed strip of ledger pages between her fingers. The ward's dim light seemed to hold the shape of things left unsaid, and the knowledge that erasure had been practiced for survival sat on her shoulders like a weight. She had found suggestive evidence—smudged entries, a torn page, burial slips half-hidden—but not a verdict. The ledger could be read two ways: as protection and as wound. Either way, it fed the revenant.

Outside, the Walled City’s alleys smelled of frying oil and smoke again. Mercy pulled the ledger fragment from her palm and read the smudged line by lamplight. Behind her, through a cracked door, the orderly went back to his slow work, and the ward returned to its hush. Mercy walked toward the shrine with the strip of transcription folded in her palm and the promise to gather witnesses: names would be asked, but only with care. The spirit would be faced not by accusation but by a recorded, witnessed memory.
