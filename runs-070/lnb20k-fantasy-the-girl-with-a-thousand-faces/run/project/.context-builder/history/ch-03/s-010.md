### world-rules  <world/rules.yaml>

These govern what is TRUE. They do not say what anyone KNOWS.
A character knows a rule only where canon says so; otherwise they may be ignorant of it, wrong about it, or in the middle of working it out — and if the story is about someone discovering one of these, writing it into their thoughts early is how that story gets destroyed.

- Setting is historically grounded: World War II–era Hong Kong (Japanese occupation). No anachronistic technology or modern institutions may appear.
- Spirits are formed from and fed by unresolved memory, trauma, and communal silence; the more contradictory and suppressed the memory, the stronger the spirit.
- Ritual efficacy depends on correct materials, words, and community context; exorcisms can bind, release, or transform spirit-anchors but always carry a measurable personal cost to the ritualist or participants (loss of memory, physical injury, diminished sight/hearing, temporary blindness to emotion, etc.).
- Mercy Chan’s craft follows a coherent, internally consistent folk system (incantations, offerings, archival testimony). She cannot perform miracles like raising the dead or reversing wartime events.
- Spirits can manifest physically (poltergeist activity, sickness) and psychically (visions, emotional contagion). Physical laws hold except where explicitly suspended by ritual; nobody gains supernatural invulnerability.
- Community consent matters: large-scale rites require the residents’ participation; deceptive or coercive rituals produce backlash that strengthens the spirit.
- The revenant is not a single person but a composite wound; it responds when specific tokens or places are disturbed and is weakened by acts of truthful remembrance and repair rather than lies or suppression.
- Time is linear; memory manifests as overlayed scenes but not literal time travel—characters can witness the past as vision, not rejoin it.
- No contradictions with later committed scenes: all new claims must be recorded with provenance in the index when written.
- The story must balance supernatural horror with historical/social realism; the haunting’s resolution requires both ritual action and community moral reckoning.

The ward smelled of attempt and failure: boiled rice turned sour, the sharp antiseptic edge of diluted iodine, and the heavy undertone of damp linen folded too long. Oil lamps fought the dim; a single barred window offered a strip of false daylight that made dust hang like confession. Mercy moved between curtained bays where cots had been plucked hastily and men’s blankets lay folded into neat, absent shapes. Everything here looked as if it had been pressed and hurried—sheets folded; instruments stacked; a basin left to dry with a rag thrown over it.

At the central table, a battered ledger sat under a clumsy weight of ration tins. Its cover was loose and the pages inside had the fatigue of being opened and closed in the dark. Mercy reached for it with the careful respect she gave objects that had been made to hold names. The ink had run in places and columns blurred into one another; some entries ended mid-line where a pen had been lifted in a hurry. One page had been torn away cleanly and the glue along the spine showed the ragged gap.

The hospital orderly stood nearby, shoulders bent like someone who had carried weights too long. His hands were callused and his voice came out like a thing that had been kept behind teeth. "We wrote what we could," he said, as if speaking the ledger’s title could unburden it. "Some nights there were too many. We buried in the side yard. Quiet. Quick. Not like a funeral—just… puttings away."

He spoke slowly, the sort of man who measured even rumor. Mercy did not press for dates. The orderly's eyes kept drifting to the barred window as if expecting patrols to glance up. "The book stops," he said, thumbing to a place where ink had been smudged into a gray. "Pages half full. Names gone blank. Sometimes a pen ran out. Sometimes a hand ran away with a page. I don't… I don't have the courage to scrape each name back out." He wrapped his fingers around a small tin and offered it to Mercy the way someone offers a bandage without the wound.

Mercy let the ledger speak with her hands. She flattened a page and traced with a fingertip where ink had been rubbed until letters had become ghosts. Near the torn spine she found a column with a smudge that might once have been a date and a list of names that ended in a blot. Beside it a narrow column of burial slips—thin rough paper—had been tucked in and then half-removed. She did not claim the slips as proof. They were suggestive: evidence of hurried accounting, not an inventory.

"You buried them?" she asked, the question small enough not to demand a narrative.

The orderly's eyes closed for the smallest beat. "We buried who we could. Not always with tags. Not always with people to say their names. Sometimes—people moved them," he said, and then let the pause stand. "Sometimes they were buried where no ledger would look."

Mercy could feel how the ward's work had been to hold bodies while the city tried to survive. The ledger’s blanked names and missing pages were not only administrative failings; they were a social practice of survival—erasure as protection. That very erasure, she understood with a quiet dread, made the kind of wound that fed her enemy. A ledger that stopped was a memory that had been allowed to die in silence, and where memory dies a revenant grows.

She did not perform a rite in the ward; this was a place for testimony and careful reading, not drama. Instead she took out a small notebook and copied the ledger's visible fragments—a transcription of a smudged line, the stub of a burial slip, a note that a page had been torn. Her hand moved in the practiced slowness of someone preserving paper rather than summoning spirits. The orderly watched, not unguarded but trusting the motion of someone who read as she worked.

"If you ask people…" the orderly said as she wrote, his voice low and practical. "Don't ask them to remember in accused ways. Ask them to tell what they saw. Offer tea. Offer a cigarette. Don't tell them what you want. They protect themselves with silence." His counsel was the kind someone who had seen how questions break people would give: a protocol of care.

Mercy left with the taste of iron at the back of her throat and a narrow transcribed strip of ledger pages between her fingers. The ward's dim light seemed to hold the shape of things left unsaid, and the knowledge that erasure had been practiced for survival sat on her shoulders like a weight. She had found suggestive evidence—smudged entries, a torn page, burial slips half-hidden—but not a verdict. The ledger could be read two ways: as protection and as wound. Either way, it fed the revenant.

Outside, the Walled City’s alleys smelled of frying oil and smoke again. Mercy pulled the ledger fragment from her palm and read the smudged line by lamplight. Behind her, through a cracked door, the orderly went back to his slow work, and the ward returned to its hush. Mercy walked toward the shrine with the strip of transcription folded in her palm and the promise to gather witnesses: names would be asked, but only with care. The spirit would be faced not by accusation but by a recorded, witnessed memory.

They chose a low parapet that looked over the central courtyard and laid the mat there, paling the rooftop's crooked tile with a clean patch of cloth. A few potted plants scratched at the dark; laundry lines sighed between poles like nets. The city below breathed in muffled rhythms—footsteps, a distant radio cough—small sounds that made the rooftop feel unexpectedly exposed. Torches had been left deliberately low on neighboring roofs; someone had obeyed the Elders' counsel and kept light and noise tight.

Mercy set her woven case open on the mat and arranged what she needed without show. The dented censer went to the center like a heart; talismans were fanned in a loose ring, chalk drawn in faint, protective half-circles, and the little knife lay spine-up on the mat. She worked with the practiced economy of someone who knew how much light one could afford.

The Elder sat opposite her and watched the arrangement as if it were a small, fragile map. "We keep the witness list short," he said. "No drums, no open fires. If it answers, we stop. If it worsens, we pull back. We name to hold, not to unloose anger." His voice was the slow instrument everyone had learnt to listen to—measured, not command.

Mercy nodded and lit a pinch of powdered incense. The smoke rose thin and neat, and for a moment the rooftop was only lamp-glow and the pale column of scent. She murmured the low naming she used for probing, the kind that asked the air to speak rather than to obey. The motion was small: a tracing of the knife through smoke, a talisman pinched and held to the lip of the censer, a chalk breath painted into an incomplete boundary.

At first the rite gave the answers Mercy expected: a tremor in the air, a hint of voices folding at the city's edges. Then the sound thickened, and something in that thickening felt wrong. The voices braided, as before, but they began to pull at one another until they took form—breathy outlines that the corner of the eye mistook for hands or small heads. Shadows where there should have been none unhooked themselves from the parapet and leaned in like curious children.

A scent hit her—a scrap of damp cloth, boiled rice—and for a half-second a doorway slid under her vision: a threshold she could not name, a domestic shape that pressed like a palm against memory. It was not evidence; it was a sensation that fitted too closely to what a person keeps without naming. Mercy blinked and the image thinned; the provenance of the feeling was a gap, and she did not try to fill it. She only felt the familiar trade: a truth bought with a small theft from her own past.

The Elder's mouth tightened. "It makes itself into what people lack," he said quietly. "It will shape itself to the wound you bring. Be ready to let go."

They tried to hold the rite to measure—Mercy counted the breaths, the slips, the light of the lamp. But the voices became insistently physical. A breath caressed the nape of her neck like a child's fingers; pressure settled across her sternum as if someone were leaning into her ribs. At the same moment she felt the familiar toll that had come with other tests: a sharp prick behind her left eye and a tightening at the base of her throat. It pulled her composure thin; her voice came out higher and thinner than she intended, as if the ritual had borrowed steadiness from her.

The Elders' witness list tensed into motion. Neighbors watched from windows and doorways, children peered round shutters, and many stayed in place to see what would happen—exactly the pattern Mercy had been warned to preserve. A few gestures betrayed the night's danger: a shutter snapped closed with a hard click; a neighbor on a nearby roof retreated half a step and then drew back behind a low wall. The room's faces had not emptied, but the mood had shifted; watchers' attention tightened into an anxious thread.

On the mat, a talisman she had not set down fluttered as if fingers had taken it. The censer clattered once, then quieted. Mercy's knees trembled; the ache behind her eye and the tightening in her throat throbbed with a raw edge. The Elder reached out and pressed his palm lightly on her shoulder—a small human anchor in a night of spectral hands.

"We stop," he said. "Now. The cost is more than a bruise tonight." He helped her fold the talismans, his fingers steady and slow. "You paid—" he began, and then left the sentence unfinished, the unsaid accounting hanging between them.

Mercy wrapped the kit closed with hands that felt frayed at the edges. Her hearing carried the high ringing that had followed earlier probes; she tasted iron at the back of her throat and felt cautious in a way that was not merely professional. The rooftop that had felt private and shielded only minutes before now seemed thinly veiled, its privacy ripped back by the way the voices had wanted to be seen. Neighbors' footsteps moved below; a hush moved through the courtyard like a held breath.

She had not lost everything—memory was not a clean ledger—but the rite had taken a toll that left her less sure. The failure cost Mercy more than a tally; it cost communal confidence in the small way a neighbor's snapped shutter costs trust. When she and the Elder descended, some faces in the stair went taut with worry; a woman who had left the roof did not return to watch another rite, retreating instead behind a closed door.

Outside, the city's ordinary noises resumed but with a thinner tone, as if everyone had learned to listen for the wrong thing. Mercy felt her certainty hollow a little; a ritual that used to mean a measured answer had become an event that could unmake parts of herself and leave the community less sure. The Elder's hand rested for a moment on the closed kit, a touch that acknowledged payment and danger.

Down in the dim courtyard, small groups whispered. Some neighbors said Mercy had overreached; others blamed the spirit's cunning. Mercy tightened her fingers on the kit's strap until the leather bit into her palm and felt, like an ember, the knowledge that the spirit had shifted a degree closer to a personal claim. It now answered to tokens and to the contour of Mercy's own unspoken memories. The rooftop had been a test; it had failed them all, and the price was paid in faith and a little of Mercy's certainty.
