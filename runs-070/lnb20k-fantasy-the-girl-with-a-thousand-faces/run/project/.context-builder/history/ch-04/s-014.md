### world-rules  <world/rules.yaml>

These govern what is TRUE. They do not say what anyone KNOWS.
A character knows a rule only where canon says so; otherwise they may be ignorant of it, wrong about it, or in the middle of working it out — and if the story is about someone discovering one of these, writing it into their thoughts early is how that story gets destroyed.

- Setting is historically grounded: World War II–era Hong Kong (Japanese occupation). No anachronistic technology or modern institutions may appear.
- Spirits are formed from and fed by unresolved memory, trauma, and communal silence; the more contradictory and suppressed the memory, the stronger the spirit.
- Ritual efficacy depends on correct materials, words, and community context; exorcisms can bind, release, or transform spirit-anchors but always carry a measurable personal cost to the ritualist or participants (loss of memory, physical injury, diminished sight/hearing, temporary blindness to emotion, etc.).
- Mercy Chan’s craft follows a coherent, internally consistent folk system (incantations, offerings, archival testimony). She cannot perform miracles like raising the dead or reversing wartime events.
- Spirits can manifest physically (poltergeist activity, sickness) and psychically (visions, emotional contagion). Physical laws hold except where explicitly suspended by ritual; nobody gains supernatural invulnerability.
- Community consent matters: large-scale rites require the residents’ participation; deceptive or coercive rituals produce backlash that strengthens the spirit.
- The revenant is not a single person but a composite wound; it responds when specific tokens or places are disturbed and is weakened by acts of truthful remembrance and repair rather than lies or suppression.
- Time is linear; memory manifests as overlayed scenes but not literal time travel—characters can witness the past as vision, not rejoin it.
- No contradictions with later committed scenes: all new claims must be recorded with provenance in the index when written.
- The story must balance supernatural horror with historical/social realism; the haunting’s resolution requires both ritual action and community moral reckoning.

The Walled City folded around them like a map being creased and uncreased. Mercy moved through the alleys with the practiced economy of someone who had learned which ledges would hold and which tiles would give; the refugee followed, fingers tightened on the fabric of his sleeve. The family photograph sat against Mercy's ribs inside her kit, a small, paler weight she could feel like a pulse.

At first the city was itself—humidity, frying oil, the distant cough of a radio—but as they crossed the narrow ways the air began to thicken into other times. Mercy saw it happen in slices: a tarpaulin became a sepia awning; a hawker's call overlapped with a sharper shouted order; a child's laugh folded into a thin, repeated cry. The walls flickered with overlays—market stalls that had once been, a stair that had borne different feet. It was the revenant's theatre: fragments of past and present pressed together.

The refugee stopped at a bend where Mercy had chalked once before. He looked at the tile and then at her as if asking permission to step into the film. "I can feel it thicker here," he said. "It presses like a hand—here—" He put his palm to the worn step where the alley tilted toward the yard, and closed his eyes.

Mercy crouched beside him. The alley smelled sudden and sharp: boiled rice beneath smoke, the tinny tang of old blood. She did not prod him with questions; she had learned that questions sometimes shuttered what wanted to be said. Instead she asked the practical, habitual things that turned gestures into maps. "Show me with your foot," she said. "Where did you stand? Which way did the people move?"

He moved as if moving gave him courage. First one step, then another, tracing a corridor no longer there. He planted his foot on a tile and pointed with the smallest, most certain motion: toward the ruined stairwell's east step, the same edge Mercy had studied with pebbles. "They came from the stair—no, they pushed toward the door here," he said, voice low and steady. "And someone dropped something—a crate, maybe—but it landed here, by this tile. The child—someone's hand—reached back for it."

Mercy set a scrap of chalk on the tile and drew a thin circle where his finger rested. She placed a pebble at the center as a marker, then reached into her kit and, with the practiced economy of her rites, pinched a folded talisman and laid it against the pebble as a boundary—kept closed, not opened. She set the photograph flat beside the pebble as a small compass. Then she asked the neighbor who had accompanied them to speak what he saw and to repeat the placement aloud, so the mark was not Mercy's private note but a witnessed fact.

The witness, Neighbor Lei, stepped forward and echoed the gesture, placing his own pebble on top of Mercy's and saying, "East step—crate—child's hand." His voice added a public weight; the act of placing the second pebble made the memory something the community could point to.

The shrine smelled of boiled rice and old incense; the low ceiling made voices close and sharp. Neighbors filled the narrow space, two witnesses from each household as the Circle had advised; bodies leaned against walls and knees found stools or the cool floor. Neighbor Lei stood near the front with his hands on his hips, the kind of posture that turned impatience into command. The kitchen woman sat near him, fingers worried in her apron. The refugee sat close to Mercy, eyes raw and watchful.

The Elder spoke first, the kind of small, ground rule that made the room hold still. "We speak only what we saw. We do not accuse the living for what the dead cannot answer. Each speaker will be given a pebble. Place it where you would place a memory. We repeat each placement aloud and we witness." His voice was a low metronome; people shifted, somewhere between obedience and the hunger to say more.

Mercy lit a pinch of powdered incense in the dented burner and nodded to the Elder. "We will offer a placation," she said. "We will invite testimony and ask that any naming be given with witnesses. No open talismans until the end."

At first the placation moved like water: a neighbor spoke of seeing bodies carried in the night and buried swiftly; another spoke of a handful of people who took what they could and left the rest; a third insisted—quiet, concrete—that they had hidden people to save them and would do it again. The stories slid and overlapped until the air itself felt torn.

Then the fighting began. When one man described a night of hurried removal as an act of mercy, another spat back that it had been cowardice and abandonment. A talisman, face down, flipped once and skittered toward the man who had just spoken of betrayals as if a hand had nudged it. The censer's smoke thickened and a scent of iron—like the cloth—pulled through the room.

If you confess, speak and allow us to hold it without judgement."

Her words did not silence the room; they redirected it enough that the Elder stepped in with the sort of phrasing that kept people from stepping too far. "Memory is a brittle thing," he said. But habit and fear were stronger than ritual rules. A household that had earlier demanded immediate action began to shout about cowardice; another, which had been quietly protective, recoiled and accused the first of collaboration. "Stop," she said, and the word moved like a stone in a pond—small and necessary. "We will not smooth this. Smoothing is forgetting. This thing wants us to forget to keep feeding. We will change the aim. We will witness and name—but we will do so with witnesses, offerings, and careful accounting. We will not shout. We will record what each witness says, we will place marks, and we will hold names with offerings so the spirit cannot claim them as absence."

We will draw attention. We can't risk names when patrols are watching." His words pulled a murmur of agreement from a half of the room.

The Circle split in a quiet way that felt louder than the shouting had: half nodded toward Mercy's plan—witnessed naming, small offerings, public accounting; the other half shook their heads, fearful of reprisal and the exposure that names might bring. The Elder folded his packet and looked at Mercy as if weighing what the community could bear.

"Names cost," he said softly. "But the city is full of unpaid costs. Bearing them is a duty, and a danger. If we do this, we do it with witnesses and a plan to hold the living through what naming releases." He did not order the room; he left the choice in their hands.

Mercy felt the strain in her chest and the tremor at her speech. She had been wrong to think a placation would close the wound. Now she had to persuade people to accept a different risk: naming as repair. She set the dented censer down and looked at the faces she had to either calm or convince. The spirit made a small, sharp sound like many doors closing all at once and the shrine's light seemed to shiver.

The meeting broke with no clear vote. A witness list was begun, names pencilled in Mercy's notebook; some volunteered to stand and bear testimony at a later, witnessed naming; others left quietly with shoulders drawn in. The community had not yet chosen the path, and the spirit had fed. Mercy folded her talismans back into her kit, feeling the strain in her body, and the Elder's hand rested on her shoulder—an acknowledgement of the road they'd taken and the danger ahead.
