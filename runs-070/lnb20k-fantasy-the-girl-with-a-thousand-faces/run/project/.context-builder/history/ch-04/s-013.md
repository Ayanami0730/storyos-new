### world-rules  <world/rules.yaml>

These govern what is TRUE. They do not say what anyone KNOWS.
A character knows a rule only where canon says so; otherwise they may be ignorant of it, wrong about it, or in the middle of working it out — and if the story is about someone discovering one of these, writing it into their thoughts early is how that story gets destroyed.

- Setting is historically grounded: World War II–era Hong Kong (Japanese occupation). No anachronistic technology or modern institutions may appear.
- Spirits are formed from and fed by unresolved memory, trauma, and communal silence; the more contradictory and suppressed the memory, the stronger the spirit.
- Ritual efficacy depends on correct materials, words, and community context; exorcisms can bind, release, or transform spirit-anchors but always carry a measurable personal cost to the ritualist or participants (loss of memory, physical injury, diminished sight/hearing, temporary blindness to emotion, etc.).
- Mercy Chan’s craft follows a coherent, internally consistent folk system (incantations, offerings, archival testimony). She cannot perform miracles like raising the dead or reversing wartime events.
- Spirits can manifest physically (poltergeist activity, sickness) and psychically (visions, emotional contagion). Physical laws hold except where explicitly suspended by ritual; nobody gains supernatural invulnerability.
- Community consent matters: large-scale rites require the residents’ participation; deceptive or coercive rituals produce backlash that strengthens the spirit.
- The revenant is not a single person but a composite wound; it responds when specific tokens or places are disturbed and is weakened by acts of truthful remembrance and repair rather than lies or suppression.
- Time is linear; memory manifests as overlayed scenes but not literal time travel—characters can witness the past as vision, not rejoin it.
- No contradictions with later committed scenes: all new claims must be recorded with provenance in the index when written.
- The story must balance supernatural horror with historical/social realism; the haunting’s resolution requires both ritual action and community moral reckoning.

They set the altar small and plain so it could not be mistaken for spectacle: the dented censer in the center, a ring of folded talismans around the smoke, a low lamp to one side. The shrine smelled of boiled rice and old incense; the low ceiling made voices close and sharp. Mercy arranged the talismans face-down in a careful ring and placed her kit open beside the mat, the locket and the folded cloth tucked deeper in the pockets where they would not be waved like trophies. The Elder took his place near the scroll, packet folded and shut.

Neighbors filled the narrow space, two witnesses from each household as the Circle had advised; bodies leaned against walls and knees found stools or the cool floor. Neighbor Lei stood near the front with his hands on his hips, the kind of posture that turned impatience into command. The kitchen woman sat near him, fingers worried in her apron. The refugee sat close to Mercy, eyes raw and watchful.

The Elder spoke first, the kind of small, ground rule that made the room hold still. "We speak only what we saw. We do not accuse the living for what the dead cannot answer. Each speaker will be given a pebble. Place it where you would place a memory. We repeat each placement aloud and we witness." His voice was a low metronome; people shifted, somewhere between obedience and the hunger to say more.

Mercy lit a pinch of powdered incense in the dented burner and nodded to the Elder. "We will offer a placation," she said. "We will invite testimony and ask that any naming be given with witnesses. No open talismans until the end."

At first the placation moved like water: a neighbor spoke of seeing bodies carried in the night and buried swiftly; another spoke of a handful of people who took what they could and left the rest; a third insisted—quiet, concrete—that they had hidden people to save them and would do it again. The stories slid and overlapped until the air itself felt torn.

Then the fighting began. When one man described a night of hurried removal as an act of mercy, another spat back that it had been cowardice and abandonment. A woman held up the family photograph like an accusation—she said, voice cracked, that she'd once seen the child in that picture near the stairwell—and two others answered, claiming they had seen a different face at that spot. Voices overlaid like film tracks out of sync; the shrine's low ceiling made the overlays a chorus that frayed nerves.

Now she had to persuade people to accept a different risk: naming as repair. She set the dented censer down and looked at the faces she had to either calm or convince. The spirit made a small, sharp sound like many doors closing all at once and the shrine's light seemed to shiver.

The meeting broke with no clear vote. A witness list was begun, names pencilled in Mercy's notebook; some volunteered to stand and bear testimony at a later, witnessed naming; others left quietly with shoulders drawn in. The community had not yet chosen the path, and the spirit had fed. Mercy folded her talismans back into her kit, feeling the strain in her body, and the Elder's hand rested on her shoulder—an acknowledgement of the road they'd taken and the danger ahead.
