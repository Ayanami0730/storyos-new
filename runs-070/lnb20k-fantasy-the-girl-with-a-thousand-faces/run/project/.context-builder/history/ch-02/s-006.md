The ruined library smelled of smoke and dry paper; dust lay thick in the light that slanted through a single half-shuttered window. Mercy moved her hands over the central table as if arranging thought into place—oil lamp to one side, a small stack of bundled papers, a locked box whose brass had been eaten by time. The Elders' Circle sat in a loose semicircle: older faces like patched cloth, wary and watchful. The Elder Storyteller bid her sit and did not hurry as if he had already measured the value of time.

He untied the ribbon and eased the battered story scroll from its cloth. When he unrolled it the paper protested softly, a long sigh of foxed pages. Marginal notes crowded the edge in different inks; names had been added and rubbed thin until some lines were nothing but a smudge or a blank where ink refused memory. The scroll was a map of interruptions: dates half-lined out, columns with names that stopped mid-word, a margin scrawled over where an argument had once been cut short.

Mercy did not expect clarity. She expected history to be a riddle. She leaned forward and read what she could: a ragged list of passes and places, fragments that suggested a night when movement was hurried and secret. The Elder tapped a place where a name had been blackened. "They wrote, and then they scratched," he said, not accusing one person so much as naming the communal habit. "This is what silence does—turns names into blanks."

Around the table the elders murmured. One insisted on witnesses before any attempt to place a name; another argued the danger of waving a name in the open. The Circle's rules were the sound of the city trying to keep itself whole: ritual with witnesses, accounting for names, small payments for permission. They tested Mercy with questions that were not technical—had she the stomach for what naming might bring, and the patience for the cost.

Mercy answered with what she had learned to furnish interrogations: a practical ledger of next steps. "We cross-reference," she said. "Let the scroll guide us to ledgers and newspapers. We do not shout names, but we do not hide them either. We ask the hospital and check the ward lists. We gather witnesses for a naming if the Circle agrees." She said it not as bravado but as method—a way to move from intuition to evidence.

The Elder nodded. He brought out a folded bundle of smuggled newspapers and a thin medical ledger from a wrapped roll; he did not say who had risked bringing them but his fingers brushed the brittle paper with a private gratitude. "There are gaps in every book," he said. "But where books fail, voices remain. We will call the ones who remember and we will keep the names with witnesses."

They set to work in the small, careful way of people who had to guard the city from both forgetting and from its deadlier opposite—bandying names without care. Mercy fed the lamp and the Elders passed around the scroll while she cross-checked fragments against ledger dates and the scrubbed headlines. The medical ledger was a narrow thing, columns and smudged ink; entries had been made in haste and sometimes stopped where a pen had been pulled away. The pairings were tentative: a date here might match a patrol there; a payroll stub might explain a surge of arrivals in a ward.

Curiosity turned into a practical experiment: Mercy asked for a small, controlled naming to test whether a focused invocation could split the overlay the spirit left. It was not a full rite—no altar, no salt line—only a diagnostic phrasing with the dented censer from her kit and a single talisman held between two fingers. The Elder watched without intervening; the Circle had agreed to witnesses and the library's corners were full of listening faces.

She lit powdered incense in the censer; the smoke curled up and the lamp's light pooled on the paper. Mercy whispered the short, low naming she used for probing—no set text recorded, only the syllabic edge of an asking—and let the smoke gather into the scroll. It acted like a mirror: for a breath layers of voice returned, not to reply but to argue. Cantonese tones braided with a child's thin sing-song and the sharp, clipped phrase of a soldier's bark. The sound made the air in the ruined room compact and heavy.

The test cost her more than usual. The high ringing at the edge of her hearing—still there from earlier rites—rose into a thin blade; for a moment the room narrowed and Mercy felt a small memory slough off, a detail that had anchored a face in her head becoming mist. She blinked hard and steadied herself on the table. Around her the elders flinched at a syllable that meant nothing to them and everything to someone else.

The result was ambiguous and terrible: the scroll's frayed column shivered as if the ink itself had been made to rise and answer, but no clear name landed in the ledger. Instead, a dozen fragments surged—an unplaced child, a shouted command, a ledger entry smudged as if someone had wept over it. The Elders exchanged looks that were equal parts fear and recognition. The library had given them evidence enough to suspect an unmarked massacre—a cluster of arrivals and hurried burials whose records stopped mid-page—but not the proof to name the dead without hazard.

The Elder tapped the margin where a blank had been left. "See this?" he said. "It is a wound kept shut. If we pry it open we must be ready for what bleeds out. Names will not pacify it by themselves. They will make it answer."

Mercy felt the responsibility settle like a weight across her shoulders. She had asked for evidence and the world had replied with more questions. The ritual had made the spirit more articulate but also angrier; the echoes she heard in the room were not merely sound but accusation. She remembered the bombed yard—how the cloth had sung to the spirit—and understood that what she could do with a name required more than courage. It required witnesses, accounts, and a plan to hold the community through what naming would release.

They closed the books at dusk. The Elders' Circle insisted on a slow road: witnesses gathered, a modest offering, and a naming that would be done in a public, controlled manner if and when they could triangulate ledger entries with oral testimony. Mercy folded the talisman into her palm and felt the ache at her temple where the test had tugged the memory loose; the price of inquiry had been paid in a thinning of a detail she wished she'd kept.

She stood to leave with the scroll rolled half back into its cloth. The Elder's hand paused near the edge and then he nodded, not as owner but as custodian: he would keep the paper safe and bring names only with witnesses. Mercy tucked the talisman back into her kit and said, softly, "We start with the ledgers. We ask the hospital orderly. We gather witnesses." It was a plan and a promise to the room; it was a step toward making the city's silence answerable.

The bombed yard kept its hurt like an old animal keeps a broken leg—guarded, tender, and stubbornly public. Mercy stepped into the pocked space and felt the scraping wind like a hand along the back of her neck. Shallow pits dotted the hard ground, patched with mismatched stones; a few small mounds suggested hurried burials, each marked by whatever scrap could be spared—a splintered board, a tin can, a shoe turned face-down.

He sat on one of the low rubble walls when she found him: thin, shoulders bowed as if the weight of everything had sat on them, a teenager in the hush between boy and man. His clothes were threadbare, sleeves patched at the elbows; his hands kept busy folding and refolding something small and careful. When Mercy crouched opposite him he did not rise. He would not give a name.

Among those few things he had gathered a cloth lay folded like a soft accusation. It was not large—no more than the size of a handkerchief—but the corner where it had been pressed against flesh had darkened and browned; a faint metallic tang rose off it when Mercy leaned close, the smell of long-dried blood and damp earth.

She did not touch it at first. That was a rule learned by habit: tokens can open the wrong doors if handled without a witness. But the yard answered to the cloth as if it were a tide to the moon. When Mercy allowed a breeze to tease the fabric's edge the air sharpened; the wail that had been thin and intermittent since the shrine swelled and folded itself around the scrap as if recognizing a voice.

The spirit's reaction was immediate and greedy. Cold seeped into the yard like iron water; the ribs of Mercy's fingers went numb where they brushed the cloth. A child's laugh rose from the far side of the yard and bent into the sound of someone calling for help; voices layered and slipped, dialects braiding until the memory the yard offered could be named in no single tongue.

Mercy kept her face governed. The ring at the high edge of her hearing—left over from the shrine rite—made the world feel brittle; it sharpened the sound of the cloth like a knife. When she let the fabric lift, a vision moved behind her eyes: not a scene she could step into but the feel of a market shouted apart by sudden orders, the panic in a narrow corridor, a child pressed into a woman's back and the weight of something lost. It was not literal history; it was affect and fragmentation pressed into the shapes memory would take when it could not find a proper record.

He said nothing about origins. He would not give a name, and when Mercy asked where he had found the cloth he only shrugged as if the shrug were an answer and then began to whisper a single word that might have been a prayer or might have been a name and then stopped, as if the syllable burned him to say. Mercy read what she could: the cloth hooked the spirit's attention and, through that hook, drew up images that made more house-breaths shallow and doors stay unlatched at night.

The yard's edges filled with the small witnesses of a city too close to its own wounds. Someone leaned from a second-story window and watched as if drawn by a moth to light; a child peered from behind a shutter, finger pressed to lips. The noise threaded up stairwells and settled in rooms that had been sleeping; doors were left slightly ajar in case someone needed to run.

Mercy laid the cloth across her palms for the shortest of moments—only to feel its history like a heat that wasn't heat—and then wrapped it back into its faintly stiff folds. She did not claim that the cloth proved anything about who had been hurt or how; the object was an anchor, not a ledger. "It will make the thing louder," she told the gathered faces, voice low. "It is an anchor. It makes it answer." She could not say more without risking the thing naming itself in the wrong way.

As the visitors watched, the yard tasted colder and the wailing lengthened; stones shifted as if a hand had passed over the ground. Mercy felt the cost of the earlier rite reassert itself—her chest tightened, a blank slipped through the corner of a memory she had tried to hold—and the numbness in her fingers lasted longer than it had that morning. The spirit's anger had found a nearer piece of its wound and pressed.

He sat very still as the yard hummed; when a neighbor reached down instinctively to close a shutter, the boy put out a hand and stopped them. There was a look in his eyes then—vacant, yes, but also like someone listening to a voice only he could half-hear—and Mercy saw the way the cloth linked him to something that was not yet a story but was already an accusation.

She rose, the cloth carefully folded back among his other things, and said, "We will take it to the shrine. We will have witnesses." It was not a promise of cure. It was a way to name the problem in a room where more people could speak. The yard held its breath like an animal waiting for the next move. Mercy felt the city tilt a degree closer to panic as neighbors returned to their windows and whispered more loudly than before. The presence had not been quelled; it had answered and grown.

She walked back through the low gate with the cloth small against her palm and the taste of iron in the air. Around her, the steps and doors of the Walled City moved closer, like lungs inhaling preparing for more.
