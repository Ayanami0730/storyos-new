- Ritual efficacy depends on correct materials, words, and community context; exorcisms can bind, release, or transform spirit-anchors but always carry a measurable personal cost to the ritualist or participants (loss of memory, physical injury, diminished sight/hearing, temporary blindness to emotion, etc.).
- Mercy Chan’s craft follows a coherent, internally consistent folk system (incantations, offerings, archival testimony). She cannot perform miracles like raising the dead or reversing wartime events.
- Spirits can manifest physically (poltergeist activity, sickness) and psychically (visions, emotional contagion). Physical laws hold except where explicitly suspended by ritual; nobody gains supernatural invulnerability.
- Community consent matters: large-scale rites require the residents’ participation; deceptive or coercive rituals produce backlash that strengthens the spirit.
- The revenant is not a single person but a composite wound; it responds when specific tokens or places are disturbed and is weakened by acts of truthful remembrance and repair rather than lies or suppression.
- Time is linear; memory manifests as overlayed scenes but not literal time travel—characters can witness the past as vision, not rejoin it.

Mercy moved through the market with her shoulders folded, carrying the day's small injuries like a private cloak. The wet market smelled of wet fish and frying oil, tarps overhead casting the lanes in green-brown light; puddles mirrored faces and hawkers called in clipped phrases that sliced through the air. Stalls selling old things clustered near the edge—boxes of postcards, talismans, a vendor who kept a battered tin of photographs under a cloth.

The young refugee hung back as she passed, hands jammed into patched pockets, eyes like a boy who had spent too long listening for the wrong sound. He kept to the tarpaulins' shade and did not barge into bargaining; he watched tokens move in other people's hands with the taut neutrality of someone who'd learned what an object could do. Mercy kept him in her peripheral vision and slowed at a small stall where paper things were sold by the handful.

The vendor shuffled a pile of postcards into a neat square and prodded a bundle toward her. She did not expect anything of consequence—tokens traded like seasoning in the market—but a folded photograph, tucked between a postcard and a talisman, caught her eye. It was small and faded; the faces had been worn to the ghost of smiles. Mercy took it out with the same precise patience she used with a talisman, and the market did the strange thing the city had been doing more lately: the world tilted to fit whatever object she held.

On the back, ink had been written once and then rubbed; there was a partial loop of letters and then a ragged smudge where a name had been dragged away. An old stallholder leaned forward and squinted at the mark. "That's a studio stamp, maybe—see the ghost of a circle?" he muttered, voice thin with age. "Or a place name—maybe '—wan'—" He did not insist; it was a guess wrapped in memory. Mercy let the fragment sit like a promise she might keep. She slipped the photograph back into her palm and said, quietly, "I'll cross-reference this with the scroll and the ledgers at the shrine." 

When she lifted the photo the market's sounds folded over one another like paper: a hawker's call braided with a shouted order, the slap of fish on a board overlaid with a child's whimper. The air thinned and then thickened; a whisper threaded her ear that felt like a hand pointing back at some moment that had been hurried and cruel. It was not a single memory but a pressure—a sense of betrayal given shape and then unmade.

The young refugee flinched as if struck. He stepped forward before he could think better of it, fingers reaching for the photograph though his jaw worked as if to keep a word from escaping. Mercy noticed the set of his shoulders, the way his eyes filled with something that was almost recognition. He would not give a name—he had not in the yard—but the photograph made a sound in his body that was too loud to hide.

Mercy kept the photograph between her palms for only a moment longer and then held it closer under the tarp's half-light. The image did not simply show faces; it suggested other faces laid atop them, a blur where two lives had been forced into the same frame. For a second the photograph acted like a magnet: neighboring stallholders blinked, one hand paused in mid-haggle, and an old woman folded her apron across her chest as if to hold herself together.

She felt the high ringing at the edge of her hearing rise into a thin sharpened wire. The diagnostic cost from the shrine and the library—little tolls she'd been carrying—tightened into something immediate: her vision narrowed at the edges and a small, bright blank opened where a detail usually sat. She tasted iron at the back of her throat and held the photograph like a small, dangerous offering.

Around her, the spirit answered as it had in the shrine: a child's laugh that bent into a shout; a market cry that became an order; a half-remembered lullaby that turned into an accusation. The stall's tarpaulin fluttered though no wind had come; a stack of postcards shivered as if fingers had brushed them. Mercy felt the attention turn from the yard's cloth to this new token. It was as if the spirit, finding a clearer face to name, tightened itself around the photograph.

The young refugee made a sound low in his throat and stepped back a pace, eyes fixed on the picture. Mercy saw a modern, practical fear in him—fever-broken attention, a person who knew what a token could do to the living. He did not reach for the vendor or demand the photograph; instead he buried his face briefly in his sleeve and came up with eyes that looked older than his years.

Mercy did not pretend she could read the photograph's truth. Photographs lie like witnesses; they are partial and they claim more than one story. But the way the market bent and the way the boy's body betrayed him told her one thing clearly: the photo was an anchor. It did not prove origin, but it focused the spirit's hunger into a point much nearer and much more personal. She told the vendor a price, slipped coins into the man's palm, and folded the photograph into the small inner pocket of her woven kit as if tucking a lit ember into cloth.

When she rose and walked away, the market kept shifting around the place where the photograph had been held. A cat leapt from a stall and knocked over a tin with a thin metallic clang that sounded, in her ragged hearing, like a single name. The young refugee did not follow; he watched her leave with the haunted attention of someone who had lost a voice. Mercy felt, almost like a pain, that the spirit's focus had narrowed. Where before it had fed on scattered tokens and general silence, it now had a face it could point to.

Holding the photograph in her kit, Mercy felt the liabilities of that hold as concretely as the photograph's paper: shepherding a thing like that into evidence invited the spirit closer, and whatever she learned from it might ask more than she could offer. The high ringing threaded through the noise like a wounded wire. She tightened her fingers on the kit's strap and moved, cautious and watched, toward the shrine where witnesses could be gathered and the photograph could be read with care.

They gathered on the stair that led down to the courtyard because there was nowhere else large enough and quiet enough to hold a meeting. The stairwell smelled of oil and old soap; children’s drawings were pinned to a beam and flapped in the draft like small flags. Mercy sat on the worn step, her kit at her knee, and let the room settle into the shape of what had to be decided.

Neighbor Lei did not wait for the circle to finish. He stood up as if he had been keeping his breath in his chest and could not any longer. "We can't wait," he said, the words hard as a lid. "Those spirits—whatever they are—are hurting our children. We need to burn them out of the yard. We need a big rite. Now."

A murmur ran like water among the gathered faces. A woman with a soot-smudged apron shook her head and folded her arms around herself as if to hold a pot to her breast. "You start that in the open and the patrols will see the smoke," she said. "They'll come with questions we don't want. We lose more than a ghost if soldiers come asking about gatherings."

The argument that followed had the quick, ugly shape of an argument over food. Some wanted decisive action; others named the cost in quiet sentences—checkpoints, fines, a dragged inquiry that would open doors the elders had tried to keep shut. Mercy listened, the high ring in her ears rasping like a distant bell. She had already paid for the day's small rites; it showed in a white ache at her temple and a thinning at the edges of memory. That made her careful the way illness makes a person careful around fire.

"If we do nothing," Lei said, and he did not sound like a man who wanted headlines, "it will get bolder. It will put a pot through a child's face, or it will break a window at the wrong moment. You want that?" The question landed and the stair fell into a measuring silence.

The Elder stood at the back, folded packet in hand, and watched the debate like someone listening for the right seam. He did not tell them what to do; he reminded them of consequence. "Ritual without witnesses will make this worse if it is done in a hurry," he said softly. "We name to hold, not to display. We take witnesses and we do not make smoke that a patrol might read as signal."

The room smelled suddenly of metal and rain. A ladle flew from a hook over an old stove and arced across the stair, clanging against the wall and dropping inches from the child leaning on the rail. A woman screamed, fingers white on the banister, and the immediate urge to lash out rose in some faces like a fever.

Mercy moved forward, not because she wanted to be brave but because she needed to measure. She set the dented burner on the step and lit a thin strip of powdered incense—not a rite, just a small test. The smoke threaded up and caught in the stair's shadows. For a breath the air stilled, and then the bowl on a landing near the stove shivered and slowly filled with water that smelled of iron; it rose until it slopped over the edge and darkened the wood, a small, deliberate flood.

The escalation did not feel random. It hit where voices were loud and where accusations were thrown. "It gets louder where we speak loudest," Mercy said, more to herself than the room, and then louder: "It feeds on contradiction and on secrets named in anger. That is how it finds edges."

Someone pulled a child's doll from a basket to hold it still, as if the smallness of it might quiet the noise. When the cloth dress was lifted, the doll's belly bore a sewn scar of dried red—stitched crudely in a child's hand. The sight made older hands go slack. "It is cruel enough to mimic a wound," the Elder said. "It mirrors with accusation."

Debate hardened into policy in the space of a minute. A group clustered with Lei at their center; they took torches and the determination of people who had decided already. Another group stayed behind, faces like drawn curtains, and murmured about witnesses and ledger checks and the danger of patrols. Mercy felt the shift as a physical thing: the commune's subtle glue had split; two ways of surviving pulled at its seams.

She tried to shape the split into something usable. "If you must go," she told Lei, "take no more than three. Do it to the yard, not to the shrine. Bring witnesses who can vouch for what you do. Do not make smoke that would signal. If you raise noise, you raise danger."

Lei's jaw tightened, but he nodded like a man who had not wanted permission so much as to be listened to. Half a dozen heads turned toward the yard as if it were already burning. The Elder watched the small departures with his packet held to his chest and said nothing more than a warning: "We name with care. We do not name to blame a living man for what the dead accuse."

As the small band gathered torches and pushed toward the yard, the rest of the stair went quiet and watchful. Doors closed softly; shutters fell into place; a neighbor leaned out to count the departure and then slid back like someone watching an illness go by. Mercy felt the city's old nervousness rise—patrols watched for heat and for gatherings—and she feared the unintended consequence more than she feared the spirit's temper.

Outside the stair, the courtyard waited like a held breath. Mercy slid her kit closed and felt the leather strap bite the side of her hand. The ringing at the high edge of her hearing hummed like a damaged wire; she pressed her thumb to the gap between strap and skin as if she could press away the ache. The spirit had made itself cruel and precise; it had shown how it used accusation and contradiction to turn neighbors into lines.

She let them go, but not without words. "Watch them," she said to the group that stayed. "If they get reckless, come back. We keep a witness list. We tell the Elders. We do not let this become a hunt."

They filed back into the stairwell like people who had taken a dangerous step. Mercy watched the torches bob toward the yard and felt the weight of a decision she could not make for them. The commune had split; the spirit had found a lever in their fear. That was its wish. Mercy wrapped her fingers tight around her kit and followed at a distance, the night smelling of oil and iron and the small, dangerous heat of torches.
