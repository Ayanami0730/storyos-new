In wartime Hong Kong’s labyrinthine Kowloon Walled City, exorcist Mercy Chan must untangle a malignant revenant whose hatred is fed by the community’s wartime wounds; to silence it she must force memory into the open, negotiate with frightened neighbors, and perform costly rituals that blur the line between ghost‑hunting and communal reckoning.

### world-rules  <world/rules.yaml>

These govern what is TRUE. They do not say what anyone KNOWS.
A character knows a rule only where canon says so; otherwise they may be ignorant of it, wrong about it, or in the middle of working it out — and if the story is about someone discovering one of these, writing it into their thoughts early is how that story gets destroyed.

- Spirits are empowered by unresolved traumatic memory: the more communal silence around an event, the stronger the spirit tied to it becomes.
- Rituals require both correct material forms (tools, words, offerings) and participation or consent from the community to fully alter a spirit’s status.
- Mercy’s ritual power is effective but limited and costly: each major rite draws on her physical and spiritual reserves and can leave lasting wounds.
- No anachronisms: the story must adhere to World War II–era Hong Kong technology, social structures, and language sensibilities; all historical facts used must be later traced to cited scenes for provenance.
- Supernatural effects must interact with social and emotional reality: exorcism succeeds or fails in ways that produce concrete consequences (injury, confessions, social rupture) within the City.
- The novel will prioritize the Walled City’s lived geography (alleyways, rooftops, communal shrines) as a character—no deus ex machina transports out of the City during rites.
- The vengeful spirit cannot be destroyed by force alone; its resolution requires addressing the historical wound that sustains it (acknowledgment, testimony, or reparative act).
