### previous-scene  <novel/chapters/ch-01/scenes/s-001.md>

Mercy Chan had learned to move like a shadow that refused to be lost in the shadow—long practised habit in a place where light came in thin ribs and you paid for every beam. The Walled City folded itself around her as if it had been built to keep secrets: stacked rooms, laundry ladders, the thin chimneys of rooftop gardens breathing smoke into the blue-grey. She kept to the alleys until she reached the squat tin drum that served as a table outside her door, and set her woven kit on it with a small, habitual care.

The kit was not a priest's reliquary but a working thing—battered into its shape by years of being opened and closed in alleys, slipped under beds, set beside stoves. She lifted the lid and the smell of old incense came up like a memory: incense, chalk, paper talismans, a small knife and a handful of worn incantation slips. She touched the slips once, to square them, and the city answered in sound: a distant kettle, a child's single note called down through the lattices, a radio coughing out static.

Mercy checked the little dent in the metal burner she kept folded inside the kit and shook out a pinch of powdered incense into its belly. She did not have a solemn method; she had methods that worked in the cramped, improvised ways this place demanded. She struck the matches—sparks smelled of sulphur and rationed tin—and held the smoke to a talisman until the ink smudged faintly. The motions were small, old, practiced: a half-gesture to name a boundary, an incantation under her breath to feel the city listen. Ritual for Mercy was equipment and patience and the willingness to bargain; it was also a ledger of debts you paid in skin and bone.

The first voice that answered the small clack of her match was not the voice she had expected. It was a man's voice, raw at the edges, calling from a doorway: "Mercy! It's that noise again. The children won't sleep." The voice belonged to Neighbor Lei—sharp, practical—and it came quick enough that Mercy folded the talismans away and slid the kit into the crook of her arm.

She did not hurry. The alleys moved slow with the pace of people counting what they had: a bowl of rice, a child's fever, the hours until curfew. She threaded down a lane where laundry hung like a ragged sky and a pan on a stall clattered once though no one was near it. At the bend the air changed—less the smell of frying oil than a long, thin ache of something like a human throat being wound out. The wailing came from Alley 13—the notorious bend where shadows pooled—and it was close enough that the tiles under Mercy's boots thrummed with it.

A knot of residents had gathered where the alley opened into the narrow courtyard: a man holding a child, an old woman with a shawl over her head, two shopkeepers whose carts had been abandoned. They parted enough for Mercy to slip through; pity and a small, wary deference marked their faces. The commune did not trust easy answers. Protective talismans—red paper at half the doors—fluttered useless and cheap in the draft.

Neighbor Lei was nearer to the wailing than the rest. He pushed forward when Mercy stopped and the practicality in his expression made Mercy think of weather—expected, inevitable, to be read before it arrived.

"It's the bend again," he said. "Came from the same place last night. The laundry's torn this morning, and little Mei kept saying a name in her sleep. We tried the talismans, put the burner out, nothing."

He had smallness in what he feared: not the shape of a revenant, the catalogues of war, only the immediate fact of sleepless children and teeth grinding in the darkness. Mercy listened with the professional patience she had purchased in years of being needed and not always thanked. She set the burner on the ground and lit a new thread of incense; the smoke raked the alleys, and for a second the city seemed to gather itself around that thin white column.

She spoke then, the words soft and spare: "Tell me exactly what the child said. Everything, or nothing we'll do will hold." She tilted her head, letting the sentence act as both question and offering. It was how she opened negotiations: the truth of an incident bound the ritual like a nail.

Lei answered—too fast, the way fear loosens the mouth. "She kept saying ‘Auntie—no, no—' and then some old name—once she said ‘Har,’ like in the old stories. I don't know what it means. I don't know any of that history."

The old woman at Mercy's elbow made a small sound—half cough, half warning—and someone else muttered about the bombed yard, where children sometimes dreamed of shells and not of feeding. The residents' faces folded up into memory in ways that should have been private; the wailing like a hand had palmed their necks and pulled.

Mercy took out one of the worn incantation slips and smoothed it between her fingers. The paper flaked a little at the edge where her thumb had been; it was a map she knew by touch. She did a quick, low naming: nothing grand—just enough to test the tenor of the noise, a whisper for what liked to hide in badly kept grief. The test cost her a small, immediate thing: a prick behind her left eye, a tightening at the base of her throat that always felt like a memory pressing through. She held it steady; the cost was a notch she'd learned to trade for information.

No miracle followed. The wail thinned and then returned with more shape—this time words under the sound, syllables looping and half-formed. Someone's pan clattered again, a child's doll skirt went still on a line, and a shadow unhooked itself from a doorway and slid away. Mercy felt the city doing what it did: offering parts of itself like clues. The spirit, if spirit it was, fed on what had been closed and left to rot.

"I'll look into it," she told Mrs. Choi.

The promise was neither bravado nor comfort; it was a transaction. Mercy folded the lit slip back into the kit, which she slung over her shoulder. She did not pretend she could set everything right—rituals bound and loosened, and sometimes they cost a thing you didn't expect. But she knew how to ask the city for an answer and how to make the commune answer with its own truths. For now that would have to be enough.

As she moved toward the bend where the alley became a throat, someone let a child slip from a hand. The boy stopped a pace away from Mercy and stared at her, eyes too old, and whispered a name she did not know. The sound dropped into the laundry like a coin. Mercy tightened her grip on her kit and stepped after it. The alley drew her in; something in the dark drew its breath and waited.
