The shrine room smelled of old incense and boiled rice, a sweet sour that sat under the air like a patient thing. Mercy set her woven case on the low altar and worked with the same economy she'd used in alleys: open, lay out, test. Inside the case the dented censer caught the light like a small bruise; talismans, a folded incantation slip, chalk and the little knife lay where fingers could find them in the dark.

The Elder stood at the far side of the room as if he'd always been there; when he placed the battered story scroll on the lacquered board the air changed, the way a room listens when paper opens. He handled the battered scroll with a careful reverence, cradling what seemed like the city's memories for a moment—no claim of possession, only the quiet duty of someone asked to hold them.

Mercy did not pretend ceremony could erase what had been done, but rituals did give shape to obligations. She lit powdered incense in the burner and watched the smoke climb. She drew a talisman from the stack and held it between thumb and forefinger, the paper soft where it had been folded, the ink faded but still legible to her touch. She touched the tip of the knife to the smoke without cutting—the motion was a question, not an answer.

She pinched a little salt between two fingers and scattered it in a thin line across the threshold of the altar. It was a small geometry: a border to measure against. She did not count; she did not recite the words aloud. The naming she used was a short, low phrase—no set text recorded, only the shape of an invocation—that called a thing to show itself so it could be answered.

At first the room did what minor rites always did: objects creaked, a moth thudded against a paper lamp, a neighbor's cough came from the next room as if on cue. Mercy felt the familiar, dull bruise behind her left eye—an old toll from testing the dark—and kept the rhythm steady. The smoke pulled at the edges of the shrine like a thread that tried to sew two memories together.

Then the resistance began. It did not arrive slow but layered: voices at once, overlapping in dialects that slid and spat and rose like different instruments playing the same wrong note. For a dizzying second she heard Cantonese, Taishanese, a clipped Japanese fragment, then a child's laugh like someone had thrown it into the machine and sped it up until the syllables frayed. The voices did not answer her; they argued among themselves about what had happened here.

The room went cold in a way that was not the night but a metal touch: Mercy's fingers went numb where they held the talisman. A pressure wrapped the base of her throat as if someone had placed a wet stone there; she swallowed against the draft and felt something unnamed shift at the edges of her memory. For a moment a name she could not hold flashed past like a photograph exposed a second too fast. It left behind only the hollowness of its shape.

She pressed the knife's spine into the incense smoke and traced a slow mark in the air. The incense guttered, then brightened, and voices folded into a chorus that made the shrine feel like a room full of strangers. The Elder watched, his face a map of patient lines. He did not speak to correct; he allowed the rite to run and kept the scroll closed beside him, as if the paper itself might be a third presence.

Mercy tried to pull a single thread—an incantation slip tucked into the weave of the talisman, the quietest of instruments—and the spirit answered by scattering memory instead of centring it. She saw, with that partial sight the cost gave her, a market call braided with a siren and a lullaby. The overlay made no sense: a vendor hawking fish where there had been medics; a child's lullaby that turned under one breath into a shouted name.

The physical cruelty of the thing pressed in. A cold like iron crawled up Mercy's fingers and into her hands; she dropped the talisman so it rattled on the lacquer board. For a second the world went loud in a way that was not sound but pressure: a high, metallic ring that flattened the middle register of her hearing. Voices around the room blurred into a far echo; she could not catch half of what was said. Tears salted the skin at her temples where the tightening had been.

She tried the knife-test—a half-gesture she had used a hundred times—pressing the flat of the blade near the shrine's smoke and then against her forehead as a borrowed measure. The blade was cold and the touch left a small, white ache at the temple where memory thinned. The rite was answered, but not answered: movement, names, the sensation of hands that weren't hands daggling in the air like threads.

The Elder finally opened the scroll. He did not unroll it wide; he pried a single page and let the smell of old paper push into the smoke. "Names are not neutral," he said, the sound a dry measure. "Calling what is hidden calls the thing that fed on hiding." He did not give Mercy a formula; he offered the scale of the harm.

Mercy felt something go again—this time not only a narrowing of sight but a small, nameless blank where a detail might have been: the shape of a doorway that had been in a memory she could almost summon vanished like fog. Her chest tightened so that breathing sounded like scraping the inside of a pot. When she tried to speak the syllables low and precise, the words rasped out hollow and short.

She pulled the talismans in and let the smoke thin. The incense burned down to a coal and then another clump of memory flared: a child's laugh that curdled into a shout and a voice that said, in a dozen tones at once, accusingly, "You left them." The accusation did not carry a single person but felt like the weight of a city being named guilty.

When Mercy finished she could not claim victory. The presence had been prodded and had shown teeth instead of truth. The shrine was quieter but the sound had moved; the feeling rested now like a bruise at the base of her skull and a ringing behind her ears. Her hands trembled; she pressed the talisman to her sternum as if to hold herself together.

The Elder closed the page and set the scroll back in its box. He looked at Mercy with something that might have been pity and something that might have been calculation. "It is not one wound," he said. "It is many, braided. We will not sew it with a single thread."

She nodded. The ritual had failed to quiet the thing; it had only shown the city's fracture. Mercy wrapped the kit closed, the leather strap cutting a line across her palm, and felt the numbness in her fingers linger. The cost was not only the thin bruise at her sight or the hollowing of a memory; it had taken away a sliver of the ordinary world—her hearing had a high, ringing edge—and left her with something like a scar.

Outside, the air at the shrine door carried the distant, brittle sound of the yard: loose stones shifting, a child's shoe scuffing, the thin breath of a wind that never quite warmed. Mercy stepped to the threshold and, with the room's half-attentive watch on her, said, quiet and steady, "We go to the yard next." The Elder's hand rested over the closed scroll as if weighing what could be said aloud. The rite had not been a cure. It had been a report; it had cost her something. The story remained—tighter, stranger, and more dangerous than either of them had expected.
