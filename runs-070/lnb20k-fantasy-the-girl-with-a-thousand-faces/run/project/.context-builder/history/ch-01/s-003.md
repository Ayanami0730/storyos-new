### world-rules  <world/rules.yaml>

These govern what is TRUE. They do not say what anyone KNOWS.
A character knows a rule only where canon says so; otherwise they may be ignorant of it, wrong about it, or in the middle of working it out — and if the story is about someone discovering one of these, writing it into their thoughts early is how that story gets destroyed.

- Setting is historically grounded: World War II–era Hong Kong (Japanese occupation). No anachronistic technology or modern institutions may appear.
- Spirits are formed from and fed by unresolved memory, trauma, and communal silence; the more contradictory and suppressed the memory, the stronger the spirit.
- Ritual efficacy depends on correct materials, words, and community context; exorcisms can bind, release, or transform spirit-anchors but always carry a measurable personal cost to the ritualist or participants (loss of memory, physical injury, diminished sight/hearing, temporary blindness to emotion, etc.).
- Mercy Chan’s craft follows a coherent, internally consistent folk system (incantations, offerings, archival testimony). She cannot perform miracles like raising the dead or reversing wartime events.
- Spirits can manifest physically (poltergeist activity, sickness) and psychically (visions, emotional contagion). Physical laws hold except where explicitly suspended by ritual; nobody gains supernatural invulnerability.
- Community consent matters: large-scale rites require the residents’ participation; deceptive or coercive rituals produce backlash that strengthens the spirit.
- The revenant is not a single person but a composite wound; it responds when specific tokens or places are disturbed and is weakened by acts of truthful remembrance and repair rather than lies or suppression.
- Time is linear; memory manifests as overlayed scenes but not literal time travel—characters can witness the past as vision, not rejoin it.
- No contradictions with later committed scenes: all new claims must be recorded with provenance in the index when written.
- The story must balance supernatural horror with historical/social realism; the haunting’s resolution requires both ritual action and community moral reckoning.

Mercy followed the whisper like a seam. The boy's name fell into the laundry and the alley took it and chewed, folding the sound into itself; she kept after the shape of that sound through the bend where the tiles sang and the shadow waited. At the mouth of Alley 13 a strip of light lay like a bar of metal across the floor; beyond it the world was a tight, breathed-in dark. Mercy stepped past the drying shirts and into a pocket of steam where the communal kitchen opened like a throat.

The kitchen hit her with a wash of frying oil and steam, the smell so sharp it made her sinuses ache; kerosene stoves blackened at their rims, benches scarred by bowls, and an open hatch—if it could be called that—looked toward the alley. People crowded the benches, leaning in as if conversation itself might keep the noise at bay. A pot clattered from an empty stall and everyone jerked as if struck. The sound had the wrong body; it came too precise to be accidental.

Someone put an arm around the old woman Mercy had seen in the alley; someone else held a child's sleeve taut as if to prevent the small body from answering a summons it would not understand. The commune smelled of laundry, of woodsmoke, and the metallic tang of ration tins left half-open on the table. Mercy set her kit down with the same careful motions she used in the alleys—no grand gestures, only the small things people could forgive—and her fingers fumbled to close the woven flap before she even looked at the room. The kit's leather strap thudded against the wood; the matches in her palm left the familiar sting of sulphur on her skin.

"Pots are falling off shelves," said Neighbor Lei, the words sharp as a scrape of tin. "The boy keeps whispering that name. Mei won't eat."

Mercy did not ask for more yet. She watched faces instead—the way fear pulled at the corners of mouths, the way people looked to other people for permission to be afraid. This was not only the child's problem; you could read the city in how the room rearranged itself to accommodate terror. She lit a thin stick of incense and let it burn down until the smoke thickened into a pale column. Then she did what she always did: a low, patient naming, a small phrase to prod what hid in closed places.

The cost came as it always did: not a thunderbolt but a small, private bruise. Mercy felt her sight narrow for a second, as if a curtain had been drawn across the edges of what she could see; tears prickled though she did not want them. She kept the naming simple—only the edges of a question, an invitation for whatever answered to show itself clearly enough to be named. The smoke curled and, for a breath, the air tasted of old iron and damp soil.

A shadow at the far doorway unhooked itself from the wall and slid across the floor like someone trying not to be seen. The children tightened their grips on sleeves; an old man whispered a name Mercy did not know. A pot clattered again; this time two plates on a shelf rattled as if someone had run a hand along their edge. The clatter echoed in the kitchen's low ceiling and hung for a moment like a sentence left unfinished.

From the back of the room, as if a person had been waiting until the right moment to step forward, the Elder Storyteller leaned on the edge of the central table. He was small-boned and folded like old paper; he eased a folded packet from his sleeve and leafed an edge open, letting the smell of brittle, incense-scented notes meet the kitchen smoke. He spoke with the sort of voice that had the whole room learn to listen: slow, economical, the vowels rounded like coins.

"This is an old hurt," he said, not to Mercy alone but to the room. "Not the sort you cover with red paper and call it whole. It answers—" he hesitated, choosing, "—to names. To stories people tuck under their mats." He let the last words fall as if they were a bowl he did not want back.

Mercy watched him. He did not approach with the authority of command so much as the weight of precedent. The Elder's words told Mercy two things: that the wound had duration, and that it fed on omission. He kept the folded packet close; he did not unroll any community scroll, only a personal copy—an old packet of notes he consulted as one consults a watch.

"There were burials out in the yard," someone muttered. The bombed yard that everyone named with the same bitter air in their throats. A woman by the stove wiped her hands as though she could scrub memory away.

Mercy did not press for specifics. Names here could become levers; names could invite something forward that would not leave. So she worked instead to make a map of what was present and what the city might spare. She asked the simple question she had learned to trust: "Who heard the voice? When? What did the child say?"

The answers were small and contradictory: Mei heard it at night; a plate fell at dawn; the laundry tore this morning; the wailing is worse near the bend. A voice in the back named the bombed yard again, quietly, then clapped a hand over his mouth as if he'd spoken too long. Mercy filed each fragment in her mind, a slow, patient note-taking that felt almost like medicine.

The Elder watched her file the pieces together. He did not offer more than his folded hints, but when Mercy closed her kit he tapped the packet with one finger and said, softly, "You cannot fix this alone, child. The City remembers more than you can ask it to carry." The use of the word child was not a diminishment so much as a way to name the order of their work: she held tools; he held memory.

She let the words sit between them. Outside, at the bend, a shadow folded itself like a hand and the wail rose, thinner this time and threaded with syllables that might one day be names. Mercy took a breath that tasted of smoke and iron, tightened her fist on the woven kit until the weave bruised her palm, and said to the room, quiet and clear, "I will go look at the yard."

It was not a promise in the ledger she had made before—this one was the kind that opened obligations. The Elder's eyes, small and unreadable, rested on her as if measuring what the city would cost her for that walk.
