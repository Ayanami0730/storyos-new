### previous-scene  <novel/chapters/ch-01/scenes/s-002.md>

They had made a small crowd of the spiral — the way a wound gathers curious hands. People leaned from thresholds, children clung to skirts, and someone kept a careful, respectful distance as if the alley might flense a name from them if they came too close. Mrs. Choi had set an upside-down pot lid on the crate beside Mercy; she kept one fist around a child's single shoe and held it to her chest as she spoke, as if the shoe itself might quiet the memory.

"It goes on at night," Mrs. Choi said again, voice low and cracked. She lifted the little red talisman above her doorway with a trembling thumb when she mentioned the calls. "It calls like someone who remembers being burned. The boys won't pass. The talisman flipped last night — face-down on the stair."

Neighbors added the variations Mercy had already heard and filed: "It starts after the sirens stop, like something scraping on the plaster." "The air went right through you, and the talisman was face-down on the step." "My talisman buzzed all night after she touched the stone." Their words layered like mortar; Mercy let them set while she assessed the markers beneath the chatter.

She moved with the slow attention of someone making a map of throb and edge. The alley held the same environmental markers the rite warned would persist. A cold patch hung near the left-hand stair — not a breeze so much as an absence that made the skin pucker. The thin black crescent on the stoop was still there, dark against the worn stone: a scorch like the bite of heat with no ash to explain it. A talisman lay face-down by a threshold, its paper rimmed with the dust of someone who'd worried it with fingers.

Mercy set the incense box on the crate as she had in the morning and readied the rite with the same unspectacular motions. The steps were small and precise: three soft bell strikes to test the air's response; a pinch of sandalwood and frankincense lit and coaxed until smoke gathered; the knife's edge traced above that smoke; the words spoken, the talisman folded over the tip and pressed to the forehead. She worked without ceremony for the crowd's sake, but the ritual's choreography tightened her fingers into the habit that kept her steady.

"Three soft strikes," she murmured, and the bells answered — once, twice, a third time. The sound came back thinner than it should have been, as if the metal had hollowed.

She watched the smoke as she lit the sandalwood; the frankincense caught slow and thick. She traced the knife's edge above the smoke and mouthed the words she used for finishing and for testing alike — a short, practiced invocation folded into a question: "If blood remembers, let it speak." When the folded talisman touched the knife-tip and she pressed it to her forehead, Mercy felt the city move under her palms. The old, familiar price followed: a brief lightheadedness and the metallic tang at the back of her mouth. Then the imprint's signatures came clear.

The smoke bent as if pulled toward a shape the eye could not hold. The bells answered with a hollow ring that shivered through the crate and set the teeth on edge. The talisman's paper thrummed in her palm like a trapped insect. The cold patch refused to warm when bodies crowded it; the scorched crescent stayed black and particular. Small metal things nearby began to tremble with a low, continuing vibration, the kind of thrum that made a talisman hum in the night.

Neighbors murmured what they saw; their voices snagged on Mercy's reading. "She held the bell and it rang hollow, like a coin with a hole," a man said. "The smoke bent around her hands — I saw it reach like fingers." Mercy let those observations stand beside the rite's indicators and set them into a pattern she could trust: this was not a household whisper or a child's fright. It had the weight and persistence of something rooted to the City's shared memory.

Mrs. Choi's hands were small and knotted. She tapped the upside-down lid as if to prove to herself the world still kept its shape. Mercy's fingers brushed the woman's hand briefly; the contact did nothing to change the cold's edge.

Searching the nearest front rooms for anything that might anchor this presence, Mercy asked to look in the old lacquer cupboard that sat against a wall in a ground-floor stall. The lock was old; someone had pried at it recently. Mercy worked the door open with a flat screwdriver borrowed from a neighbor and peered into the dim. Tucked behind a false back, hidden as if hurriedly, she found a photograph folded and stained, the edges feathered with age and damp.

It was a small black-and-white of a barefoot boy in a plain shirt, no more than seven or eight. Something dark stained the corner of the image — a smear that could have been blood or rust. The boy's mouth was a thin line; his eyes were steady and looking slightly away from the camera, the way children sometimes look when posed and told to be brave.

Mercy lifted the photograph with two careful fingers and pressed it flat on the crate. The crowd bent toward it without permission, but Mercy closed her hand over the card and slid it into the inner fold of her robe instead. She could not name the face yet; she suspected a wartime origin, a boy lost or taken in some skirmish, but she had only the photograph and the alley's markings to make that suspicion live.

She straightened and let the crowd's murmurs and averted eyes fill the spiral once more. "This isn't the small haunt that forgets when you pass by," she said, low enough that only the nearest could hear. "It answers to more than one house. It answers to the City."

That was the promise she meant for Mrs. Choi: not a tidy ending but the beginning of a search. She had a photograph pressed to her heart and a set of imprint markers to follow. Mercy closed the box and prepared to take the lead out of the spiral — to move it from rumor into something that could be read.
