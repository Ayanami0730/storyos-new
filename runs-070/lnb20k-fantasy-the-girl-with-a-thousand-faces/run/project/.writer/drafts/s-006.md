They gathered on the stair that led down to the courtyard because there was nowhere else large enough and quiet enough to hold a meeting. The stairwell smelled of oil and old soap; children’s drawings were pinned to a beam and flapped in the draft like small flags. Mercy sat on the worn step, her kit at her knee, and let the room settle into the shape of what had to be decided.

Neighbor Lei did not wait for the circle to finish. He stood up as if he had been keeping his breath in his chest and could not any longer. "We can't wait," he said, the words hard as a lid. "Those spirits—whatever they are—are hurting our children. We need to burn them out of the yard. We need a big rite. Now."

A murmur ran like water among the gathered faces. A woman with a soot-smudged apron shook her head and folded her arms around herself as if to hold a pot to her breast. "You start that in the open and the patrols will see the smoke," she said. "They'll come with questions we don't want. We lose more than a ghost if soldiers come asking about gatherings."

The argument that followed had the quick, ugly shape of an argument over food. Some wanted decisive action; others named the cost in quiet sentences—checkpoints, fines, a dragged inquiry that would open doors the elders had tried to keep shut. Mercy listened, the high ring in her ears rasping like a distant bell. She had already paid for the day's small rites; it showed in a white ache at her temple and a thinning at the edges of memory. That made her careful the way illness makes a person careful around fire.

"If we do nothing," Lei said, and he did not sound like a man who wanted headlines, "it will get bolder. It will put a pot through a child's face, or it will break a window at the wrong moment. You want that?" The question landed and the stair fell into a measuring silence.

The Elder stood at the back, folded packet in hand, and watched the debate like someone listening for the right seam. He did not tell them what to do; he reminded them of consequence. "Ritual without witnesses will make this worse if it is done in a hurry," he said softly. "We name to hold, not to display. We take witnesses and we do not make smoke that a patrol might read as signal."

The room smelled suddenly of metal and rain. A ladle flew from a hook over an old stove and arced across the stair, clanging against the wall and dropping inches from the child leaning on the rail. A woman screamed, fingers white on the banister, and the immediate urge to lash out rose in some faces like a fever.

Mercy moved forward, not because she wanted to be brave but because she needed to measure. She set the dented burner on the step and lit a thin strip of powdered incense—not a rite, just a small test. The smoke threaded up and caught in the stair's shadows. For a breath the air stilled, and then the bowl on a landing near the stove shivered and slowly filled with water that smelled of iron; it rose until it slopped over the edge and darkened the wood, a small, deliberate flood.

The escalation did not feel random. It hit where voices were loud and where accusations were thrown. "It gets louder where we speak loudest," Mercy said, more to herself than the room, and then louder: "It feeds on contradiction and on secrets named in anger. That is how it finds edges."

Someone pulled a child's doll from a basket to hold it still, as if the smallness of it might quiet the noise. When the cloth dress was lifted, the doll's belly bore a sewn scar of dried red—stitched crudely in a child's hand. The sight made older hands go slack. "It is cruel enough to mimic a wound," the Elder said. "It mirrors with accusation."

Debate hardened into policy in the space of a minute. A group clustered with Lei at their center; they took torches and the determination of people who had decided already. Another group stayed behind, faces like drawn curtains, and murmured about witnesses and ledger checks and the danger of patrols. Mercy felt the shift as a physical thing: the commune's subtle glue had split; two ways of surviving pulled at its seams.

She tried to shape the split into something usable. "If you must go," she told Lei, "take no more than three. Do it to the yard, not to the shrine. Bring witnesses who can vouch for what you do. Do not make smoke that would signal. If you raise noise, you raise danger."

Lei's jaw tightened, but he nodded like a man who had not wanted permission so much as to be listened to. Half a dozen heads turned toward the yard as if it were already burning. The Elder watched the small departures with his packet held to his chest and said nothing more than a warning: "We name with care. We do not name to blame a living man for what the dead accuse."

As the small band gathered torches and pushed toward the yard, the rest of the stair went quiet and watchful. Doors closed softly; shutters fell into place; a neighbor leaned out to count the departure and then slid back like someone watching an illness go by. Mercy felt the city's old nervousness rise—patrols watched for heat and for gatherings—and she feared the unintended consequence more than she feared the spirit's temper.

Outside the stair, the courtyard waited like a held breath. Mercy slid her kit closed and felt the leather strap bite the side of her hand. The ringing at the high edge of her hearing hummed like a damaged wire; she pressed her thumb to the gap between strap and skin as if she could press away the ache. The spirit had made itself cruel and precise; it had shown how it used accusation and contradiction to turn neighbors into lines.

She let them go, but not without words. "Watch them," she said to the group that stayed. "If they get reckless, come back. We keep a witness list. We tell the Elders. We do not let this become a hunt."

They filed back into the stairwell like people who had taken a dangerous step. Mercy watched the torches bob toward the yard and felt the weight of a decision she could not make for them. The commune had split; the spirit had found a lever in their fear. That was its wish. Mercy wrapped her fingers tight around her kit and followed at a distance, the night smelling of oil and iron and the small, dangerous heat of torches.
