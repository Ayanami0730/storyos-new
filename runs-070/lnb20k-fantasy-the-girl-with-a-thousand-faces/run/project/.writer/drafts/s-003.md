The temple sat like a stopped throat in the middle of the City: an old banner drooping above a narrow stair, a door that remembered more respectful hands. Mercy and Lau climbed down together, the damp smell of the basement rising to meet them — old incense, mildew, lamp oil. Lau fidgeted with a knot of string in his hands; he was fifteen and watched Mercy with the mixture of dislike for being told what to do and the hunger of someone trying to learn a trade that might save them both.

The Elder Storyteller waited at the low altar as if he'd always been there. He moved like memory does — slow at the edges, sudden at the core; his hands were marked with ink and incense, and he handled the brittle scroll with the kind of reverence people reserve for family ledgers. He was a lay keeper of the temple — guardian of the scroll, not its formal priest — and he made that distinction in the way he spoke and in the small rituals he allowed in his rooms.

He hummed an old line, fingers worrying the edge of the scroll before he unfurled it. The paper was foxed and creased; marginal notes in different hands crowded some pages, and a faded thumbprint smudged one annotation. He would not let others touch it without a quiet negotiation — he set the scroll between them like a thing both alive and fragile and kept his hand over it until Mercy had bargained with the room.

Neighbors had filled the main hall, their faces grainy in the candlelight. They were practical people, used to making decisions by ad hoc vote; their fear of the occupation and of old shame made them slow to hand over the key to what lay under the temple. Mrs. Choi stood near the doorway, her knuckled fingers white where she kept folding and refolding the child's single shoe. "We don't open that room since the soldiers came," someone said. "They say the names were buried under the floor."

The Elder tapped the scroll, and then, in a voice like dry paper, offered two fragments that sat like hooks in the air: "they buried names in the under-floor," he said, and later, "the men taken at the water's turn." The words were not a story so much as a nudge — a direction toward the kind of wound Mercy already smelled in the alley.

Negotiation settled into the usual economy. People were afraid of what the past would demand; they wanted protections. An elder woman crossed her arms and said plainly: "We will let you search, if you leave a talisman under the altar and tell us any names you find. Also — the lock must be fixed afterward. Two measures of rice for the lock, and we will close the door again."

Mercy could have argued the ethics of exchange and the cost of keeping a place of shame sealed. Instead she counted the truth of things: the City kept its own governments; an occupier's law sat beyond the thresholds of negotiations like a separate weather. "I will leave a talisman under the altar," she said. "I will tell the names I find. Two measures of rice for replacing the lock — that is fair."

The crowd loosened by a degree. The Elder's eyes narrowed as if he were checking their small bargain against a ledger only he could read. He pushed the scroll closer and pointed at a margin with a thumb that had ink in its lines. "There are marginal notes," he said. "Different hands. Some names rubbed thin. You must be careful what you brush dust from."

Lau made a small, embarrassed sound and tucked the knot of string into his sleeve. He had been with Mercy long enough to know the ritual shapes of such bargains: a promise, an offering, a witness. "I'll hold the talisman," he said quickly, eager in that messy way apprentices are when offered a task that looks important.

The Elder's hand came down on the scroll with a soft authority. "The room hasn't been opened since the occupation began," he said, as if stating a fact that would guard them from any lazy assumptions. The phrase folded into the hall like a small quake. People exchanged looks that avoided names and looked instead at the low altar and the shadowed stair.

Mercy set two measures of rice on the altar herself, the grain bright and ordinary under candlelight. She left a folded red talisman where the smoke would settle and, in a voice meant to be heard, promised to read aloud any names she found. The Elder watched, his fingers touching marginalia with the same careful curiosity he'd used on the scroll. He hummed again, an old note that might be a blessing or might be a warning.

Permission, when it came, was thin and full of conditions: a talisman under the altar; Mercy's spoken promise to tell names; two measures of rice to pay for the lock's replacement. The Elder's approval was not merely technical — it was a social sanction. He tapped the scroll and said, "You may search the basement locked room. But take witnesses and bring the names back to the hall."

Mercy felt the weight of that sanction settle like a second robe. She did not like promises that held costs, but she understood their logic: the room's secrecy had been a social contract; opening it without recompense would break the contract and invite more harm than the risk of a search. She nodded.

Lau's fingers trembled as he turned to fetch a lamp. He moved with the clumsy deference of a boy who had been given a task that was not merely fetching but protective. The Elder folded the scroll back to a safe page and, in the hush that followed the bargain, added, almost to himself, "Mind what the marginal notes hide. Names rubbed thin hide the worst parts."

They descended toward the low stair to the locked door in a slow line: Mercy at the head, the Elder with the scroll at his side, Lau carrying the lamp, and a small knot of neighbors behind them as witnesses. The thin banality of the rice on the altar and the folded talisman under the faded banner felt, for a moment, like bridgework: a way to cross from silence into something that could be named.
