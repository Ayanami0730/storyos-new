The candle guttered once, then steadied as Mercy Chan tilted the small lacquer tray to catch the last of the resin smoke. The room was a cut-out chest: a low mat, shelves crowded with jars and folded talismans, a sheathed blade hung above a cracked basin. She worked by habit and temper — fingers that had learned the heavy economy of rites in a place that owed its quiet to practice rather than blessing.

She lifted a pinch of night-membrane from the incense box, closed her eyes to the City’s muffled night and struck a match. "Light the resins," she mouthed, more to set the words in rhythm than to order herself. The resin flared, the smoke bright as an old bruise. She undid the red cloth and drew the spirit knife free; the metal was cool against her palm. "Trace the knife's edge above the smoke," she said, and her voice smelled of chalk and frying oil.

"If blood remembers, let it speak," she said aloud, the sentence rolling once like a coin across the back of her tongue. She folded a strip from the spare talismans, pressed its crease to the tip of the blade, and leaned the paper against her forehead until the wax of the candle warmed it. The instant the paper kissed skin a taste like coins filled her mouth: copper and the brief, clean panic of dizziness. She blinked away a pulse of lightheadedness and swallowed the metallic tang.

A knock hammered the thin door, the sound not waiting for the end of a breath. Mercy set the knife back into the red cloth with hands that still remembered the pressure of the ritual and opened. A face crowded the doorway — knuckles raw, hair dusted with flour and soot: Mei-sook from the noodle stall two floors below, voice pitched so the walls might keep it from seeping away.

"Mercy! Mercy, you must—" Mei-sook’s words came staccato. She held herself like someone halting a loss, palms to her chest as if to keep the world together. "Child saw it. Little Lin Wei. He says—he says a black shadow, no face."

The name folded into Mercy’s room like another gust of city wind. She threaded a shawl about her shoulders, the medal of routine sliding into a new shape: a summons. "Show me where," she said. The metallic taste at the back of her mouth made her cheeks hollow. "Stay in the stair. Keep him away from the talismans."

The spiral alley smelled of dust and the after-raw of raided roofs — air-raid dust settling like a second skin. Scaffolding and ration lines cut the light into strips; the Walled City breathed through narrow vents and coughed where makeshift clinics patched its wounds. Elder Lau waited at the foot of the stair, a battered cardigan leaving only the necessary flesh of authority. His eyes had the slow patience of someone who had held too many small panics and turned each into a story.

Lin Wei stood close to the wall, knees pressed inward as if the stone might fold him away. He was small enough for Mercy to measure with a look: about ten years old, his clothes a half-fit of someone else’s hands. There was a refugee’s sharpened watchfulness in him — unaccompanied, she thought, no elder’s hand on his shoulder, no sibling’s sling. He spoke with the bluntness of a child who has learned to make the world answer.

"It looked like a shadow with no face," Lin Wei said. He kept his eyes on the seam where the wall threw its own shadow and then lifted them to Mercy as if to hand her the phrase whole. "Keoi mou min — she had no face. She moved like water. She stared at me then went through the wall."

They showed Mercy the place. A patch of air that bites — colder than the rest, like an empty breath — waited in the stairwell where the children often leaned and counted pebbles. A talisman lay face down on the stone, its red like a heart turned over. And on the edge of the step a thin, black crescent scored the concrete, no smoke clinging to it, just a dark sear that did not belong to any nearby pyre.

"Child say — black shadow, no face," Mei-sook told Mercy, and the sentence stitched the small crowd together into a single concern. Elder Lau knelt and pressed his fingers near the cold patch without touching; his face was unreadable. "We put talismans in every door, but it flipped. It flipped on its own."

Mercy crouched. The cold bit, the kind that sank past fabric into a hollow. The taste in her mouth sharpened like a tally. She traced the edge of the scorch with a gloved fingertip, careful and slow. "No soot," she murmured. "Not fire. Not from us."

A child’s gesture and a displaced paper gave the ghost its geometry; someone else’s echoes filled in the rest. "Hear it after the raid — like someone scraping the walls," a neighbor said, voice small. "There was a sound — like fingernails, or a bowl dragged."

Elder Lau looked to Mercy as if she were both a ledger and a verdict. "It’s not a bad talisman that fails, girl," he said. "It is something asking to be remembered."

Mercy’s hands went to the red cloth at her waist, to the slit where the spirit knife slipped into its sheath. She had not asked for this wear, and she felt it already — the cheap calculus: a rite costs; a night of sleep costs; the skin at the back of her throat would taste like metal until she drank water. She tucked the knife back into the cloth and closed her fist about the small lacquer box at her belt. The incense in it smelled of frankincense and sandalwood, familiar and necessary.

She made a decision in the silence that followed — as practical as any arithmetic in the City. "I will stay with the child until the watch changes," she told Mei-sook and the leaning faces. "I’ll come back with the mirror if I must. And I will not let anyone touch the talisman." The last sentence was softer; promises here required fewer words.

Lin Wei watched her, the corners of his mouth loosened as if a puppet’s string had been retied. Mercy felt the knife’s old weight in her memory and the newer, narrower weight of the choice she had just made. The Walled City tightened its ribs around them both, the stair a small throat through which memory might be expelled or swallowed.

She walked back toward her room with the neighbors’ eyes on her shoulders. On the lacquer tray, half-hidden beneath a paper, a faded photograph lay with a dark, blood-darker stain at its edge — a thing she had not been asked about tonight, but which kept its quiet claim on her. She put her hand on the tray and felt the familiar rumple of ritual tools; the taste in her mouth lingered.

Outside, the City settled. Mercy lit the candle again and let the smoke curl, noting each small readiness as if inventory might be armor. Tonight, she had a child’s fear in her hands and a scorched crescent on stone; she had the community watching. For a thing fed by silence, that watching was at once a weapon and a wound.
