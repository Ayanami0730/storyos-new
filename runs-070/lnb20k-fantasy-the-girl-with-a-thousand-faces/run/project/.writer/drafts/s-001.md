Air-raid dust settled over the City like a second skin, catching in the braids of laundry and in the teeth of the scaffolding. Light came in narrow shafts between stacked balconies; a few bare bulbs hung like tired stars. Distant sirens had finished their long, wheezing wail and left a brittle hush that made voices sound too close. Coal smoke and cooking fat mixed with the sour tang of rationed kerosene, and the whole place seemed to breathe through tiny vents — a compressed chest of alleys that took what the world gave it and held on.

Mercy moved through that breathing with the economy of habit. She moved like someone who had learned to make small clearings in chaos: hands precise, shoulders squared, eyes that count exits. The incense box was always folded into that posture, palms resting on its lid as if on a small animal. A battered wooden box sat on her knees when she turned it open: it contained a pinch of sandalwood-resin, a pinch of frankincense, a small satchel of powdered 'night-membrane' herb, a folded red paper talisman, a string of tiny iron bells, a faded photograph tucked in a corner and a wrapped sliver of glass that rattled like a memory.

She was thirty-eight, a hair past the age when other women in the City had started to accept safe trades and quieter lives. Mercy's work had never been safe. She had lost a younger brother in the early fighting; the hollow that made her take the work was the kind people do not map on days of calm. It sat with her in the box: a private liturgy folded in with the talismans, one absence against another.

The call came at the door. Mrs. Choi, who lived two flights up and sold braised duck from a narrow stall, stood framed in the stairwell, her hair a scatter of white at the temple and her hands knuckled around the banister.

"Mercy," she said, voice scabbed with fear. "Come. Quick. There's wailing in the main alley — after the raid. Children won't go. They say — she had no face — mou jat maan (佢冇面)."

At the City's edge, mercilessly visible even in the alleys' clotted geometry, the checkpoint sat in place like a last, indifferent god: sandbags, a half-dozen sentries, a faded banner in the occupiers' script. The presence of order was a thing everyone acknowledged in practice and ignored in speech. Mercy heard the pattern of it in the way people moved: routes skirted, bargains whispered. The checkpoint was a reminder that the violence feeding the Walled City did not start here.

She nodded once to Mrs. Choi. "Show me." When she walked the alleys people made room in a practiced way, part respect and part calculation. Neighbors pressed to the lip of the spiral as Mercy reached it — faces like coins, mouths that had learned the economy of rumor. A child on a father's hip whispered one of the phrases Mercy had begun to hear and file away: "Child say — black shadow, no face."

The Main Alley Spiral was a throat of stone and plaster. Doors overlapped; windows looked over one another like tired witnesses. The scent of burnt cloth came from where someone had tried to burn a blanket after the raid. A scorch marked the stone by a stoop — like fire and like nothing anyone could smoke into explanation.

Mercy set the box on a low crate and went to work without spectacle. She did not make a show of ritual; she did not speak for the crowd. First came a small diagnostic: a bell from the string, three soft strikes, enough to test the air’s response. She watched the smoke as she lit the sandalwood, the frankincense catching slow and thick. She traced the knife's edge above the smoke and mouthed the words she used for finishing and for testing alike — a short, practiced invocation folded into a question: "If blood remembers, let it speak."

The phrase was not a cure so much as a listening. When the talisman touched the knife-tip and she pressed it to her forehead, the city shifted under her hands. Mercy felt lightheaded and tasted metal at the back of her mouth; a brief dizziness and the metallic tang of old blood followed the gesture. It was a familiar cost — the ritual's small exact price — and for a blink she had to steady herself on the crate.

But the signal that answered was not the usual murmur of a memory finding a voice. The smoke bent as if pulled; the bells answered with a hollow that set the teeth on edge. Mercy had learned to read degrees of absence. Grief sat differently from rage; an old mother's regret smelled one way, a child's confusion another. This was heavier. It felt like an accusation left in the mouth of a place: sharpened, bright, and hungry.

Mrs. Choi's hand found Mercy's sleeve. "They say it stole a face," she whispered. "It goes on at night. It calls like someone who remembers being burned."

Neighbors repeated what they'd seen — "Hear it after the raid — like someone scraping the walls" — and Mercy catalogued them into the kinds of things that could be soot and rumor. She let the crowd's words settle like sediment, then ran her own small tests. She held the wrapped sliver of glass to the light; no face lingered on it, but the metal taste in her mouth flared as if the sliver had nicked something under her tongue. She pressed the folded red talisman against the stone where the scorch lay and felt the talisman's paper thrum beneath her palm.

Something in the alley was bound to the City's wound; Mercy sensed the current much as a doctor senses fever. It was not merely a household grief. The impression ran like a thin, hot line through the alley's shared walls. The diagnostic rite gave her a reading she could not shelve as usual: this had stamina, an aftertaste that would not let the story close. The presence was louder than the casual haunt she'd patched before in kitchens or over laundry lines. It left a residue that tasted of old blood and old shouts, of silence not yet forgiven.

She straightened, closing the box with the same small ceremony she used to start and end every job. "I'll look into it," she told Mrs. Choi. She saw the woman's relief — a clean, bone-deep thing — and knew the promise had weight. Mercy did not say how deep she would go; promises had costs. The ritual had already charged something in her chest: memory as if put to a blade.

As Mercy stepped deeper into the spiral, past the line where the light thinned to the color of old paper, she felt the alley press close, as if the city's layers of rooms and losses were leaning in to listen. This was not the usual kind of wail. It answered to more than grief. It answered to things the City had kept quiet, and Mercy's work for once felt less like cleaning and more like reading a ledger that wanted payment.
