The ward smelled of attempt and failure: boiled rice turned sour, the sharp antiseptic edge of diluted iodine, and the heavy undertone of damp linen folded too long. Oil lamps fought the dim; a single barred window offered a strip of false daylight that made dust hang like confession. Mercy moved between curtained bays where cots had been plucked hastily and men’s blankets lay folded into neat, absent shapes. Everything here looked as if it had been pressed and hurried—sheets folded; instruments stacked; a basin left to dry with a rag thrown over it.

At the central table, a battered ledger sat under a clumsy weight of ration tins. Its cover was loose and the pages inside had the fatigue of being opened and closed in the dark. Mercy reached for it with the careful respect she gave objects that had been made to hold names. The ink had run in places and columns blurred into one another; some entries ended mid-line where a pen had been lifted in a hurry. One page had been torn away cleanly and the glue along the spine showed the ragged gap.

The hospital orderly stood nearby, shoulders bent like someone who had carried weights too long. His hands were callused and his voice came out like a thing that had been kept behind teeth. "We wrote what we could," he said, as if speaking the ledger’s title could unburden it. "Some nights there were too many. We buried in the side yard. Quiet. Quick. Not like a funeral—just… puttings away."

He spoke slowly, the sort of man who measured even rumor. Mercy did not press for dates. The orderly's eyes kept drifting to the barred window as if expecting patrols to glance up. "The book stops," he said, thumbing to a place where ink had been smudged into a gray. "Pages half full. Names gone blank. Sometimes a pen ran out. Sometimes a hand ran away with a page. I don't… I don't have the courage to scrape each name back out." He wrapped his fingers around a small tin and offered it to Mercy the way someone offers a bandage without the wound.

Mercy let the ledger speak with her hands. She flattened a page and traced with a fingertip where ink had been rubbed until letters had become ghosts. Near the torn spine she found a column with a smudge that might once have been a date and a list of names that ended in a blot. Beside it a narrow column of burial slips—thin rough paper—had been tucked in and then half-removed. She did not claim the slips as proof. They were suggestive: evidence of hurried accounting, not an inventory.

"You buried them?" she asked, the question small enough not to demand a narrative.

The orderly's eyes closed for the smallest beat. "We buried who we could. Not always with tags. Not always with people to say their names. Sometimes—people moved them," he said, and then let the pause stand. "Sometimes they were buried where no ledger would look."

Mercy could feel how the ward's work had been to hold bodies while the city tried to survive. The ledger’s blanked names and missing pages were not only administrative failings; they were a social practice of survival—erasure as protection. That very erasure, she understood with a quiet dread, made the kind of wound that fed her enemy. A ledger that stopped was a memory that had been allowed to die in silence, and where memory dies a revenant grows.

She did not perform a rite in the ward; this was a place for testimony and careful reading, not drama. Instead she took out a small notebook and copied the ledger's visible fragments—a transcription of a smudged line, the stub of a burial slip, a note that a page had been torn. Her hand moved in the practiced slowness of someone preserving paper rather than summoning spirits. The orderly watched, not unguarded but trusting the motion of someone who read as she worked.

"If you ask people…" the orderly said as she wrote, his voice low and practical. "Don't ask them to remember in accused ways. Ask them to tell what they saw. Offer tea. Offer a cigarette. Don't tell them what you want. They protect themselves with silence." His counsel was the kind someone who had seen how questions break people would give: a protocol of care.

Mercy left with the taste of iron at the back of her throat and a narrow transcribed strip of ledger pages between her fingers. The ward's dim light seemed to hold the shape of things left unsaid, and the knowledge that erasure had been practiced for survival sat on her shoulders like a weight. She had found suggestive evidence—smudged entries, a torn page, burial slips half-hidden—but not a verdict. The ledger could be read two ways: as protection and as wound. Either way, it fed the revenant.

Outside, the Walled City’s alleys smelled of frying oil and smoke again. Mercy pulled the ledger fragment from her palm and read the smudged line by lamplight. Behind her, through a cracked door, the orderly went back to his slow work, and the ward returned to its hush. Mercy walked toward the shrine with the strip of transcription folded in her palm and the promise to gather witnesses: names would be asked, but only with care. The spirit would be faced not by accusation but by a recorded, witnessed memory.
