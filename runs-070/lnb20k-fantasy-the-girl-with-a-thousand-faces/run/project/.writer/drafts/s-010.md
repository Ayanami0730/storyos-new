Alley 13 was thinner at dusk, its shadows long and honest. A small knot of neighbors had gathered where the laundry lines crossed and the tiles still held memory like a cold coin. Mercy walked in with her kit under her arm and the photograph folded in the inner pocket; the young refugee waited against the far wall, hands clenched, shoulders moving with small, anxious breaths.

He looked at her when she stopped. For a moment he was a boy with a child's distrust, and then some private thing in him gave way and he folded. He did not speak a story so much as let a sound out—first a swallowed sob, then a ragged exhale that seemed to empty months at once.

"I remember a smell," he said at last, voice small. "Rice, and… smoke. And someone—someone called—" He broke off as if the mouth had been struck. His fingers found the photograph in Mercy's palm without asking and traced the faded face with a trembling fingertip. He pointed, not at a name but at a place in the picture's background: a wall, a doorway, a curve of cobble.

Mercy crouched beside him. The alley smelled of frying oil and stale incense; a child's shoe lay by the drain like an unfinished sentence. She laid the photograph flat on the tiles where the light was least harsh. "Show me with your hand," she said. "Where did it feel like then? Where were you standing?"

He put his hand over the image and then set a trembling finger to the tile beside the mat. He shuffled forward two steps and then a third, his feet making a small pattern in the dust as if retracing a path he had not wanted to name. The neighbors watched with the kind of close attention that serves as evidence; two of them—Neighbor Lei and a thin woman who had kept the communal kitchen's keys—stood a short distance off and nodded when Mercy asked them to bear witness.

Mercy took a scrap of chalk from her kit and drew a thin circle where his finger rested. She placed a pebble at the center as a marker, then opened a talisman and folded it into a small square, placing it against the pebble as a boundary. She sketched the outline of a doorway where the refugee had indicated and added a short arrow to show the direction he said people had moved. Each mark she made she spoke aloud and asked the witnesses to repeat: "This is where he stood. This is where people moved. Do you see this?" Their quiet affirmations stitched the act into record.

As they marked the ground, the alley answered with a tightening. A laundry line above them twitched though no wind moved it; a single pan in a nearby stall rang thin and long, and a child's voice—someone's memory—exhaled a note that might have been a name. Mercy felt the high ringing at the edge of her hearing climb an octave and pull at the edges of her speech. Her throat tightened the way it had on the rooftop; she steadied herself against the low wall and let the sound wash through.

The refugee's breath came in short, sharp gulps. He closed his eyes and whispered a fragment—no clear name, only the shape of a command and a child's hand reaching—and then he made a small gesture that landed them at the ruined stairwell by the yard. Mercy drew a quick sketch in her notebook: the pebble, the chalk circle, the arrow toward the stairwell. She wrote the refugee's gesture as a line—"pointed t'ward stairwell"—and initialled it in the margin. The witnesses repeated the placement out loud, two voices like a covenant.

Mercy did not perform a cleansing rite there; this was a mapping of witness, not a confrontation of ghosts. She did, however, set a low, diagnostic naming in the air—a whisper of smoke from a single, folded talisman held near the pebble—just enough to see whether the spirit tightened around the mark. The effect was immediate and local: the laundry line that had twitched strained again and a cold ripple moved along the tiles toward the pebble.

The spirit's reaction was sharper than before: not a general swell but a focused compression, a dozen voices stacking into one pointed accusation that seemed to hover over the pebble. Mercy felt the pressure like a hand at her sternum, and the ringing in her ears thinned into a single, higher note. She held the photograph closer to the light, noting how the image's background seemed to align with the chalk doorway she'd drawn.

When the refugee opened his eyes, they were wet and clearer. Mercy put a hand lightly on his shoulder—an ordinary, human touch—and said, quietly, "We map it. We do not name to accuse. We will go to the stairwell and see what traces remain, but only with witnesses and care." Her voice caught on the last word; the ritual toll tugged at the edges of her composure, but she kept the instruction steady.

He nodded, the motion small and frightened, and a neighbor stepped forward to speak: "I'll stand at that point and bear witness. We'll say what we saw; we won't accuse. We'll keep it honest."

Mercy folded the photograph back into her palm and slipped it, carefully, into the kit's inner pocket. She tucked the small sketch into the notebook and closed it with a clip. The mapping had been a ritual of witness: marks made, bodies placed, and neighbors sworn to remember what a boy's finger could not yet name aloud.

They agreed to meet at the ruined stairwell at first light, two witnesses and Mercy and the refugee, to look for traces the refugee's gesture had suggested. Mercy felt the spirit narrow its attention toward that stairwell as if the map had given it a sharper face to name. The alley's wail rolled in the distance and folded into a dozen small sounds—pots, footsteps, the whisper of cloth. Mercy tightened her grip on the kit's strap; the taste of iron lingered at the back of her throat. They walked away with the chalk circle still fresh on the tile, a recorded mark and a promise to return.
