They chose a low parapet that looked over the central courtyard and laid the mat there, paling the rooftop's crooked tile with a clean patch of cloth. A few potted plants scratched at the dark; laundry lines sighed between poles like nets. The city below breathed in muffled rhythms—footsteps, a distant radio cough—small sounds that made the rooftop feel unexpectedly exposed. Torches had been left deliberately low on neighboring roofs; someone had obeyed the Elders' counsel and kept light and noise tight.

Mercy set her woven case open on the mat and arranged what she needed without show. The dented censer went to the center like a heart; talismans were fanned in a loose ring, chalk drawn in faint, protective half-circles, and the little knife lay spine-up on the mat. She worked with the practiced economy of someone who knew how much light one could afford.

The Elder sat opposite her and watched the arrangement as if it were a small, fragile map. "We keep the witness list short," he said. "No drums, no open fires. If it answers, we stop. If it worsens, we pull back. We name to hold, not to unloose anger." His voice was the slow instrument everyone had learnt to listen to—measured, not command.

Mercy nodded and lit a pinch of powdered incense. The smoke rose thin and neat, and for a moment the rooftop was only lamp-glow and the pale column of scent. She murmured the low naming she used for probing, the kind that asked the air to speak rather than to obey. The motion was small: a tracing of the knife through smoke, a talisman pinched and held to the lip of the censer, a chalk breath painted into an incomplete boundary.

At first the rite gave the answers Mercy expected: a tremor in the air, a hint of voices folding at the city's edges. Then the sound thickened, and something in that thickening felt wrong. The voices braided, as before, but they began to pull at one another until they took form—breathy outlines that the corner of the eye mistook for hands or small heads. Shadows where there should have been none unhooked themselves from the parapet and leaned in like curious children.

A scent hit her—a scrap of damp cloth, boiled rice—and for a half-second a doorway slid under her vision: a threshold she could not name, a domestic shape that pressed like a palm against memory. It was not evidence; it was a sensation that fitted too closely to what a person keeps without naming. Mercy blinked and the image thinned; the provenance of the feeling was a gap, and she did not try to fill it. She only felt the familiar trade: a truth bought with a small theft from her own past.

The Elder's mouth tightened. "It makes itself into what people lack," he said quietly. "It will shape itself to the wound you bring. Be ready to let go."

They tried to hold the rite to measure—Mercy counted the breaths, the slips, the light of the lamp. But the voices became insistently physical. A breath caressed the nape of her neck like a child's fingers; pressure settled across her sternum as if someone were leaning into her ribs. At the same moment she felt the familiar toll that had come with other tests: a sharp prick behind her left eye and a tightening at the base of her throat. It pulled her composure thin; her voice came out higher and thinner than she intended, as if the ritual had borrowed steadiness from her.

The Elders' witness list tensed into motion. Neighbors watched from windows and doorways, children peered round shutters, and many stayed in place to see what would happen—exactly the pattern Mercy had been warned to preserve. A few gestures betrayed the night's danger: a shutter snapped closed with a hard click; a neighbor on a nearby roof retreated half a step and then drew back behind a low wall. The room's faces had not emptied, but the mood had shifted; watchers' attention tightened into an anxious thread.

On the mat, a talisman she had not set down fluttered as if fingers had taken it. The censer clattered once, then quieted. Mercy's knees trembled; the ache behind her eye and the tightening in her throat throbbed with a raw edge. The Elder reached out and pressed his palm lightly on her shoulder—a small human anchor in a night of spectral hands.

"We stop," he said. "Now. The cost is more than a bruise tonight." He helped her fold the talismans, his fingers steady and slow. "You paid—" he began, and then left the sentence unfinished, the unsaid accounting hanging between them.

Mercy wrapped the kit closed with hands that felt frayed at the edges. Her hearing carried the high ringing that had followed earlier probes; she tasted iron at the back of her throat and felt cautious in a way that was not merely professional. The rooftop that had felt private and shielded only minutes before now seemed thinly veiled, its privacy ripped back by the way the voices had wanted to be seen. Neighbors' footsteps moved below; a hush moved through the courtyard like a held breath.

She had not lost everything—memory was not a clean ledger—but the rite had taken a toll that left her less sure. The failure cost Mercy more than a tally; it cost communal confidence in the small way a neighbor's snapped shutter costs trust. When she and the Elder descended, some faces in the stair went taut with worry; a woman who had left the roof did not return to watch another rite, retreating instead behind a closed door.

Outside, the city's ordinary noises resumed but with a thinner tone, as if everyone had learned to listen for the wrong thing. Mercy felt her certainty hollow a little; a ritual that used to mean a measured answer had become an event that could unmake parts of herself and leave the community less sure. The Elder's hand rested for a moment on the closed kit, a touch that acknowledged payment and danger.

Down in the dim courtyard, small groups whispered. Some neighbors said Mercy had overreached; others blamed the spirit's cunning. Mercy tightened her fingers on the kit's strap until the leather bit into her palm and felt, like an ember, the knowledge that the spirit had shifted a degree closer to a personal claim. It now answered to tokens and to the contour of Mercy's own unspoken memories. The rooftop had been a test; it had failed them all, and the price was paid in faith and a little of Mercy's certainty.
