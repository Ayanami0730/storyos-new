The central courtyard had been hushed and steadied into a theater of memory. Low lamps guttered; torches were kept tight at the rims of the tiers. The inner ring held witnesses—two from each household whose pebbles had been placed in the alley—faces set to a duty that tasted like salt. The Elder sat near the battered scroll, packet folded, his hands folded as if in prayer. Mercy stood by the low altar, the locket and the folded cloth wrapped and placed at the center on a clean cloth; her kit lay open with its talismans face-down.

Mercy moved through the list she had practised in a thousand small diagnostics: powdered incense in the dented censer, offerings of rice and tea in small bowls, talismans kept folded until names were corroborated. She spoke the rules aloud so the sound would be a public contract: witnesses repeat placements, each memory is given with two witnesses, the Elder reads a fragment to set the scope, offerings are placed for what cannot be named. The sound of her voice—steady, thin—was a cord that braided the crowd together.

The Elder pried a page and read the fragment that had drawn them here: a smudged notation that repeated in different hands—"moved to side-yard," "placed, not named,"—and a margin where ink had been scraped away until the paper was raw. He did not pronounce it as accusation; he let the fragments sit like stones between people.

One by one the households came forward. The ritual was slow and careful: a witness spoke what they had seen—sensory phrases, gestures—placed a pebble at the altar, repeated the placement aloud and initialled Mercy's ledger. No speaker named a living person; the point was not to accuse but to make absence visible.

When it was the refugee's turn he stepped to the inner ring and spoke, small and clear, of the east step and the child's hand he had held. The witness who had stood with him repeated his words. Mercy touched the closed talisman to the smoke and watched the way the censer's breath rose around the photograph and the pebble they'd brought. The spirit tightened in response: a chorus of layered voices rose, a gust of cold swept through the courtyard, and for a heartbeat the sky above seemed to swell with the city's old panic.

Voices overlapped until the air itself sounded like accusation. A woman who had been there the night read aloud a fragment she had kept—how lights had been put out, how doors had been braced. A neighbor who had hidden a blanket in a stairwell placed his pebble and said, "We covered them," his voice small enough that the act of saying it felt like unbuttoning a wound. Each declared fragment was witnessed and recorded.

Then the spirit struck. It was not a single blow but a pressure that made the stones sing: a sound like falling boards, a dozen small thuds that rolled across the courtyard; a wash of images—sepia flashes of hands and faces—flicked at the edges of the witnesses' sight. Whispers crowded into sharper syllables that seemed to name absence itself. A lantern blew and the bow of the altar rocked where the offerings had been laid.

The community trembled. Some stepped back, mouths clamped; others pressed forward because the act of stepping forward was now its own defense. Mercy felt the old accounting in her body—the sharp, private ache that had been the currency of her craft. She had warned them: naming would cost. It would be paid in a thing that could be counted and in a thing that could not.

She had prepared for the cost in the only way she could: by choosing to trade what the revenant loved to take—an absence—from her own chest. She drew from the kit a small folded scrap she'd kept for herself: a soft cloth wrapped around a token no one else had touched, a private relic she had meant never to show. She did not name the memory aloud; to name it would have been to remake it in public. Instead she took the token, held it to her palm, and said, so the crowd could hear but not the world beyond, "I give what I keep for myself. I give the small thing I hold to remember. Take it and be counted." She laid the token on the altar and let the folded talisman rest on it.

Then she spoke the memory into the air the way a midwife speaks the name of a child into a new life, but Mercy spoke to undo—short, sensory lines that refused proper nouns: a warm cup left by a bedside, a shadow on a low wall, the small pocket where a child kept a shoe. As she spoke, the token loosened in her mind like a thread being cut.

The cost took immediate form. Mercy felt a private detail—one she had often used to steady herself when bargaining with the city—slip like a coin from a palm. It sank away not like a lost object but like a small door closed: she remembered saying the memory aloud, but when she reached for the picture that had held it, there was a thin, sharp blank that had not been there before. Her spine clenched; the ringing she had carried since the rooftop rose. Her voice came back thin and altered.

The act was recorded: Mercy folded the empty cloth into the altar and the Elder noted the sacrifice aloud in the ritual's ledger. The communal witness protocol was followed—two witnesses at each name, pebble placements, offerings set to the side—and the scroll's fragment read again to tie the names into the paper's pattern of erasure.

The spirit's reaction was a single, changing sound. For a long moment the chorus screamed and the air filled with small reenacted fragments; then something in the howl thinned, as if the absence the spirit had fed upon had been matched by a voluntary absence. The fury did not vanish like a candle; it reorganised. Where there had been a seething chorus there was now a quieter presence: layered voices no longer seeking to erase but to be held—an uneasy mourner who would not feed on silence but would stay, named and tethered.

The courtyard exhaled. Some neighbors wept openly; others stood with faces like carved stone. A few shook their heads as if disbelieving the bargain they'd watched. The Elder closed the scroll and, with a voice that trembled, said, "It has changed. It is not gone, but it is held. We will keep witnesses and offerings. We will work to name what was left un-named."

Mercy felt the loss as a new hollow where a small private certainty had been. It would change how she worked—an instrument slightly altered. She had paid with a memory that had once been hers to steady a bargain for the many. The Young Refugee came to her side and put his hand over hers on the altar—solid, warm—and Mercy felt for the first time the trade's meaning: a memory lost was matched to a community's chance at not feeding the wound.

They left the courtyard exhausted. The spirit no longer roared but watched like someone seated in sorrow; its immediate threat had lessened, though the shape of loss remained. The Elder set the date for follow-up witnessings and offerings. Mercy closed her kit with hands that trembled not just from strain but because something she had relied on was no longer there. The cost had been paid, the ritual recorded, and the community would now begin the longer work of holding what the dead had been given back—a hard task with brittle hope at its center.
