The Walled City folded around them like a map being creased and uncreased. Mercy moved through the alleys with the practiced economy of someone who had learned which ledges would hold and which tiles would give; the refugee followed, fingers tightened on the fabric of his sleeve. The family photograph sat against Mercy's ribs inside her kit, a small, paler weight she could feel like a pulse.

At first the city was itself—humidity, frying oil, the distant cough of a radio—but as they crossed the narrow ways the air began to thicken into other times. Mercy saw it happen in slices: a tarpaulin became a sepia awning; a hawker's call overlapped with a sharper shouted order; a child's laugh folded into a thin, repeated cry. The walls flickered with overlays—market stalls that had once been, a stair that had borne different feet. It was the revenant's theatre: fragments of past and present pressed together.

The refugee stopped at a bend where Mercy had chalked once before. He looked at the tile and then at her as if asking permission to step into the film. "I can feel it thicker here," he said. "It presses like a hand—here—" He put his palm to the worn step where the alley tilted toward the yard, and closed his eyes.

Mercy crouched beside him. The alley smelled sudden and sharp: boiled rice beneath smoke, the tinny tang of old blood. She did not prod him with questions; she had learned that questions sometimes shuttered what wanted to be said. Instead she asked the practical, habitual things that turned gestures into maps. "Show me with your foot," she said. "Where did you stand? Which way did the people move?"

He moved as if moving gave him courage. First one step, then another, tracing a corridor no longer there. He planted his foot on a tile and pointed with the smallest, most certain motion: toward the ruined stairwell's east step, the same edge Mercy had studied with pebbles. "They came from the stair—no, they pushed toward the door here," he said, voice low and steady. "And someone dropped something—a crate, maybe—but it landed here, by this tile. The child—someone's hand—reached back for it."

Mercy set a scrap of chalk on the tile and drew a thin circle where his finger rested. She placed a pebble at the center as a marker, then reached into her kit and, with the practiced economy of her rites, pinched a folded talisman and laid it against the pebble as a boundary—kept closed, not opened. She set the photograph flat beside the pebble as a small compass. Then she asked the neighbor who had accompanied them to speak what he saw and to repeat the placement aloud, so the mark was not Mercy's private note but a witnessed fact.

The witness, Neighbor Lei, stepped forward and echoed the gesture, placing his own pebble on top of Mercy's and saying, "East step—crate—child's hand." His voice added a public weight; the act of placing the second pebble made the memory something the community could point to.

The spirit answered by tightening. The laundry line above them twitched twice, a pan sang thinly in a stall, and the air turned colder in a palpable thread between Mercy and the pebble mark. Voices stacked into a single, sharper phrase that skimmed the edge of accusation; Mercy felt the thin wire of ringing in her ears climb a notch. Her throat tightened at the memory's edge and she steadied herself on a breath, pressing a thumb back to the place behind one eye as if to hold the film from tearing.

The refugee's face changed under those sounds. The terror that had folded him into silence unknotted into something like agency—small, frightened, but true. He reached down and brushed the photograph's corner with a fingertip, then pushed his palm flat to the tile where he had stood. "That's it," he said, clearer. "They moved like that. I was holding her shoe."

The words were not a full story; they were a fragment: a child's shoe, a held hand, movement toward the stairwell. Mercy put her hand over his on the tile—ordinary and precise—and said, "We write this down. We ask witnesses. We test the step. We don't name others now. We record what you remember." Her touch cost her composure in a small way—the ritual's accounting—but it gave him steadiness.

The witness who had stepped forward tapped the pebble and repeated the placement aloud, and Mercy set a second small mark on the tile so the alley held the memory. She did not make a broad claim; she recorded the breadcrumb: east step, crate landed, child's hand reached, witness: Lei. The pebble would be the point they returned to with the Elder and the scroll.

She tested the step with the knife's spine and a folded talisman smoke—the economy of motion she'd used before. The smoke curled and the laundry line twitched as if acknowledging the placement. The overlay softened into a sensory wash: the child's hand, a shout, the smell of rice. Mercy wrote the short phrase into her notebook—brief, for cross-reference later—and initialled it.

They left the alley with the east step marked by a pebble and Mercy's map clipped; the city's overlays thinned, leaving ordinary sounds of cooking and footsteps. Mercy felt the spirit's attention tighten where the pebble lay, as if the revenant had been given a sharper face to name. At the lane's mouth she turned to the refugee and said, quietly, "We show this to the Elder and the scroll. We bring witnesses. We do not shout names. We hold what we have with care."

He nodded. The motion was small and solid; it was the first clear step of his that was not only fleeing. Mercy tightened her fingers on the kit's strap, tasting iron at the back of her throat and feeling the old ringing like a friend whose presence meant the work was only beginning. The pebble on the east step would be their breadcrumb: a place, a witness, and a reason to bring the scroll next.
