She had practiced the weight of the decision in the hours when the city around the island was nothing but a smear of smoke and doorframes: how it felt to fold a life into a ledger, to press one's name so it would not lift again.

The Great Hall smelled of polished wood and boiled tea. Late light came in through the high windows, falling in ruled bars across the benches where a hundred girls sat like a counted thing. Rue stood at the end of one bench, the packet of papers heavy in her hands, the Contract itself wrapped in waxed cloth. It was thinner than she had imagined and thicker than the promise in her head.

Across the room the Headmistress waited behind the Folio, her gloves folded calmly at her wrist. The Folio lay open on a lectern: the page was a page older than the Hall, browned at the edges and written in a hand that did not tire. A template was printed, the heading a tidy law of the house.

“Folio Entry — Name: __________________; Age: __; Date: ______; Intake: One hundred; Provenance (sold/placed/orphaned): __________; Signature: __________________; Witness: Headmistress / Scribe.”

Rue read the printed line the way one reads an account book: as if the blanks were already waiting to be filled. She had no number to write for age she would make herself a number out of the look the clerk gave her and the soft way the girl beside her kept folding the corner of her sleeve. At home the ledger had been smaller, a family book with a soft spine where debts were rounded to a sum and forgiveness was a pencilled margin. She could see the spine now, the cramped handwriting of someone who kept track of coughs and missed dinners as if they were the same thing.

The Contract's clause was quiet in the packet but not shy: ten years. The words sat like a coin at the bottom of a bowl — something to be counted and not to be touched. The Contract promised education and shelter and apprenticeship, the learning of trades and the promise of a place to stand after ten years. In the margins—near the witness line—a single cramped note had been made in an ink older than the paper. It read, in a hand that looked like the hands in the Ink Room had always used, tethering.

When the Headmistress spoke, she spoke the Academy’s sentence as if it were both a comfort and a ledger entry.

“Out of the hundred who enter each year, only three leave at the end of their ten-year term. The rest remain under the Academy's charge as is recorded.”

Her voice had no need for ornaments. It sat in the room the way the folio did: present, hard, final. The girls shifted. Rue felt the ledger of their breathing shift with them, a tide that counted itself in the quiet between words.

The Headmistress closed the folio with a practiced gentleness and read the ceremonial lines that would be ink and law.

“So entered under the charge of the Mercy Academy. Let the Folio stand as record and law. Let those within this house remember that mercy is accounted for by service and attention, not by indulgence.”

A scribe dipped the pen. Rue dipped her own hand into the little bowl of water that was meant to clear the nib, palms damp from the autumn cold on the ferry. She tasted metal on her tongue and thought of the ferryman's hat, of a coin sliding from fingers that did not want to part with it but had to. She did not write her age. She hesitated, feeling the shape of her life as if it were a ledger to be balanced: a small credit here, a small debt there. At home debts had a smell—musty and faintly medicinal—and the memory of that was what had put her in the Hall today.

When she set the pen to the Contract she wrote 'sold' in the space the Folio called Provenance. The nib slipped a little as if surprised by the honesty of the word. She signed her name with a hand that would make do.

The Binding Seal waited on the table, a mound of beeswax and a small iron signet beside a bottle that smelled of tincture. Even in the dry light she could tell it smelled of beeswax and a bitter iron tang. The scribe pressed the wax, the signet sank, and for a sliver of a second Rue felt as if a small chord had tightened around the place where her wrists would rest.

The Amber Bell was on the table, a warm piece of glass-grey amber held in a loop of brass. Someone — the Headmistress or the scribe, Rue could not see past the lectern — took it up. The sound it made was not bright so much as decisive. It cut across the room like a stamp.

"By amber and ink, so sealed," someone intoned, low enough that its syllables felt like a private thing. The bell answered, and its note spread: a single clear ring that seemed to set the page of the Folio into a new place.

Rue felt the weight of the signet at her back and the thinness of the money in her pocket. The Headmistress's hand hovered over the witness line and then pressed her glove to the Folio as if that touch itself were a closing entry in a book. The girls around Rue moved as if they had been measured and set; some smiled slightly, a nervous accounting of relief. A few did not move at all.

When the clerk folded the Contract away, tucking it in the same waxed cloth, Rue did not know whether she had chosen mercy or ledger. She only knew what she had traded: a name, a year, the small liberties of an unrecorded life. Her heel scraped the floor as she left the bench; the Hall kept going, businesslike and slow, the Folio closed like a chest.

Outside, the gardens smelled of late roses and salt. Rue walked toward the ferry with the packet safe against her ribs. The bell's note sat underneath everything she felt — a seal, a promise, a count that would always remember where to place her.

The list was already up when we came in: a narrow column of names pinned beneath the Folio’s page, the rest of the book gone for the afternoon as if it were too heavy for public eyes.

Light came in strips between high windows and fell on rows of narrow two-tier bunks, trunks shoved under the lower pallets as if storage and sleep had been married in haste. A plank floor muttered when you walked; voices moved around it like careful traffic. The dormitory smelled like the underside of a roof in rain and the sharp sour of too-white soap.

Loren was there at the doorway, a broad shape in a sober coat, palms folded over a sheaf of paper. He did not call names from the Folio; he watched the room with the slow economy of someone who measured a thing by what it made of itself. When a girl hesitated under the list he stepped forward and, without a smile, tapped the rota pinned beneath the board and said the word that set a place: “Garden.”

Rue had a place that night between two trunks and a girl who kept folding the corner of her sleeve until it was nervous and neat. The girl introduced herself as Aya with a grin that came quick and a little startled; she dropped her trunk on the lower bunk opposite and sat cross-legged as if she had practiced being comfortable in any small space. She kept one hand in her hem, palm wrapped around something dry and small; when she showed it a moment later it was only a crust of bread she had hidden like a private coin.

"Keep your head down and your hands busy," Aya said before Rue could find anything to ask. Her voice hopped the room like a coin. Small things about her—how she turned a question into a joke, the bright angle of her laugh—made Rue notice movement before lineaments. Aya's fingers worried the crust and then tucked it away again, as if saving both the bread and the gesture.

Supper came with the Bell's clear note. The ring cut across our chatter and, as always, words from the Hall followed—ceremony turned to schedule—and we moved like a practiced thing toward the long table. Food was ladled into tin plates and placed before us; the scrape of spoons and the sighs at the edges of the meal sounded like the work the house expected.

After, under the wash of lamplight, the head of dorm stepped into the middle of the room with a blanket folded into a tidy square. She held it up like a tutor showing a diagram and said, "Watch and learn—this keeps the bed from taking you in the night."

A girl near the foot of the bunks, fingers clumsy from travel, reached to copy the fold and the head of dorm took her hands between weathered palms, guiding each finger with a quick, correcting pressure. "Not so soft," the woman said, breath warm and faintly scented of starch. Her nails scraped the canvas of the blanket as she tucked and smoothed. "Pull the corner under, see? Snap the edge so it holds. You won't have a child crawling over you because you left a flabby corner." The girl tried again; the blanket snapped into a square under the woman's hands and a small laugh escaped her—embarrassed and proud at once.

The correction was small and private in its cruelty: the head's hand left a warmth where it had been and the girl sat straighter, fingers clean and more careful. Rue watched the scrape of the woman's nails and the way the light hit the new square. Discipline showed itself then not as a rule written in a book but as a practice applied to a body—fingers guided, a corner folded, a laugh curbed into habit.

When curfew came the dorm folded itself tight. The trunks shoved under the bunks like animals settling in. The board under the entry light showed our names in their neat column and gave Rue a particular, domestic kind of relief. The Bell rang and the room went small and immediate; the noise of the world beyond the garden walls was reduced to the soft hiss of someone breathing.

Aya leaned toward Rue across the narrow blanket and, without drama, said, "Don't trust the board. It tells what they want recorded. We tell each other the rest."

There was a story shared in the low corners of the bunks that night—Mara's story—about a girl who had tried to bargain out a rota and spent a week in the scullery for her trouble. The quiet that followed was not comfort but accounting.

Before sleep Rue felt the Folio as if it were a presence: a book that had been read and then closed, its list copied and put where we could see it. Someone had taken a thin list from the Folio and pinned it below the names; not the full book, only the day's intake transcribed into a narrow column. The Folio's presence was public enough for us to see it; the Archive would take the book later, the woman who posted the list had said, but we had the list.

Rue fell asleep with Aya's hand warm by her elbow and the Bell's clear note sitting under all that closeness like a seal. In the dark she thought of how the house counted things: work, witness, record. It felt precise and heavy and, even then, like something you could trade a day of yourself for and never quite buy back.

Loren's classroom sat where the workshops did: an honest room of a repaired floor and benches that had belonged to other hands before ours. The air smelled of cut wood and iron, and the needle's thumb felt like a metronome tapping the lesson into your palm.

He put a length of rough cloth before Rue and said, without ceremony, "Pull tighter; the seam keeps its promise if you keep your edge." The words were dry as a rule—no sermon, just a sentence that told you what the trade expected. Rue bent her head and let her fingers learn the rhythm: needle in, thread through, pull until the cloth lay like a promise kept.

Loren moved between the benches with the slow, patient economy of someone used to making shortcuts into habits. His hand found Rue's wrist once to turn her stitch, the motion small and correcting. "Not from the elbow," he said, tapping the joint with the blunt of his thumb. "From here." He tapped the base of her palm and the sound of wood on wood punctuated the point. The bench clacked when someone shifted and the room held the noise like a ledger taking an entry.

By the time the lesson changed to the gardens, Rue could count stitches the way she had once counted lop-sided coins: careful, automatic. The gardens were a patchwork of taught beds and wild hedgerows where Loren liked to test patience as much as skill. He showed them how to prune a rose so that it would not take the whole branch—a small, decisive cut that demanded attention to the plant's lean.

"Measure twice, cut once," Loren said, the phrase bringing a boyhood proverb into these beds. He handed Rue a small knife with a worn handle and had her find the right angle, watching the way her jaw set when she decided. "You make that cut as if you're writing into a ledger; you mean it."

They walked the beds with rows of students trailing like a counted thing. Some learned quickly; others learned the lesson of patience by breaking a bud or misplacing a branch and being made to rebind the trellis for an hour. Rue felt the pattern of rules braid into the work: the bell would call lessons and meals, the rota would ask for garden shifts, and every measured cut came with the whisper of consequence.

The Ink Room was not a classroom so much as a thing the house kept in its throat. When they led us there the door was a slab below the library's stacks, heavy and unadorned. Loren did not open it; a scribe's hand, quick and exact, showed us the glass along the inner case where the Binding Seal rested.

Behind the glass the seal looked like a small, round relic, a mound of beeswax capped by a dark iron signet. Rue remembered the smell more than the sight—something of beeswax and a bitter iron tang rose up from her memory of signing. The scribe's finger tapped the display with a care that felt like punctuation; no one touched the seal. Loren's voice, low, said only what could be said: "You see its order. You do not lift the seal."

Stories about the Three were told to us as if they were both threat and promise. In the long hours in the benches and the beds, they came up like smoke: Elaine's name said as a small, astonished hope—she left and carried a scar that meant she had paid; Maris mentioned as a tidy example, the kind the House liked to point at; Sal's name dropped like a cool stone meant to be avoided: leave unasked and the Academy will not be kind.

They were a mantra and a warning in different mouths. One girl said, almost reverent, "Elaine came back to show how it might be done." Another rolled her eyes and said, "Maris is a pamphlet; they've sold her story well enough." A boyish mimic in the corner made Sal's departure into a gesture of secrecy and the room swallowed the laugh that followed it. The Three meant more in the telling than in any fact: miracle, commodity, closed mouth.

In the afternoons, Rue practiced stitches until her hands were small machines. She mended hems and learned the economy of a neat seam: how a stitch could hold a pocket for a coin and how an extra thread could be the difference between a trade well kept and a trade that cost you a reprimand. Sometimes she would sew with one eye on the Ink Room door, imagining the scribe's neat hand and the seal behind the glass. Each time she threaded her needle she felt the ledger close a fraction tighter.

There were compromises that started as small private things. Once, while binding a scrap for the apprentices' chest, Rue let another girl's thread stand because the other girl had been late to class and begged—Rue's hands moved to hide the omission in the seam and, when Loren passed, he gave her a look that held both instruction and calculation. He did not scold; he slid a marked tally into the others' ledger with a pen whose scratch she could not see. Rue felt the quick weight of owing.

So the lessons kept to their trades and to their counts. The work taught skill; the house taught measurement. Rue learned both: to pull the seam tight, to choose the cut that would not demand a second one, to fold a ledger's worth of small favors into the margin of a day. By the time the sun left the gardens and the benches cooled, she knew the rhythm of the place—how a life could be practiced into order, how giving a stitch spared a reprimand, how every small kindness could be recorded as a debt.

Walking back through the courtyard with her palms smelling faintly of sap and stitch, Rue thought of the seal behind glass and of the Three's stories braided into the beds. The Academy taught its girls to measure things precisely: angles and seams, work and witness. It also taught them to measure what they were willing to lose.

She kept her hands busy and, as always, the ledger watched.

Rue kept the family ledger folded beneath the mattress of the ferry bed for a week after she left. At night she would open it by lamplight and trace the cramped lines, the pencilled sums and the pencilled coughs. At home the ledger had held everything that was small and urgent: a debt counted as if it were weather, a promise pencilled in the margin because it made the other debts easier to bear.

That was why she had written "sold" in the Provenance field, though she had thought the word like a coin you could not feel until it had gone. She had promised, not with a seal but with a whisper beside her mother's pillow, to make the numbers stop climbing. She had not said how many coins would be needed or the hours that would be taken; she only said the one practical thing that would move the sum: "I will stand where there is a roof."

Aya laughed at that promise the first night: the sound was too quick to be sad. "You sell yourself and call it a plan," she said, tucking the crust of bread deeper into the hem of her skirt as if hiding a small, private covenant. Rue had no answer that was not a hand-shaped accounting of the cost—just a look that meant she had chosen the roof and would pay its price in stitches and hours.

Kindness was small there. One morning, when Aya's hands trembled from a fever she would not admit, the head of dorm—herself a woman with palms like worked leather and a voice that did not rise—came in and sat on Aya's bunk. She took Aya's wrist, felt the pulse, and smoothed the hair away from her brow. "Wrap your scarf tighter," she said, and then pinned an excusal slip to Aya's trunk: a thin parchment stamped with the dormmistress's cipher. It was an official exemption that would keep Aya from the garden rota that afternoon but note an extra wash shift to be made up at week's end. The gesture was quick and official; it had the taste of mercy measured in chores.

Micro-violence answered kindness like a shadow. The board under the lamp displayed names and small symbols; when a girl left a pan unclean the symbol appeared by her name and a note would be pinned to her trunk: a week in the scullery, an extra hour at the boil. That afternoon a paper slip appeared on Mara's trunk—one hand had set it there with the same economy as a ledger entry—and Mara's face went flat as if a coin had been taken.

Rue mended that afternoon, and stealing small kindnesses was a part of the trade. She took Aya's crust one evening and folded it into a napkin, boiling water to warm it and pressing it between two clean boards until the crust softened. "Share," Rue said, handing the napkin over. Aya's grin was a fold of relief; she pressed the softened bread into Rue's palm like repayment for the touch of warmth.

Loren moved through these intimacies with a quiet that was not pity but calculation. He came to Rue's bench and, without a word, set a small scrap of cloth before her—an old piece from the teachers' basket, a mending the house had set aside. He slit a neat mark with his thumb where the stitch should start and tapped the spot with a blunt finger. "Keep your edge," he murmured. "Make it hold."

His mentorship was not a gift of sentiment; it was a counting of possibility. When Rue hesitated on a stitch because she was thinking of the ledger, he touched her wrist and guided the needle. The motion was finite and firm, and the correction carried both instruction and a tacit accounting: the favor had been noticed and catalogued. Later, when she tucked a seam of another girl's apron so the fabric would not gape in the sink, Loren gave her no praise, only the half-smile of someone who had marked the tally down in a private column.

At night they told stories in the narrow sleep between the bell's last call and the first cough from beyond the gardens. One girl leaned forward and said, "Elaine left—came back with a scar, they say," the name turned into a small, astonished hope that made a girl on the far bench clutch her blanket tighter. Another, bored and practised, rolled her eyes and muttered, "Maris is their example; they've polished her story into a pamphlet." A third made Sal's name into a quick, secret gesture and the room swallowed the laugh that tried to follow. Rue felt her fingers tighten on the ledger beneath her pillow and glanced once at the pencilled sums as if to count what her choice had cost.

Rue thought of the promise beside her mother's bed and the way it had already changed her. She had traded the small freedoms of being uncounted for a place that would teach her how to count and how to be counted. The house taught its girls to be useful: how to make a seam hold, how to prune a rose so it did not waste itself, how to set a pot so the water would not boil over. It taught them, equally, the language of debt: the way a marked tally could be slid across a table and the world would look smaller for it.

One evening as the sun lowered beyond the glasshouses, Aya pressed her palm into Rue's and, without ceremony, said, "They think they have us counted. But we can count each other." She held out the crust in a palm made soft by steam and mending and Rue understood the ledger had another column: loyalty.

Rue's answer was practical; she folded the bread in half and handed it back, careful as if returning a coin. "Then let's start an account," she said, and the words were small and absolute.

She kept her hands busy, not to forget, but to make sure that when the ledger looked back, there would be more than one kind of entry written there.

The Headmistress received Rue where the Hall narrowed toward the lectern, a place that always smelled faintly of old varnish and ink. The benches had been pushed back; only the Folio and the amber bell sat between them, a small island of ceremony in an otherwise empty room.

She stood when Rue arrived, gloves folded at her wrist, and offered a greeting that was all surface polish. "Rue," she said, "sit. We must speak plainly about what the Academy promises and what it asks in return."

The Headmistress's voice was the voice the town heard at the Folio: steady, public, untroubled by the particular. She unfolded the Academy's case in sentences that could have been typed into a pamphlet: rescue from hunger and danger, the conversion of debt into apprenticeship, the shelter of a roof and the promise of a trade. "We take the unsettled and make them steady," she said. "We teach; we provide; we expect service in return."

She touched the Folio as if to make the words physical. The ledger was closed, its leather worn; the amber bell sat like a punctuation on the lectern. "So entered under the charge of the Mercy Academy," she quoted, the ceremonial language finding its place in the room, "Let the Folio stand as record and law. Let those within this house remember that mercy is accounted for by service and attention, not by indulgence."

Rue listened the way she had learned to listen in the classrooms—quiet, counting the gaps between the sentences. The Headmistress's phrasing had a tidy moral arithmetic to it: charity for work, shelter for measure. It sounded, in the empty Hall, like a ledger balanced in public.

After the public lines the Headmistress smiled and folded a different hand: the calculation beneath the gloss. "The Contract's clause is simple in form: ten years," she said, tapping a finger against the binding of a paper packet on the lectern. "On its face we promise education, shelter, apprenticeship. In the margins, we record the particulars that make those promises practicable: orders of apprenticeship, sponsorships, and the logs the house keeps to see a term through." She did not read a margin; she only named it as if naming settled the fact.

Rue's mouth shaped a question and did not speak it. She thought of the sealed wax she had felt when she signed; the smell of beeswax had come back to her in the Hall like a small, unpleasant memory. The Headmistress noted the silence and, with the same polite economy, addressed the unasked concern.

"We are accountable to those who fund and to those who live under our care," she said. "The Binding Seal is not theatre. It makes claims enforceable when we must ensure a promise is kept. But enforcement is not a wolf at the door; it is a tool of reliability. We do not seek to make prisoners—rather, we seek to make citizens of trade and duty."

The words hung in the Hall like a set of scales. To the assembled public the sentence read as reassurance; to Rue the sentence read also as policy. There was a difference, a thinness of brightness: the Headmistress spoke of tools and reliability, and Rue heard the implied cost where a tool became tally.

She tried another angle. "How often is the Folio consulted for departures?" she asked, keeping her voice low so the question would not become a petition in the public hearing.

The Headmistress straightened and folded her hands. "The Folio is our record. Its pages are consulted when the house's obligations are complete and when there is cause for review. Departures are not casual things; they are accounted for and recorded when they are rightly earned."

There was a softness in the words that suggested process, a quiet insistence that the house's work was administrative and orderly. But Rue had seen the people's ledger at home, and she knew how a neat process can take a night's mercy and parcel it into a year's account.

When Rue rose to leave the Headmistress's hand closed on the Folio's edge, the fingers briefly covering the page as if to guard what was written there. The Headmistress offered Rue a small, plain benediction—"Learn; keep faith with the work; be of use"—and Rue took it the way one takes a receipt: practical, necessary, and not an answer.

Outside the Hall the gardens smelled of rose and sea. Rue walked away with the Headmistress's sentences turning under her tongue—rescue, apprenticeship, shelter—and with the memory of the Binding Seal's smell prickling at the back of her throat. The Hall had offered a public scripture and a private ledger; Rue felt the two hands of the place closing around the same accounting and left with more questions than she had when she entered.

The Hall had been dressed for ceremony: tapers in iron sconces, banners looped along the rafters, and a band of patrons in muted cloaks gathered where the benches broke for the lectern. The hundred names were not all present in person—the intake was a year complete now—but the room counted them with a single gesture: a long ledger closed on the lectern, its leather dulled and the Folio's page turned like a breath.

The Headmistress stood at the lectern with the amber bell beside her, a small, bright thing on a field of polished wood. A hush fell like a hand when she spoke; her voice did what it always did—smoothed the rough edges of debt and need into policy and purpose. "We mark the year by the work done and the hands taught," she said. "One hundred entered this household this term; they leave different by trade and by duty."

Praise and patronage moved through the room in neat phrases. A patron read a short commendation—charity turned to apprenticeship, coin turned into craft—and the benches responded with measured applause. Somewhere, a woman murmured Elaine's name the way people murmur of distant miracles; someone else lifted a hand in a quiet sign to Maris's tidy example; Sal's name hovered in the air like a warning and the room took it in without exclamation.

When the formal words closed, the Headmistress laid a hand on the Folio's binding and gave a slight, official nod. A scribe—small, quick-fingered—stepped forward and unlatched a clasp. "The ledger for this intake is closed and copied," the scribe announced in a voice linen-thin with formality. He ran a thumb along the edge of the page, and for all the room's music the action felt like a seam being finished. "A thin list will be kept for public record; the full Folio will be carried to the Archive for safekeeping."

Before the applause could rise, the Headmistress lifted her chin and gave the Hall a line that sat the myth beside the praise. "Across a decade," she said, voice crisp in the hush, "only three have been recorded as departing after ten years—Elaine, Maris, and Sal. Let their names stand in the Folio and in our memory as both testament and caution."

A bell rang—clear, decisive. Whether the Headmistress had lifted it herself or some other hand made it speak did not matter in that moment; the ringing set the Hall's hours into a firm place. The notes cut the air and the guests stood like ledgers balanced. The patron ceremony folded into smaller salutations and the murmuring thinned to business.

Loren found Rue at the edge of the benches, needle stilled in her palm as the world slid back into ordinary rules. He put a hand on her shoulder with the easy authority of someone who had always been the first to call a name. "The house will want apprentices assigned by sundown," he said, voice low. "Come to the registry table. You'll take three; make them hold the work."

At the long table, a scribe had unrolled a narrow log—its columns already filled with shifted names and notes. Loren tapped a column with a pen like a baton. "We take the new apprentices from the intake list and pair them with working benches," he said. "Sponsorship is noted in the logs; training hours recorded."

Rue watched as Loren called names and the scribe wrote them in a neat hand. The ritual was small and administrative: a name read, a nod, a pen scratch, a folded slip pinned to a trunk to mark where the apprentice would sleep. Rue felt the apparatus of the house tighten around her—reports, sponsorships, tallies—made visible in ink and slip.

When her turn came she stood at the table and took the slips offered: three folded pieces of paper, each named and numbered. They were anonymous in the way of registers—no grand introductions, only a shorthand that said: learn, serve, be counted. She folded her fingers around the paper and felt the weight like a coin.

Loren leaned close and, as he had a thousand times before in a workshop, set her hands on the first slip and showed her how to smooth the crease that would make the name a working thing. "Pull the stitching taught in the morning; make the seam hold so they can carry a pocket for a coin," he murmured. The instruction was both craft and custody: teach skill, take responsibility.

Rue walked the benches that afternoon with Loren at her elbow, the slips tucked into her apron like small ledgers. She met three girls in the sunlight that came through the high windows: a thin girl who kept her thumb inched into the hem of her sleeve, a broad-shouldered one whose hands were already callused, and a quiet one who watched with the slow calculation of a ledger. They did not speak much; names and work did the introducing. Rue thought of the ledger at home and the pages she had closed for a roof.

She set the girls to their benches, showed them the angle of a cut or the tension of a stitch, and marked their first hours in the house log with a neat pen stroke. Each time she wrote a name she felt a small tally accrue in the house's account and, with it, in her own. Apprenticeship was given as a trade; it was counted as an obligation.

By dusk, as the Hall emptied and candles guttered, Rue sat with the slips—three little folded ledgers of beginning—and felt the double edge of her work. The year's ledger had closed; the Folio would be carried away. She had, in the same movement, been entrusted with other names to hold and teach. The bell's note still seemed lodged under her ribs.

Loren's hand came to her shoulder once more. "Keep careful ledgers, Rue," he said. "Teach well. The house notices both."

She folded the final slip into the inside of her palm as if tucking away a coin. For the first time she understood how a life inside the Academy could become not only a way to be counted but a way to count others. She left the Hall with the slips at her breast and the Folio's closed leather heavy in her mouth of memory—an accounting that had closed one book and opened three more.

The Ink Room smelled of old paper and iron—a smell Rue had come to know as the house’s private breath. The door was a slab below the library; even in the bright hour the light leaned away from the cases. An attendant sat at the long table, sleeves rolled, eyes like a bird's—quick and watchful. The Folio was under glass on a lectern in the center, its binding banded and its page splayed like the cheek of a careful thing.

Rue had not meant to come that day. She had only meant to check a name on the thin list against a trunk slip and found, instead, the slip pinned to a trunk whose number did not appear on the public column. The slip was neat, folded, a folded knowledge that had not been transcribed. That sort of absence was a hole in a ledger; holes collected in Rue's mind the way rain filled hollows.

She told the attendant. "May I—" she began, and the attendant's hand was already moving toward a tray where a small iron key lay, stamped dark with a crest. The key caught the light like a small coin. He did not look at her, only lifted it and said, "You'll need to sign for it."

They made the little ritual of sign-out beneath the lamp: a name on a page, a careful loop of ink that said who took the key, and the hour in a marginal column that might as well have been a hinge. Rue's name looked small among the clerical loops. The Archive Key rested in her palm as if it belonged there now, its iron cold and definite.

The attendant unlatched the cabinet with a practiced twist. The air that escaped smelled of dust and varnish and something that might have been the memory of wax. Rue watched the clasp give and the cabinet open on the slow hinge of habit. Hands that had known many such openings reached in and, with a precise motion, drew the Folio free.

On the page something had been altered. It was the sort of thing the archives taught you to feel before you read: ink density like tide marks, a careful crossing-out in a column where names should be even, and a faint, scoured smear at the margin as if someone had tried to breathe an erasure into being. There was also a smaller, cramped addition near the witness line—too small to read at a glance, written in a hand that crowded the letters like something hiding.

The attendant closed the cabinet door and watched Rue watch the page. "Discrepancies happen," he said, fingers steepled. "Scribes have their hands—different inks, different days. We note them and we note why."

"Why is a slip pinned to a trunk but absent from the intake column?" Rue asked, and the attendant's quick face smoothed into a practicable quiet. "Perhaps it was misfiled," he said. "Perhaps it was noted elsewhere. The house has logs."

That answer was the same pattern Rue had seen before: a naming of procedures without a showing of margins. It fit exactly where it should: explanation by management rather than disclosure. She felt an edge of something thin and metallic in her chest, the shape of the Binding Seal's memory when she had leaned to sign.

She leaned closer to the Folio and, as if the book were a person, she read the page for the feeling of it. Her fingers hovered but did not touch; the rules were still rules, even when a hole wants to be filled. The crossing-out was not a neat correction but a heavy scrawl, hurried at some later hour; the erasure at the margin looked as if someone had tried to scrape the ink back into air. The cramped note near the witness line sat like a swallowed word.

The attendant shrugged with both shoulders. "If you have concerns, speak with the Headmistress. She hears these things when they are given importance. We keep the record here in the Ink Room; we do not adjudicate."

Rue found the deflection like a hand brushing dust from a ledger page. "I will speak with her," she said.

The Headmistress received Rue in the corridor off the Hall with the practiced ease of someone used to being the country's public margin. Her gloves were folded at her wrist; her voice was the same placid instrument Rue had heard before. Rue showed her the slip and described the crossed entry and the faint scouring.

The Headmistress's mouth tightened, a small, quick measure. She touched the Folio as if to make the book's weight real and said, "We maintain our records with care. Sometimes a later hand clarifies an old line; sometimes a note is compressed into a margin for administrative reasons. These are not, in themselves, conspiracies."

"Then why is the slip missing from the intake list? Why is there an old crossing where a departure should be?" Rue asked, meaning the questions to be simple and precise.

The Headmistress, with the calm of the public scripture she had practiced, named the house's mechanism instead of showing it. "The Folio is consulted when obligations run their course. The Ten-Year term is a crucible, Rue. Those who reach its end are rare because the house's work is not casual; it is a shaping. Records are kept and reviewed; departures are accounted for when there is cause for them."

The way she said the last words—'not casual'—felt like the closing of a ledger to Rue. The Ten-Year period had been a number before; the Headmistress made it a test framed in the language of duty and craft. Rue heard the sentence as both explanation and closing.

She left the Ink Room with the Archive Key returned to its velvet tray and her questions heavier than the iron in the key. The attendant slid the register closed with a soft scrape. The crossing-out sat on the Folio page like a healed scar; the cramped, small addition in the margin remained unreadable to her still. That small, confusing line folded into Rue's mind with the weight of a promise that had not yet been paid.

Outside, the gardens smelled of salt and clipped hedges. Rue felt the hollow of a ledger in her chest and the new, sharp instinct to watch the pages that had been set aside. The Ten-Year crucible, she realised, was not just a term in ink; it was a pressure that could be read in erased margins and in the way a house called mercy by tallying service. She would count, and she would be counted.

Rue went back to the Ink Room as if she were returning to a wound. The light under the library's stacks was low and the cases threw long teeth of shadow. Loren met her at the door with a bench of cloth and didn't ask what she wanted; he only set a lamp and a tray of papers between them, the old ease of two hands who had mended the same hole before.

She had signed for the Archive Key before she came—an inked loop in the register, the hour written like a hinge—and the key had felt cold and final in her hand. Now she eased the leather packet open and the smell found her first: beeswax and iron and a bitter tang that the memory of signing had kept like a bruise. The Binding Seal was not here; but the page she had unfolded sat like a throat open to speak.

The Contract itself was older than the packet she had first signed; the paper was thin in places and the margin had been crowded with hands that had tried to add notes without making a show. Rue ran her thumb along the witness line and found a tucked half-clause: a strip that had been folded into the seam and then stitched over as if to hide it in plain sight.

She eased it free. The letters were cramped and the ink had pooled in tiny rivers where a later hand had gone back and tried to scrape the stroke. In the seam she could make out a single, breath-sized phrase—one word pressed into the margin like a thumbprint.

"tethering."

It was not a sentence; it had no ritual words attached, no syndicate of verbs that explained how it worked. But the word sat against the witness line like an instruction and the margins had the small earnestness of a thing that had been argued for and then whispered into place.

Rue felt the old willing self in her chest, the young woman who had put pen to paper without knowing the weight of a particular word. She remembered the lamp's light then, the way the wax had pooled under the signet and the way her hand had smelled after she pressed her name. She had signed willingly; she had believed in the roof and the trade and in the promise that a counted life could be steadier than the one she left behind.

But the seam and the cramped word made that willingness look smaller, like a coin pressed flat in a palm. The Binding Seal's memory rose in her mouth—beeswax and a bitter iron tang—so familiar she could taste the room where she had signed. If the word meant that there was a tether, then what did the tether take? The books said nothing. The margins had been crowded and then smudged as if someone had changed their mind.

"You found it," Loren said, not looking up from the tray where he was smoothing a scrap. His voice was small and steady, a thread that ran down the bench.

"One word," Rue said. "Tethering." She let the syllable sit between them. "Near the witness line. Folded into the seam."

Loren's fingers stilled for a second; he did not take the packet from her. "Margins hold a lot," he said carefully. "Notes, sponsors, conditions. Sometimes a phrasing is left that looks smaller in a book than it operates in practice."

"Does it bind?" Rue asked.

He looked at her then—Loren's face weathered in a way that did not entirely hide the private calculation beneath. "It ties people to an obligation of service and craft," he said, the answer a pared-down fact and not a ritual. "What it costs depends on what the house logs against a name. Often it's time. Often it's trade. It is rarely what someone thinks when they sign: a neat exchange of bread for hours."

"So it takes something else," Rue said.

"Everything has a ledger," Loren said. "We count craft, we count hours, we count the work you do. The binding makes the counts legible. That's all I can say that won't be a lecture."

He might have meant it as mercy; Rue heard it as the house explaining itself with the blunt tools it had—measurement and record. She had come looking for a liturgy and found arithmetic instead: a hand that rendered decisions into tallies.

She thought of the Three then—the names the Hall had set in a public sentence—and of the small, cramped line in the seam that had no flourish but carried force. The Ten-Year span that had seemed a number at the Folio now sounded like a pressure: time pressed into a shape until someone fit its opening.

Rue folded the strip back into the seam the way she had found it, not to hide, but so the line of the page would hold together. She did not want to tear the paper; she wanted the book whole enough to be read again.

Loren slid a scrap across the table and the lamp made the ink shine. "Be careful with what you ask the books to show you," he said. "They will give you facts, but they will not give you remedies."

She left the Ink Room carrying the packet like an accusation. Outside, the gardens smelled of cut roses and distant sea. The Binding Seal's scent hovered in memory and the word tethering followed her like a small, precise footstep. She had signed in hope and now she understood the shape of what hope had cost: not only hours and apprenticeship, but the way a name could be counted into a house's account. The moral fog that had softened her first choice thickened; she would have to reckon with the cost of a tether she had agreed to without seeing its full length.

Years folded like new hems under Rue's hands. The montage began in the small seams of ordinary days: a bench that creaked the same, a lamp that burned low in the same corner, a slip of paper folded and handed across a table until the motion became a rite.

Loren's hand found wrists the way it always had, but his hair silvered at the temples and the river of his teaching voice ran a touch slower. He still tapped a thumb at the base of a palm—"From here"—and a new apprentice's elbow stiffened into the proper seam of a stitch. Once, as he showed a new apprentice the angle of a cut, Rue caught the old, patient economy in his face and thought how many small corrections could hold a life together.

Aya kept her hands in herbal pots and brews more often than in her once-hidden crust, and the scent of camomile and rosemary threaded the workshops. She passed a jar across a bench: "Soak it in three nights," she told a girl whose fingers had split on a needle. Her hands moved like a remedy; the kindness was clinical and not sentimental. Sometimes Rue would find Aya in the glasshouses pressing leaves between pages for a poultice and laughing low at a joke the years had taught her to keep small.

The Folio came and went in the rhythm of the house: closed, copied, a thin list pinned for the Hall, the full book carried away. Each closure was a small ceremony—tapers in iron sconces, the bell's note—an accounting that smelled of varnish and ink. A scribe's thumb would run along the edge of a page and the announcement would fall like a seam being knotted: "The ledger for this intake is closed and copied." The bell that answered knotted a year's hours into place.

The Three changed faces with the program. One year a poster was set where the benches met the doorway—Elaine's name and a softened account of return, a photograph and a line about living with a scar and the trade she had made of it. Another season's pamphlet held Maris up as an example—polished language about opportunity and stability, a neat photograph and a line about generous sponsors. Sal's name was used differently: a curt injunction in dorm speeches—"Remember Sal"—that stopped conversation like a struck match.

She taught a hundred and then another; cohorts cycled under her hands until faces blurred into rhythm and then particularity again. But one year in the patron hall the montage tightened to a single moment that would keep its weight in Rue's memory.

The benches were set with taper-bright shoulders and a ribbon lay coiled on the lectern like a promise. Rue had chosen the apprentice: a thin girl who kept her thumb stitched into the hem of her apron, eyes quick as a needle's point. Rue smoothed the ribbon between two fingers and watched the way the girl's mouth tried to be brave.

"For steadiness," Rue said, and the words felt more like counting than praise. She tied the ribbon to the girl's shoulder with a practiced knot—the motion small and exact—and the apprentice's fingers flexed as she felt the cloth's weight. Around them the Headmistress's lines—mercy accounted by service and attention—had already settled into the ritual; when the bell rang the sound made the moment official.

Rue felt the note strike under her ribs. The ribbon's loop sat warm against the girl's sleeve; Rue's thumb brushed the fabric and she felt, for a second, the ledger's other face: the human thing that the house measured. The apprentice looked at the ribbon as if it were both an ornament and an invoice; she touched it with the care of someone who knew a count had begun.

After the hall emptied Rue walked the benches with slips tucked into her apron. She set girls to their benches, showed them the angle of a cut or the tension of a stitch, and marked their first hours in the house log with a neat pen stroke. Each name she wrote carried with it a small tally, and each tally settled into the house's ongoing book.

Time scoured some things and inked others deeper. Loren's shoulders stooped a hair; the lamp's light had crept across his knuckles as if to count the years. Aya's laugh took a shape that had learned to carry fewer needless risks. Rue's hands—stitching, knotting, teaching—kept their steadiness; the ledger's habit lived in her muscle as surely as the stitch.

Friendships formed as quick as a folded napkin and dissolved as quietly: a knot of laughter over shared stew, a silence at a trunk when a slip had been pinned and a week in the scullery waited. The House used those human arrangements as it used its ledgers: material to be shaped. Where a girl once needed bread and found the crust, she might, in years, be the one teaching another how to soften and share it.

Occasionally the Folio's margins whispered back. Rue would catch a clerk's fingers tracing a line in a closed book, or see a note pinned that did not appear on the thin public list. Sometimes the crossing-out she had once peered at in a single page reappeared in other hands: an erasure here, a cramped marginal addition there. The Ink Room kept its private work below the library and the Archive's doors moved like a slow pulse; every time the book was taken the house preserved and re-hid its decisions.

At the year's end, at a patron gathering where candles burned low and the bell made the same decisive note, Rue would stand among the benches like a fulcrum—older, surer, and aware that she was both product and producer of the house's counting. She carried apprentices' slips in her apron and returned names to the register with a pen that had learned to weigh.

The montage slowed then into the long present: apprentices learned, ribbons were tied, the bell's note marked endings and beginnings, and the cycle turned. Rue's life had become a ledger in motion—stitches, hours, names, the small mercies and bruises kept in a margin only she sometimes read. She had become an anchor in the house's machinery: someone who taught, who kept accounts, who remembered the cost and the craft. When she left the Hall that night the gardens smelled of rose and sea, and the Folio's closed leather left a quiet pressure in her palm—a reminder that the house counted, and that she counted with it.

Rue saw them from the west stairs, where the Great Hall's windows let the harbor in like a picture. It was late morning and the light fell flat across the flagstones; the benches were half-filled with people folding away the day's work. She had been mending a cuff when a murmur moved through the Hall like a wave—the kind of small, precise change that makes everyone look up at once.

They arrived modestly: a small boat at the quay, a party stepping ashore, coats buttoned against a wind that smelled of tar and iron. The War Envoy walked with the economy of someone who had been taught how to occupy space without wasting it. He wore no colors Rue could read; his voice later would be like a law. For now he moved between porters and clerks and the Headmistress's own attendants like a question that did not take shape until it reached the lectern.

The Headmistress met him on the steps, gloves folded at her wrist as she had folded them for ceremonies and private audiences. She greeted him with the courtesy she reserved for those who could make trouble into an expense. Rue watched their two shapes as if she were looking at a pair of ledgers: one public and polished, the other austere and unreadable.

The Envoy's words were a careful thing. He placed his hands behind his back and said, slow and precise: "Conflict moves toward your shore. Armies that were distant are now marked by their approach. I come with terms and with notice: the war seeks the island."

There was no flourish in his voice; the sentence landed like a flat statement of arithmetic. The room folded itself taut. Someone's spoon stopped midair; a child's laugh died where it had been. Rue felt the Hall contract around the words.

The Headmistress listened with the expression Rue had learned to read as both shield and calculation. When he finished she said, politely, "We take notice, and we will consider any terms in counsel. Mercy Academy serves not only its students but the city that keeps it. We will respond in kind to any legitimate need, within the bounds of our charge."

Her answer was public and careful—an immediate translation of threat into procedure. But Rue watched the way she paused on the word "consider" and the small narrowing at her temple when she said "within the bounds of our charge." That narrowing was not a refusal so much as the sound of her measuring what she might give away.

The Envoy did not press. He placed a folded paper on the lectern—a dry thing of parchment and seal—and inclined his head. "This is notice," he said. "We will return in ten days for a formal answer."

At those words the Hall changed shape. The Headmistress's attendants moved like hands at a loom, gathering lists, speaking names in low voices that Rue did not catch. A clerk stood and took the parchment from the lectern and, with a small, official flourish, carried it toward the Archive corridor as if its presence should be filed where things of weight were kept.

Rue felt the shift like an animal senses a sudden wind: the everyday loosened. The benches that had hummed with quiet industry came alive with a different sort of business. Apprentices pulled sleeves down and smoothed aprons; a woman ran a glance toward the garden gates; somewhere, a bell's distant note—ordinary, and suddenly not—took on the sound of a last marking.

She descended the stairs as the Envoy prepared to leave, and for a moment the two of them were near enough that she could see the Envoy's face close: pale, controlled, with the small, implacable set of someone who carries bad news as fact. He did not meet her eyes; he had the look of a man who had been trained to keep the world clear and his hands empty of apology.

Loren stood in a doorway by the benches, arms crossed, watching with the slow patience of someone who measures things by their repair. He gave Rue a brief nod—the kind of communication that said: notice taken, act later. She wanted to ask him if the house should do something now, but there were questions that had procedures and questions that had to wait.

When the Envoy's party filed out across the flagstones to the quay, Rue heard the murmured words in the wake of their motion: "ten days" and "formal answer." Those words sat like a clock. She imagined the Headmistress in her study, counting the hours as she counted ledgers, measuring what could be promised and what could not.

People began to move with a new cadence. The Headmistress spoke to a cluster of senior staff in clipped sentences—orders, not panic—and Rue watched the machinery begin to engage. Lists were pulled from drawers, names spoken and crossed; a rota was reconsidered in a whisper. The Academy's insulated life had been given a poke and its surface rippled into practical concern.

Rue stood at the window and watched the harbor where the envoy's boat had waited: a small dark shape against the bright water, a reminder that the world beyond the walls still had force. She thought of the contracts she had signed, of the seam and the margin where 'tethering' had been written; she felt suddenly that the house's tallies might meet the tally of a war and that the things recorded in ink could be claimed by a different account.

At dusk the Hall felt altered in a way that would not be fixed by one night's sleep. The Headmistress had bowed to the formality of the Envoy and had met notice with a considered line; Rue had seen the private calculation behind the public answer. Outside, the harbor wind came in with a taste of salt and smoke. The island that had seemed a circled life to Rue now felt like a place on a map with edges that could be pressed.

She left the Great Hall with a small, precise unease. The Envoy's notice had a time attached to it—ten days—and with that number in the air Rue understood that the Academy's ledger would have to account for more than apprentices' hours. The house's tallies might be called upon; someone would have to answer for them.

Rue had taken to standing near the lectern when there were affairs of weight in the Hall; it put the harbor in the windows behind her and let her watch both the city and the house at once. The benches were full in a different way than before—faces alert, fingers folded, a dozen small cloths already smoothed for someone who might be called to hurry.

The War Envoy moved with the same patient, economy she had seen at the steps: precise, unadorned, his coat buttoned against the cold. He did not carry a banner; he carried a notice and a manner that read like a law. When he spoke he did not ask for entry so much as present it, as if the war itself had already written the words for him.

"The island is drawn into the campaign," he said, voice even. "Our supply lines shorten and our needs increase. We come not to take from you blind, but to ask of what can be measured and offered. In time, claims will be made for bonds or levies; in time, support of manpower may be required. We expect counsel and an answer in the period given."

The phrasing was careful—no single demand yet, only the architecture of demands. It was worse for being schematic. Rue heard the list and did the arithmetic: bonds; levies; manpower. Each word landed like a weight placed on the Hall's long table.

The Headmistress heard it the way she heard everything—slowly, as if testing a measure against a rule. She did not blush and she did not bristle; she arranged her reply with the precision of a clerk. "The Mercy Academy is a house of instruction and shelter," she said, palms folded at her wrist. "We can muster kitchens, offer shelter, and keep neutrality within our walls for those under our charge. We will not give names of our girls as a commodity."

There was a small, audible intake in the benches at her refusal. The Envoy's face did not change; his eyes were the unblinking calculation of a ledger. "Neutrality may be of value, but neutrality is accounted for. Kitchens and store are matters of grain and pot; shelter is matter of space and time. The city will ask for something in return—guarantees that sustain the war effort."

The Headmistress's reply turned soft at the edges but not conciliatory. "We will account for what we can spare. We will offer kitchens and shelter as humanitarian assistance. Where civic claims ask for manpower, we will consider how best to meet obligations without breaking our trust to the house's charge. We will not hand over our charges as raw conscription."

It was a bargain wrapped in a refusal and a promise. Rue heard the difference between the two as plainly as if she had been taught to read ink: the Headmistress protected the house's people by moving the payment to accounting—by promising to make a ledger of what the house would give. She would trade counted things, not names.

Someone in the benches could not hold it. A young apprentice—no more than a handful of years beneath the Academy's roof—stood before the Headmistress as if the Hall's ritual let her speak. Her voice trembled, small and raw: "If they come for people, what will we do? What will happen to those with ten years left?"

Her question landed like a thin bell. The Headmistress's eyes softened, and for the first time Rue saw a flash of the woman behind the gloves. "We will shelter those under our charge," the Headmistress said, and there was a syllable in her tone that was promise. Then she added, immediately, with the same clerkly precision: "And we will account it. We will keep a record—who is housed, who is fed, what is pledged. Those records will be how we bargain." The words were both comfort and contract.

The Envoy inclined his head. "Records are useful," he said. "They make claims legible. They will be considered in counsel."

Rue felt a small, private panic she could not put to work. The Headmistress had promised shelter and had promised the ledger. To Rue the two things looked less like alternatives than a single equation: protection in exchange for accounting. The house would not hide; it would count and present its counts for negotiation.

Voices in the benches rose in soft clusters—apprentices whispering, an older woman saying a name as if to test how it sounded under threat. A clerk moved quietly, taking notes. The Hall's machinery engaged itself with the practical speed of a practiced loom: lists were pulled, rotas rethought, kitchens inventoried. Rue watched fingers lift and cross names, the same gestures she had learned in apprenticeship now repurposed for an adult calculus.

Loren stood at the back, sleeves rolled despite the sudden formality, and his jaw set in a way Rue had seen when a stitch would not hold. He gave her a small look—no words—an urging to keep steady and to be counted among those who treated work as defense.

The Envoy's party left as they had come: clean lines, small steps, and a deadline that had the taste of arithmetic in it. He had not named a levy or set a figure; he had only shown how such things might be asked for—bonds, levies, manpower—left to the city's counsel. The Headmistress had named the house's boundaries and promised shelter in exchange for careful accounting.

Rue walked down the aisle after the guests, trailing behind the Headmistress's shadow. The Great Hall felt narrower and more urgent. She thought of the sewn seam of contracts, of the marginal note that had said 'tethering,' of the way the house made its people legible in ink.

Outside, the harbor's air smelled of tar and a hint of smoke on the wind. The Envoy's counsel had not broken the Academy; it had made clear the terms under which the Academy might be compelled. Rue understood, with a sudden, bitter clarity, that the ledger could be pulled into another ledger and that protection might cost a different sort of accounting. The war, in its quiet arithmetic, had already begun to take its place on the page.

They said Elaine would come back like a story—soft on the edges, the kind you pass around a bench to steady a night. When she came into the Hall, she came like proof: not triumphant but plain, a scar at her temple pulling the light into a thin, private line.

The benches shifted when she walked to the lectern; the hundred held its breath the way a person holds a measuring cup steady. Rue stood near the side, hands folded into the cuff of her apron, and felt a small, precise astonishment at the sight of her. Elaine had been the name people used when they wanted to believe something could be done—soft praise on a poster—then a private hope passed hand to hand. Now she was neither poster nor pamphlet but a woman with a narrow set of shoulders and a look that suggested she had counted the cost of things.

The Headmistress rose with the utmost courtesy and introduced Elaine as one of the Academy's returned. "We welcome testimony of life beyond our walls," she said, voice even and gleaming. "Tell us, Elaine."

Elaine's voice came without ceremony. She set her palm on the lectern as if steadying a page and spoke to the benches more than to the lectern. "Markets have narrowed," she said. "Sponsors keep accounts that have teeth. There are bargains made in port houses and in sponsor ledgers—debts tied to ships' manifests; claims that ask for more than coin. I do not say the Academy trades in names, but I say this: the world outside keeps accounts that are not always written in the Folio's neat hand."

The words went through the room like a draught. They did what a sudden wind does to loose paper: exposed the seams. The Headmistress's face did not betray the flinch Rue expected. But her gloves tightened, and the polite smile thinned at the edges. "We publish successes and we celebrate return," the Headmistress said slowly. "The stories of Elaine and others are public examples of craft and steadiness. If there have been arrangements beyond the public pages, they are matters for the house's counsel and procedure."

"Arrangements," Elaine said, and the single word made the benches speak in whispers. "I learned a trade beyond the town when I left. I found sponsors. I found ways to feed a scar. Those sponsors asked for accounting in turn. Sometimes accounting is coin. Sometimes it is names or hours. The forms change, Headmistress. The cost is not always visible where you set your lectern." Her look swept the room and rested on Rue for a second—an acknowledgement, a passing of a thing that had been said elsewhere.

The Headmistress stepped forward, palms flat, the posture of someone restoring a document's margin to order. "We do not trade our girls as goods. The Academy's charge is to train, shelter and provide trade. We will not—"

A voice cut through then, not from the benches but from the far end of the Hall: a kitchen woman who had seen tides and barns and men go hungry. "When grain is short and the city calls, accounts are tallied in ways that leave no loose sums," she said, voice blunt and sure. "We all know what counting can ask."

There was an answering murmur. Rue watched hands, small and practiced: a clerk at the back of the Hall rose and moved to a drawer, the motion crisp as a stitch. He drew out a narrow book—its spine soft with handling—and opened it beneath the lamp. "Sponsorship ledger," someone muttered; the clerk's finger ran a line and read aloud, quietly, the heading: "Sponsors—claims noted." His voice was brisk and official, but Rue heard the list as if it were a tally called at a binding.

The Headmistress's reply sharpened. "If there are private agreements that affect the house's charge, they will be brought to counsel. We keep records. We make our bargains transparent where we must."

Elaine let a small, humorless smile touch her mouth. "Transparency is a ledger's cousin," she said. "You can put down what you choose to tally. You can turn a name into a line if that's what the ledger asks. I'm here to say that sometimes the world asks for entries that are not made in a public page. And when it asks, the choices someone makes are not merely administrative. They mark a life."

Rue felt, in the back of her throat, the remembering of her own signing: the wax, the signet, the small sharp breath when the pen left the paper. The word 'tethering' slid like a memory along her ribs. She thought of small favors that had been tallied as debt, of Loren's soft thumbs correcting her stitch, of a tally slid with a pen no one noticed.

The Headmistress, for the first time that morning, looked less like a clerk and more like someone who kept a whole house's decisions in her hands. "We serve the city," she said, voice steady but thinned. "Where obligations are raised we will review them and seek to protect those under our care. I will not have the Academy's example used as a cudgel where we can conceive better—"

"Conceive better," Elaine echoed, not unkindly. "Sometimes those better conceptions are made at a price. I have come to say that leaving was not only leaving but also taking a place in other ledgers. It made my life possible, and it made me owe things that are not all paid in coin."

A young apprentice near the front sobbed softly, the sound small and jagged. The Hall shuttered inward for a moment as if to keep the sound from flying out. Rue moved to the girl's side as others did; she felt the tautness of fear that the war had leveled into a new kind of accounting.

The Headmistress, seeing the apprentice, softened and stepped toward the benches. She took the girl's hand with an efficiency that was almost tenderness. "We will shelter those under our charge," she said to the room, then, carefully, to the girl: "We will not let you be taken as currency. We will record, we will feed, we will hold." Her voice had the cleric's cadence of promise—and the clerk's addendum: "And we will record it."

Elaine inclined her head slightly, the small motion of someone who has been offered a measure and can name its limit. "Remember what you tell the girls," she said. "The world outside will always have its own ledgers. Know which ones you let count you into them."

She stepped down from the lectern without fanfare. The Hall stayed a little unsettled after she left, the way a table stays trembling after someone has set a weight upon it and gone. Rue touched the margin of her own faith and found it thinner; the Headmistress's public lines had been complicated in one tidy moment by a life that could not be reduced to a pamphlet.

When the clerk closed the sponsorship ledger and slid it back into the drawer, he did it with a small, perfunctory flourish. The room bent back to business with a new thread in its pattern: the Headmistress would defend with rhetoric; some returns carried costs not spelled out in the Folio; whatever the Academy promised, the world outside kept its own accounts, and those could touch the girls in ways the house's pages did not always show.

The Hall had a new electricity in it, the kind that gathers when a machine is about to be set to work. Rue felt it as a thrum beneath the benches; the benches themselves seemed to edge toward the lectern as if the room expected a balance to be struck.

The War Envoy had gone; his deadline hung in the air like chalk on a slate. The Headmistress had called a council and then opened the Hall to witnesses. It was a tactical move—the public watching made the bargains easier to justify later. Rue stood where she always did in affairs: near the lectern, near the Folio, where ink and speech met.

People divided as if by geography. On one side were the clerks and the registry women, the sort who thought every problem could be solved by the right column; they spoke of making an account the Envoy would read. On another side were the instructors and the older apprentices—Loren among them—who said fortify, close gates, make the place a citadel. Voices rose in the low, urgent way of people who had worked in close quarters for years.

Loren's voice was low when he spoke to those clustered near him. "Counting is what we do," he said, "but beware that counts become chains. We can offer hours and skill; we cannot make the girls into columns on an enemy's tally."

A clerk, sleeves rolled, tapped a finger against the ledger's margin. "The Envoy asked for legible claims. Legible claims are what bargainers trade. If we present kitchens and a schedule of labour, we can keep names off the table."

"Kitchens and labour will be counted against us," an older woman in the back said. "Counts can be used to hold us. I have seen how a ledger eats a life."

The Headmistress moved among them with the sure economy of someone used to making difficult arithmetic look like policy. She listened, palms folded, and when she spoke the room leaned in.

"We are not reckless with our charge," she said. "We must protect the girls in our care and the house's work. I have considered fortification. I have considered refusal. But the Envoy's counsel showed that neutrality has cost. We cannot shelter if the city collapses around us."

A murmur. Rue felt the memory of Elaine's words—"transparency is a ledger's cousin"—slide behind her eyes.

The Headmistress lifted a hand. "We will make a civic pledge. We will offer the kitchens and a measured rotation of labour hours as a civic contribution. We will not hand names. We will keep records so that any claim may be accounted for and argued on the house's terms. I will instruct the clerks to draw a civic ledger entry now, stating what we pledge to spare."

The words were a cut. A bargain wrapped as protection. Someone near the front hissed; a young apprentice threw up her hands as if the air itself had been made heavy.

Loren's jaw tightened. "So we bind hours in exchange for shelter?" he asked, voice more dangerous than Rue had heard it in years.

The Headmistress's answer was steady. "We bind counted aid, not persons. That is our line. We offer what can be measured and reclaimed. It keeps names from being traded." Her eyes skimmed the benches as if to see whether the house accepted the calculus.

A clerk moved to a drawer and produced a narrow register, the kind Rue had seen in the Ink Room. He opened it beneath the lamp and set a pen in readiness. "Civic pledge—kitchens, rotation of labour to be scheduled and recorded," he murmured as he wrote the heading aloud. His voice had the official brass of ritual. Fingers pointed to columns; numbers were left blank for the house's accounting.

There was argument immediate and sharp. A woman who ran the garden rotas spoke up: "If we promise labour, the girls will lose hours for their apprenticeships. That reduces their trade, not only their time."

"We will structure rota so training continues," the Headmistress said. "We will not break apprenticeship promise. We will prioritise the inexperienced under shelter."

"And who decides?" someone demanded. "You, from your desk? The clerks? The Envoy's counsel?"

A shout rose—an old, raw sound from a bench where someone remembered scullery weeks. Rue felt it like a struck rod. The Hall had split into people who trusted the ledger to save them and people who had seen ledgers take more than they gave.

Rue thought of her own signing, the wax, the signet, the seam where 'tethering' had been pressed. She felt pulled: to the Headmistress, who preserved roofs and work; to Loren, who warned that counted hours could harden into obligations no one meant to sign for. She walked to him as the clerks wrote the civic heading, feeling the pull of both arguments.

"You know counts," she said to Loren quietly. "If we pledge hours, can we keep apprentices' craft?"

He looked at her as if weighing a stitch. "We can try to shape the rota. But the ledger will be used by others. Records make things legible. Once legible, they can be transferred. The binding—legal or otherwise—follows a tally. Be careful."

The Headmistress, seeing the stir, rose and addressed the room. "We will make the pledge public and record it in the house logs. We will name no individuals. We will set limits. We will review claims as they come to counsel."

That was the choice: a public bargain recorded as a civic pledge, offering counted aid rather than names. The Envoy would have what he wanted—an account to read—and the house would answer with its books. To some that would be compromise and prudence; to others, a surrender of principle.

A small girl at the front—a new apprentice—began to cry. Rue stepped toward her, smoothing the girl's sleeve with hands that had tied many ribbons and steadied many stitches. The Headmistress touched the girl's shoulder with a counter of comfort and calculation. "We will shelter you," she said, voice clear. "We will write what we pledge and we will hold to it."

Loren's hand went to his coat pocket, where he had kept an old scrap of folded cloth for luck. He did not speak, but his eyes met Rue's and in them she read both warning and instruction: hold the craft; keep the counters honest.

Outside the Hall the harbor light had changed. The ten-day clock ticked on the walls of Rue's mind. The Headmistress's decision would be written into house logs; the clerks would note it, the rotas would shift, and somewhere—she did not know where—sponsors and city counsel would translate the numbers into need.

Rue left the Hall with her hands in her apron and the Folio's leather like a pressure in her palms of memory. The house would count; the world would count back. The choice had been made for now. It forced a division that would not be healed by a single ledger entry.

The first sound was a far knock that made the glasshouses shutter. Then the harbor answered: a raw, doubled report that ran along the water and came at the Academy like a hand. Smoke came up low from the quay and the light in the Great Hall went flat; someone began to ring the Amber Bell as if by muscle memory.

"By amber and ink, so sealed," the bell's phrase cut through the Hall—an emergency made to sound like a ritual. The note dropped into the room and every head turned. Rue's hands went still on the cuff she had been mending; the Hall became a place where people were counted and called.

Outside, the approaches burned in stuttering tongues—boats thrown to the shore, ropes on fire, a line of black shapes moving where the harbor should have been quiet. No one in the Hall named the attackers; they were a shape and a noise. The Envoy's notice had become action.

The Headmistress rose and made a single, sharp order. "Clerks, the civic ledger is active. Scribes—Ink Room. Clerks—draw the civic pledge's columns. Muster points now. Kitchens, double your knife shifts. Rota—prioritise shelter. No names to be given." Her voice cut the air like a tally being set.

Clerks moved as if on cue. One slipped to the drawer where the narrow register lived and took it to the lamp. With an inked loop he signed the Archive Key's register and took the key from the tray; another unlatched the cabinet as he had done in calmer times. "Folio—consulted for obligations," the scribe said as the cabinet opened; hands used the practiced economy of people who had handled weight before, extracting the thick book and laying it under lamplight.

Rue followed them to the threshold of the Ink Room and watched the small stage where the Folio was opened. The Binding Seal sat behind glass in its case in the corner; its beeswax and iron scent rose to meet her memory like a bruise. A scribe drew out a clean sheet and set a quick column: "Civic pledge—kitchens; labour rotation; sheltering lists." The clerks bent to their pens and the Folio's page caught the light like a witness.

In the dorms the excusal slips became shelter passes. Rue took a stack and wrote with a hand that did not tremble: name, trunk number, 'shelter pass.' She pinned the slips to the trunks for the ones who would be moved into the Hall—little papers stamped with the dormmistress's cipher—passes to let someone in where there might be no other room. The practice was the same as before but the purpose had been sharpened: these slips now meant a place in the Hall rather than a skip of chores.

Loren had already moved to the glasshouses. "Buckets and lines!" he called, his voice flint. He hauled a length of rope and pointed where women should stand. The older apprentices answered as if a stitch had been called: hands that knew knots became hands that would move water and rope, a makeshift defense against flame and wet wood. They did not pretend it would stand against everything; they did what they could.

A scribe at the Folio read a heading aloud, low and crisp: "Sheltered—names to be logged." The ink gathered in a thin river and the clerk's pen scratched as if counting time. Every name written was a count; every count might be a future claim. Rue felt the weight of each letter as if it were a small coin dropped into a communal fund.

The Amber Bell rang again, not as ceremony but as command. Rota boards were cleared and rewritten; cooks moved like a practiced army, ladling and measuring. The Headmistress walked the benches, touching shoulders with a touch that was both reassurance and a tally. "Keep the logs," she told the clerks. "Record who is sheltered, who is fed, what is pledged. We will argue from the numbers."

But the Ink Room did more than note. A scribe removed a clean vellum and, following the Headmistress's instructions, pencilled a margin note: 'Civic pledge in effect—kitchens and labour rotation active.' It was not an enchantment; it was a document, and the document itself would be the thing negotiators read later. Rue watched the scribe's hand, thinking of how a line of ink could shape a life.

In the Hall the Hundred moved with the steady panic of people who had practised together. A small girl clung to Rue's sleeve and would not let go; Rue knelt and tied a knot in the girl's apron, a small domestic action that felt like a shield. The Headmistress paced and, at intervals, checked the Folio's open page, touching the leather as if to make the book's weight real.

The defenders on the walls worked with the mundane instruments available: ropes, buckets, quick barricades of benches and trunks. Rue helped where she could, carrying a bucket, pressing a wet cloth into a hand, counting the names of girls as they passed through the Hall into the shelter. Each count was a promise made under pressure.

There were no miracles. The Binding Seal stayed behind glass, its presence a recalled scent rather than a released force. The scribes read from the Folio and wrote into the house logs; the Headmistress's tactics were clerical and tactical rather than sorcerous. The academy's defenses were human and ledgered: shelter by count, food by ration, labor by rota.

Smoke thickened along the quay and the sound of the assault moved from the water toward the walls. Someone shouted that a small boat had been grounded and was burning. A woman in the front fainted and apprentices bent to help, voices breaking into prayer and orders.

Loren gripped Rue's shoulder once, in a quick, fierce clasp. "Keep the counters honest," he told her. "Record who goes in and who comes out. Hold them." His eyes, usually patient, had the sharpness of someone who had seen a seam tear.

Rue did as she had always done: she counted, she comforted, she recorded. The Folio's open page absorbed names, the clerks' pens moving like the tide. The Amber Bell's note—half ritual, half alarm—kept time for them all.

When the first hush of night came the Hall smelled of smoke and broth; the lamplight tilted and the Folio lay open with new entries. The Academy had bound itself—by choice and by tally—to a civic pledge that would have to be defended in counsel. The cost of that accounting was already manifest: sleepless names, rationed bread, and a ledger that would be read by people who kept their own books.

Rue sat on a low bench, hands inked and tired, and felt the Folio's weight in her thoughts. The house's bargains had history now—inked and immediate. The Hundred had paid in fear and in work and in the slow tallying of strong, necessary things. Outside, the sound of the siege kept coming in waves; inside, their records would have to hold.

The Ink Room smelled colder with people in it, as if the lamplight had to share its warmth. Rue stayed near the lectern because she could not bear to be at the windows; the Great Hall's benches were full and the Three stood close as living footnotes to their own names.

The scribe who had taken the Folio from the cabinet set a magnifier in place and folded his sleeves higher. He did not read aloud at once. Instead he ran his thumbnail along a pale, scoured margin where the paper had been rubbed thin. "This line has been tampered with," he said to the room, voice low. "I will need to scrape the blot carefully to see what lies beneath."

It was a small, almost surgical motion: the scraping of ink and the soft raising of paper dust. Rue watched as the blade worked and the lamp made the fibres glimmer. At first there was only paler paper where ink had been; then a shadow of letters resolved. The clerk leaned over and breathed, careful. "Here—an added note," he murmured, and his finger pointed to a cramped set of words beside a name in the sponsorship ledger laid beside the Folio. He read, quietly, as if making the room hold its breath: "Exchange noted."

The two words landed like a coin dropped into a bowl. They were not numbers; they were a notation that promised another page elsewhere. Elaine's face did not change when the clerk read them; Maris's mouth tightened in a way that looked like defence. Sal's hand went small and distant to the scar at her temple.

"Exchange noted," Elaine repeated, not as correction but as confirmation. "That is the language of sponsors—an account recorded in another book. It does not say what was exchanged. It only names that an exchange occurred."

The Headmistress's fingers tightened on the Folio's leather. "If there are entries that affect the house's charge, they will be brought to counsel," she said. "We will review these marginal notations as part of our records."

"Records make things legible," Loren said from the back, his voice low enough that the clerks might write but not that the walls would hear. "Legible things can be traded. Be careful what you make visible."

Rue's chest felt like a ledger being closed and reopened at once. She had come to the Ink Room expecting a ritual answer—something tidy and sanctifying—and found instead the bluntness of notation. The Folio's columns, the sponsorship ledger's headings, the clerk's perfunctory reading: each was a small instrument by which people might be measured, presented, and, sometimes, claimed.

Maris stepped forward then, smoothing her hands as if arranging the sleeve of a pamphlet that might be read aloud. "I left," she said, voice even, "with help that had conditions. I paid with my time and my obligations. I do not regret the life I made, but do not pretend that the return came without accounting." She glanced at the clerk's open ledger and the small phrase 'exchange noted' beside a faded line. "Some ledgers have teeth."

Sal did not speak; she only folded her hands and looked into the lamp, as if the light might show her truth. For an instant Rue thought Sal's silence said more than any explanation.

The Headmistress moved as a woman who must both comfort and buffer. "We will audit these entries," she said. "Where a marginal note affects our charge, we will bring it to counsel and protect those under our care. We will not be reckless in drawing conclusions without examination."

"Examination will take time," Elaine said. "And time is a currency sometimes spent elsewhere. I am not here to demand vengeance; I am here to say the margin exists and that its existence matters."

The clerk closed the sponsorship ledger with a small, reluctant snap and slid it back to the scribe for safekeeping. The act—closing a book whose edges had held another accounting—felt to Rue like a small amputation. The room had changed. Where once she had imagined a ritual to explain away a seam, she now saw a series of counters and quiet notations that could be read by other men with other ledgers.

Loren's hand found Rue's shoulder as if it were a stitch that needed holding. "Keep an eye on the counters," he said. "Write what you see. If you are to be counted, count honestly."

Rue turned back to the Folio, fingers hovering over a page while heat and lamplight made the ink gleam. She did not look for a single word to absolve her; she felt the slow, certain understanding of what her signature had allowed: not a ritual enchantment to be undone, but a set of records that could be used, read, and argued over in a world that kept its own books.

Elaine's scar caught the lamplight as she stepped down from the lectern. She inclined her head to the Headmistress and then to Rue. "Know which ledgers you let count you into," she said simply, then left as quietly as she had come.

The scribe wiped his hands and closed the magnifier. The Folio was shut for a moment, heavy and unyielding. The Ink Room's door closed on the quiet scraping and the whisper of pages turning—on the tally that would be read again and again. Rue left the room with none of the neat, ritual absolution she had sought. She carried, instead, the ledger's new weight: the knowledge that the Academy's myths were recorded in ink and that ink had a way of calling accounts due.

The council had been a blur of ledger talk and bitter arithmetic; the siege had made every choice immediate. Rue found herself at the small table in the corner of the Great Hall not because the Headmistress summoned her but because the house wanted someone with hands that still knew stitches and the courage to cut a seam.

The Envoy waited at the lectern like an unread statute. He had returned early, impatient with counsel that moved in circles. The Headmistress stood between him and the Folio, placid and lined with the work of a life spent translating trouble into entries. Rue set her palms on the worn wood and felt the ledgered pressure under her skin.

"We can bargain with numbers," she said, voice small but steady. "We can trade what is countable. But we must do it in a way that leaves people free to be themselves after. I have been counting for a long time. I know how a tally can harden into a chain."

Loren, at her shoulder, tilted his head. "You know the counters, Rue. You know what a ledger will do."

She did. She had signed once as a young woman and learned how a pen could be a rope. The Folio lay open where the clerks had set it; the scribe had put the sponsorship ledger beside it and, beneath the lamplight, the phrase 'Exchange noted' still seemed to pulse like a small accusation.

Rue's plan was not a magic. It was paperwork and witnesses and a bartered sacrifice. "We will offer the civic pledge as we said—kitchens, measured labour hours—but we will make a witnessed transfer mechanism," she told the room. "We will record, here and now, a conditional column in the Folio: 'Pending release—exchange recorded.' The Envoy will accept our civic pledge as partial satisfaction for certain sponsorship claims, provided those claims are publicly reclassified as 'satisfied by civic exchange' with clerks and sponsor or municipal witness. Those entries will be stamped and witnessed, and then the named apprentices may petition counsel for review."

Silence became the room's examiner. The Envoy folded his hands. "Legible claims are legible," he said. "If your civic pledge is recorded and the city accepts it as fulfillment, those claims will be accounted for."

The Headmistress's eyes narrowed, the accountant's knot forming behind her calm. "It requires sponsor acknowledgment and a municipal acceptance—legal witnesses. It will not erase a binding without that. But it will create a path that can be argued in counsel."

Rue knew the cost. "The house will redistribute apprenticeship hours to meet the civic pledge," she said. "Training will slow. Rations will be measured. Some women will carry extra shifts. I volunteer to be guarantor—my time and my instructional hours—to underwrite the first set of petitions. Let the record show a guarantor with a name who will be accountable." She said the words and felt them land like a tally on her chest.

Loren's breath caught. "You would put your name to it?"

"Yes," she said. "If a ledger is what frees them, let it be a ledger with witnesses and with someone to answer the cost. I will take on the shifts in the workshops; I will trade my hours so others may be counted out."

The Headmistress considered, fingers folding a small motion of thought. The Envoy watched as if weighing a contract. A clerk stepped forward to the Archive drawer and signed the register with the inked loop they used for the key; the Archive Key was handed to the scribe who would open the deeper cabinets if needed. "We note the guarantor action," the clerk said aloud, writing: 'Guarantor: Rue — instructional hours pledged.' He set his pen down and looked up. "Now the civic pledge entry must be made canonical."

Scribes bent to work. The clerk wrote the heading into the Folio: 'Pending Release — conditional upon sponsor or municipal acknowledgment; civic exchange recorded.' He left a narrow column for names and another for witnesses. Then, with the Headmistress in attendance and the Envoy's official nod, the clerk read the sentence aloud and the lamp made the ink shine. The Envoy inclined his head and, in a small, efficient motion, noted the city's receipt of the civic pledge—an acceptance in principle, not full ratification.

Maris watched the line as if it might spit fire. Elaine's face showed something like approval, tempered with the memory of what exchanges demand. Sal's silence kept the scar at the temple in shadow.

The cost was immediate. The rota boards were adjusted: apprentices would lose hours; ration tickets were cut; cooks doubled their output. Rue signed her name—the same script that once marked her sold provenance—beneath the new civic column. The pen felt colder this time; it felt like choice.

"Names will not be handed over as raw conscription," the Headmistress said, voice steady. "But a path has been created: public, witnessed, and accountable. Those with claims satisfied by civic exchange may petition counsel for release."

Loren put his hand on Rue's shoulder, rough and certain. "You have set the counters to a certain honesty," he said. "Keep them honest when the world reads them." His eyes held the old warning: counts become chains unless watched.

Rue thought of the years she had traded. She had been practical when she was young; now she was practical in a different way. She had traded some of her future instruction hours as a guarantee; she had asked for a witnessed mechanism that could be read by sponsors and municipal counsel. It might not free anyone immediately. It might not be enough. But it was a path that used the house's own language—ink and witness—to make a claim legible and contestable.

The Headmistress nodded once and tapped the Folio's leather as if sealing a sentence. "We will convene counsel within three days to consider petitions under this entry," she said. "Let the clerks prepare the lists."

Outside, the harbor smoke had not lifted. Inside, a new column stood in the Folio and rue's name shielded one small, human possibility. The Hundred would pay—hours, food, and a debt of patience—but there would be a record to read and a room in which to argue. Rue had chosen the ledger as tool and guarantor, and she felt its weight settle into her palms like an old stitch.

Later, when the clerk copied the new civic entry into the house logs and slid the paper into the Archive under a stamped witness, Rue understood the cost: a slowing of craft, a thinning of bread, and her own time bound as surety. She also understood what she had done: she had used the house's language to carve a way out.

Whether that way would be enough would be decided by sponsors, counsel, and the Envoy's city. Rue had made a sacrifice in ink and hours; she had set a procedure that might, in time, let some leave. She had owned the consequence of her sale and set the Academy toward a new, ledgered reckoning.

Counsel met in the hall beneath a lamp that had seen a hundred decisions and would see a few more. Rue came not as supplicant but as guarantor, her palms stained with ink from the hours she had pledged. The Folio lay open and the Pending Release column gleamed like a new seam.

The Headmistress called the council to order with a motion of her hand that had shut down arguments before and would have to hold them now. The clerks drew up the petition lists while the scribes placed witness slates and stamped paper where signatures would be taken. Outside, the quay still smoked; inside, the house was a machine set to make a single kind of work visible.

Rue watched as the clerk passed the first stack to the Headmistress. Each petition was a tight thing: a name, a sponsor reference where present, the clerk's note that an exchange had been logged as 'satisfied by civic exchange' in the sponsorship ledger. The language was careful—'Approved, pending sponsor/municipal acknowledgment'—but the effect of the ink felt immediate. It made space.

When the Headmistress read the first verdict aloud the Hall leaned forward. "By counsel and witness," she intoned, "the petitions listed under Pending Release are approved for provisional release pending sponsor or municipal acknowledgment. The house provides escort; the city will be notified of the civic exchange recorded." Her voice was both sentinel and scribe.

A clerk stamped the first petition with a seal that echoed the sound of a small, decisive coin hitting a table. Rue's guarantor line—her own pledged hours—sat beneath the approved names in a neat clerk's hand. The backing paper was folded and carried to the Ink Room where another scribe made a copy, drew a witness line, and slid it into the Archive under the stamped witness. The mechanical economy of paperwork turned and turned: signature, seal, record.

Eleven petitions were read; eleven names received the provisional stamp. It was not a final freedom—there remained signatures to be gathered, municipal ratification to be sought—but it was a change from the sealed state of the Folio: entries had moved from margin to motion. The Amber Bell, used for more than ceremonies now, was rung once—low, an acoustic stitch of decision—and the sound spread like a ledger being closed on one page and opened on another.

Rue felt each approved name as both gift and subtraction. The rota boards had already been altered: apprenticeship hours redistributed, rota columns narrowed, ration tickets adjusted. The cost was literal—less time at the bench for many, more labor for others—and it was moral: Rue's pledged hours, inked in the Folio, would be drawn down over months as she kept the workshops running for those who stayed.

Elaine watched the proceedings with a steady, private patience. When one of the approved petitions listed a woman who had once sat beside Maris in the gardens, Maris's hand found Elaine's and squeezed. Sal stood apart, watching the clerk's pen glide: she did not smile.

One apprentice—small, breathy—stepped forward when the name that touched her friend was read. The Headmistress offered a quiet nod and the clerk called an escort: two women from the guard and a scribe to carry the petition copy. The escort would not ferry them into freedom unobserved; it would be a measured walk toward a quay where sponsors might sign and municipal officers might stamp. The apprentice's eyes found Rue's and, in the briefest of sounds, said, "For now?"

Rue could only fold the girl's hands into hers and answer with a small, certain pressure. "For now," she said, and meant the provisional part: that the ledger could be the instrument of release but that release would be argued in other rooms as well.

After the petitions were stamped, the Headmistress set a counsel date—three days out, as she had promised—and ordered the clerks to prepare a public summary to be read to the benches. The clerk wrote the lines: 'Civic pledge honoured; Pending Release column updated; eleven petitions provisionally approved; municipal notification requested.' The words would sit in the ledger and be carried out to the city and, with luck and argument, would pull names through.

Rue left the Folio with ink on her fingers and a weight on her shoulders that felt like the weight of a coin pressed into pocket. She returned to the bench not to resume her old life but to begin the work she had promised: extra hours at the loom and the bench, teaching those whose names remained in the house, smoothing stitches as if she could mend ledger lines with thread.

In the weeks that followed the island changed the way islands do: schedules bent, markets thinned, travel across the water was measured by permits and stamped papers. The Headmistress's office filled with civic ledgers and petition slips; clerks moved between rooms like a city's arteries.

Rue's days narrowed and lengthened. She taught at the bench with a calm she had built over years; she kept a register of hours she had pledged and watched the tally fall. Aya, who had tended herbs and wounds, turned some of her remedies into small parcels that went with the escorted women—gifts that smelled of rosemary and steadiness. Loren's hands ached less with age than with the work of keeping a seam from tearing; he stayed at Rue's side, a steadying presence in the cramped and necessary arithmetic of care.

When the first of the escorted women returned with municipal acknowledgment—a small signature on a municipal scroll, a sponsor's stamped endorsement—she left the quay quietly, no trumpet, no pamphlet. The scribe had written 'Approved—satisfied by civic exchange' beside her name in the ledger; the clerk had ticked the Approved column. The Amber Bell rang softly in the Hall the evening her departure was noted; the tone was not triumph but a measured sealing.

Not all petitions would be approved. Some sponsors refused to accept the civic exchange; others required time the city could not yet grant. Those names remained inked and waiting in the Pending Release column. The Folio, patient as ever, held both sorts of entries with the same indifferent neatness.

Rue lived her last months in that steady place between counting and mercy. She watched those who left walk down the quay under escort and noted their names in her private notebook—the family ledger she had kept since her first decision. She wrote little things: 'Anna—approved—left 3rd month; lesson count: 24 hours pledged and served.' She made sure the records she touched were honest.

When the season bent toward autumn, the Headmistress asked Rue to ring the Amber Bell once at the year's closing—an honor in which Rue felt both pride and the ache of price. She struck the bell and the note settled over the Hall like a ledger clause closing for a day. The Three—Elaine, Maris, Sal—stood together for a moment in the doorway, each with a different expression: acceptance, a tremor of cost, silence.

Rue's last act in the Hall was not a speech but a folded paper handed to the clerk: a brief note, her final guarantor accounting—hours pledged and hours owed, a tally she had kept with meticulous care. The clerk took it and slid it into the Folio's Pending Release column with a witness stamp. It was not a dramatic gesture. It was a record.

Outside, the island had lost some of its bustle; inside, its pages had gained a new column. The ending was not the undoing she might have wished nor the full absolution she had once hoped for. It was, instead, a ledger in motion: inked entries, conditional releases, and a slow reweaving of life. Rue walked the glasshouses at dusk, hands empty of binding seals and full of stitches, and understood that bargains leave marks. She had made one more tally—a public act that might let some go—and she had taken the cost upon herself.

What the island owed its people, and what they owed themselves for the choices they made in desperation, would be argued in counsel and in years to come. For Rue, the reckoning had become a daily stitch: to teach, to count honestly, and to keep the ledger from taking more than it should. The Folio recorded the new path; the Amber Bell kept time; and the island, which had been a circled life, had edges that would now be read and negotiated.
