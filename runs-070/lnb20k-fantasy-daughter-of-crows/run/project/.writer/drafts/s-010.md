Rue saw them from the west stairs, where the Great Hall's windows let the harbor in like a picture. It was late morning and the light fell flat across the flagstones; the benches were half-filled with people folding away the day's work. She had been mending a cuff when a murmur moved through the Hall like a wave—the kind of small, precise change that makes everyone look up at once.

They arrived modestly: a small boat at the quay, a party stepping ashore, coats buttoned against a wind that smelled of tar and iron. The War Envoy walked with the economy of someone who had been taught how to occupy space without wasting it. He wore no colors Rue could read; his voice later would be like a law. For now he moved between porters and clerks and the Headmistress's own attendants like a question that did not take shape until it reached the lectern.

The Headmistress met him on the steps, gloves folded at her wrist as she had folded them for ceremonies and private audiences. She greeted him with the courtesy she reserved for those who could make trouble into an expense. Rue watched their two shapes as if she were looking at a pair of ledgers: one public and polished, the other austere and unreadable.

The Envoy's words were a careful thing. He placed his hands behind his back and said, slow and precise: "Conflict moves toward your shore. Armies that were distant are now marked by their approach. I come with terms and with notice: the war seeks the island."

There was no flourish in his voice; the sentence landed like a flat statement of arithmetic. The room folded itself taut. Someone's spoon stopped midair; a child's laugh died where it had been. Rue felt the Hall contract around the words.

The Headmistress listened with the expression Rue had learned to read as both shield and calculation. When he finished she said, politely, "We take notice, and we will consider any terms in counsel. Mercy Academy serves not only its students but the city that keeps it. We will respond in kind to any legitimate need, within the bounds of our charge."

Her answer was public and careful—an immediate translation of threat into procedure. But Rue watched the way she paused on the word "consider" and the small narrowing at her temple when she said "within the bounds of our charge." That narrowing was not a refusal so much as the sound of her measuring what she might give away.

The Envoy did not press. He placed a folded paper on the lectern—a dry thing of parchment and seal—and inclined his head. "This is notice," he said. "We will return in ten days for a formal answer."

At those words the Hall changed shape. The Headmistress's attendants moved like hands at a loom, gathering lists, speaking names in low voices that Rue did not catch. A clerk stood and took the parchment from the lectern and, with a small, official flourish, carried it toward the Archive corridor as if its presence should be filed where things of weight were kept.

Rue felt the shift like an animal senses a sudden wind: the everyday loosened. The benches that had hummed with quiet industry came alive with a different sort of business. Apprentices pulled sleeves down and smoothed aprons; a woman ran a glance toward the garden gates; somewhere, a bell's distant note—ordinary, and suddenly not—took on the sound of a last marking.

She descended the stairs as the Envoy prepared to leave, and for a moment the two of them were near enough that she could see the Envoy's face close: pale, controlled, with the small, implacable set of someone who carries bad news as fact. He did not meet her eyes; he had the look of a man who had been trained to keep the world clear and his hands empty of apology.

Loren stood in a doorway by the benches, arms crossed, watching with the slow patience of someone who measures things by their repair. He gave Rue a brief nod—the kind of communication that said: notice taken, act later. She wanted to ask him if the house should do something now, but there were questions that had procedures and questions that had to wait.

When the Envoy's party filed out across the flagstones to the quay, Rue heard the murmured words in the wake of their motion: "ten days" and "formal answer." Those words sat like a clock. She imagined the Headmistress in her study, counting the hours as she counted ledgers, measuring what could be promised and what could not.

People began to move with a new cadence. The Headmistress spoke to a cluster of senior staff in clipped sentences—orders, not panic—and Rue watched the machinery begin to engage. Lists were pulled from drawers, names spoken and crossed; a rota was reconsidered in a whisper. The Academy's insulated life had been given a poke and its surface rippled into practical concern.

Rue stood at the window and watched the harbor where the envoy's boat had waited: a small dark shape against the bright water, a reminder that the world beyond the walls still had force. She thought of the contracts she had signed, of the seam and the margin where 'tethering' had been written; she felt suddenly that the house's tallies might meet the tally of a war and that the things recorded in ink could be claimed by a different account.

At dusk the Hall felt altered in a way that would not be fixed by one night's sleep. The Headmistress had bowed to the formality of the Envoy and had met notice with a considered line; Rue had seen the private calculation behind the public answer. Outside, the harbor wind came in with a taste of salt and smoke. The island that had seemed a circled life to Rue now felt like a place on a map with edges that could be pressed.

She left the Great Hall with a small, precise unease. The Envoy's notice had a time attached to it—ten days—and with that number in the air Rue understood that the Academy's ledger would have to account for more than apprentices' hours. The house's tallies might be called upon; someone would have to answer for them.
