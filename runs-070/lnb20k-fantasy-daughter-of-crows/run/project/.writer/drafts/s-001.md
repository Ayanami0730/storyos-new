The Chapel smelled of beeswax and dust. Rue kept to the shadows by the nave until the Amber Lantern swung close enough for its honeyed light to pick out the lines of her hands. It made the ink on the Contract Folio catch like a promise.

The Headmistress stood at the dais in a coat that had seen both court and cloister; when she spoke the room leaned in as if to catch a bargain. Behind her, the bronze of the Bell sat on its velvet cushion, mute and waiting. The Hundred had been arranged in two neat blocks—faces younger than the stone—and when the Headmistress’s voice came it filled the vault like a measured gospel.

"By the mercy of this house and the law that holds it," she said, "we accept into our keeping those who come by will or by obligation. This year we admit one hundred. The Folio will record names, ages, dates, provenance, and signatures. Ten years hence the Archive will judge what has been lived here; history shows that, in ten years' measure, only three have walked back out. We offer board, instruction, and work; we take labour, obedience, and service. Let the terms be plain and witnessed: the Academy keeps; the Academy keeps account."

The words were ceremonial and precise at once—mercy named as a charge, not a sentiment. Rue had learned to listen for the clauses that mattered. Shelter. Work. Ten years. Account.

A scribe stepped forward with the Contract Folio under his arm, a slab of leather bound like an anchor. He opened to a fresh page and read, aloud, the form as he set his pen: "Folio Entry— Name: __________________; Age: __; Date: ______; Intake: One hundred; Provenance (sold/placed/orphaned): __________; Signature: __________________; Witness: Headmistress / Scribe."

Rue had rehearsed the numbers in her head as if they were coins. Eighteen years. Bed. Bread. Three years of apprenticeship with the binders, then the ledger's slow climb to something steadier—if the Academy kept its side of the ledger she could buy time and skill. It was not a poem; it was a calculation.

When her turn came the amber light settled over the Folio so that the white of the paper looked like bone. She stepped forward without the theatrics other girls tried—no wide look to the crowd, no trembling hand. The scribe dipped his pen. "Name?" he said.

She laid her hand flat and heard the dry scrape of writing when she wrote R-U-E in a hand she did not much like. The scribe's voice marked each line as he copied: "Name: Rue. Age: 18. Date: this day. Intake: One hundred. Provenance: Sold by consent."

The words sat on the page like small seals. "Signature," the scribe said.

Rue put her name where the line ran, then stared at it as if to be sure the ink had settled. She could have had a last look at the green beyond the chapel windows—she let the thought go. There were people who made speeches about sacrifice; she had a stomach that disagreed with speeches. Mercy was useful; hunger had no rhetoric.

The Headmistress bent her head slightly and repeated the closing clause as the scribe lowered his pen. "So entered under the charge of the Mercy Academy. Let the Folio stand as record and law. Let those within this house remember that mercy is accounted for by service and attention, not by indulgence."

A young warden, pale at the mouth with the weight of his responsibility, took the Amber Lantern from the steward and held it over the inscription so the light lay on her signature. "Witnessed by the house," he intoned. The amber glass threw Rue's name in warm relief.

Then the Bronze Bell was lifted and struck. The sound rolled through the stone like the closing of a gate. For a moment it seemed the note had no end; when it did, someone near the back of the Hundred made a small, involuntary noise that the Headmistress ignored as if it had not been allowed.

The scribe closed the Folio with a solid hand. "Recorded: Rue—Age 18—Provenance: Sold by consent—Intake: One hundred," he read as the leather thudded together. The sound felt final in the way doors can be final.

Rue stepped back, and the lantern was lifted away. She felt the weight of her choice like a coin in a palm—small, hard, real. She did not believe for an instant that the Academy was kind; she believed that it kept its records. That belief, she had decided, would be enough.

As the girls were guided from the Chapel toward the dormitory, the Headmistress’s words followed them—an instruction dressed as mercy. In the corridor the scribe folded the page into the Folio's seam; ink dried, leather cradled the entry, and the house's accounting closed another line.

Outside the dormitory door, Rue touched the side of her wrist where the lantern’s light had warmed her and, without flourish, walked on. The Bell's echo lingered in the chapel long after the door had shut. It lodged there like an instruction to be obeyed.
