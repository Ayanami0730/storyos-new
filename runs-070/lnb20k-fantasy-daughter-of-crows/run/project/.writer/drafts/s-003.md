The Archive smelled of vellum and cold dust — everything there kept its breath low. They put me to work with the smallest things first: wipes for the shelves, a brush for the folio's edges, slips to copy into the day's list. The chores were matter-of-fact. You learn an institution the way you learn a machine: by touching the parts you are allowed to touch.

"We don't hand the Folio about like a fidget toy," the junior woman who oversaw the ledger room told me on my first morning. "Full Folio is kept in the Archive; only staff and supervised apprentices may handle it."

Her voice was not unkind. It was precise; there are sounds institutions make when they mean to be tidy. My job was thin but steady: fetch a page, hold the lamp, wipe a smudge, copy a single address into the day's foliolist. For that, you needed two kinds of care — a hand that would not smear ink, and a head that would not be pleased with secrets.

Over months I proved careful with ink and order; a junior Archivist granted me supervised access to consult older margins. They told me the reasons as if they were rules: the full book belonged to the House; the thin list could be posted; marginalia belonged to the scribe's hand and to the Archive's memory. Apprentices were allowed to copy, to fetch, to index. We were not allowed to take the Folio down the stairs to the sunlight.

The margins were where the House kept its private sentences. Some notes were thin and explanatory — "(child present)" or "* petition deferred" — small, bureaucratic and indifferent. Others were blunt: a line drawn through a name; a dash that seemed to eat the letters beside it. Once, on a page threaded by decades of hands, I saw a small cluster of shorthand that made the air in my chest go colder: "— vanished (no trace)."

I had learned to read those marks the way you learn to read the weather. An asterisk could be nothing. A parenthesis could be explanation. A dash might be nothing at all. And then you would see the bed across a dormitory and find it stripped to the slats, the quilt folded and gone, the place empty the way an absence feels more like a missing tooth than a missing thing.

On a quiet afternoon, carrying a lamp and a handful of slips, I saw such a bunk — a bunk a few rows down had been stripped bare; no trunk stood at its foot. Nobody spoke of it aloud while we worked. Later, in the ledger room, I turned a page and found a name with only a dash at its margin: a blunt, impatient mark that the scribe had left and then gone on with other business.

We had a language for lesser things — mending notes, a child recorded as "(child present)" so that rations could be adjusted — but the dash was a different grammar. Mara, who worked in the scullery on and off and had an appetite for telling stories, said it was better not to pry. "Silences are their own laws here," she told me once, while we shelved a stack of used forms. "Ask wrong and you get asked to scrub the drains until your hands forget what sky feels like."

The Archive had its own small theatre of authority. The Archivist — the person who kept the key and set the hours — had the responsibility of the ledger room. The house kept the ledger room under strict watch: two locks, a register at the door, and an expectation that removal of folio pages required two witnesses and an entry in the Archive register. That was not a story; it was how the junior woman explained it, in the same curt way she explained where to hold the lamp.

Being trusted with the lamp and the slips had its rewards that were quiet and precise. I learned marginalia conventions as an apprenticeship: the asterisk for petitions, the dash for abrupt absences, the parenthetical for temporary notes. I learned which turns of phrase meant to look away and which meant to go home and count spoons. The work gave me small satisfactions — the clean corner of a page, the neatness of a copied line, the hush when a page lay flat and the ink did not run.

And yet the hush also had a weight. When a bed was empty and a dash sat in the margin beside a name, the room did not make a fuss; it kept the arithmetic of the House tidy. You felt the omission in the way you felt a missing coin in a purse. You noticed, you adjusted, you moved on. That was part of learning to live under the Folio's governance: to keep your accounts and to keep your eyes measured.

I ran errands into the Garden sometimes, carrying forms that smelled of vellum and damp earth. The Garden was where we could breathe and say louder things in safer company. I told Lin about the bunk that was bare; she frowned and handed me a crust as if buying the silence. "Keep it between us," she said. "We make each other safer by noticing, not by shouting."

I had been brought to the Archive because I kept my handwriting flat and my back straight before the desk. That small trust did not change the ledger's arithmetic: the House recorded, the House decided. What it did change, for me, was the angle from which I began to see the rules — not as a distant policy but as marks on paper, and as absences in the bunks we slept in.
