The Ink Room smelled colder with people in it, as if the lamplight had to share its warmth. Rue stayed near the lectern because she could not bear to be at the windows; the Great Hall's benches were full and the Three stood close as living footnotes to their own names.

The scribe who had taken the Folio from the cabinet set a magnifier in place and folded his sleeves higher. He did not read aloud at once. Instead he ran his thumbnail along a pale, scoured margin where the paper had been rubbed thin. "This line has been tampered with," he said to the room, voice low. "I will need to scrape the blot carefully to see what lies beneath."

It was a small, almost surgical motion: the scraping of ink and the soft raising of paper dust. Rue watched as the blade worked and the lamp made the fibres glimmer. At first there was only paler paper where ink had been; then a shadow of letters resolved. The clerk leaned over and breathed, careful. "Here—an added note," he murmured, and his finger pointed to a cramped set of words beside a name in the sponsorship ledger laid beside the Folio. He read, quietly, as if making the room hold its breath: "Exchange noted."

The two words landed like a coin dropped into a bowl. They were not numbers; they were a notation that promised another page elsewhere. Elaine's face did not change when the clerk read them; Maris's mouth tightened in a way that looked like defence. Sal's hand went small and distant to the scar at her temple.

"Exchange noted," Elaine repeated, not as correction but as confirmation. "That is the language of sponsors—an account recorded in another book. It does not say what was exchanged. It only names that an exchange occurred."

The Headmistress's fingers tightened on the Folio's leather. "If there are entries that affect the house's charge, they will be brought to counsel," she said. "We will review these marginal notations as part of our records."

"Records make things legible," Loren said from the back, his voice low enough that the clerks might write but not that the walls would hear. "Legible things can be traded. Be careful what you make visible."

Rue's chest felt like a ledger being closed and reopened at once. She had come to the Ink Room expecting a ritual answer—something tidy and sanctifying—and found instead the bluntness of notation. The Folio's columns, the sponsorship ledger's headings, the clerk's perfunctory reading: each was a small instrument by which people might be measured, presented, and, sometimes, claimed.

Maris stepped forward then, smoothing her hands as if arranging the sleeve of a pamphlet that might be read aloud. "I left," she said, voice even, "with help that had conditions. I paid with my time and my obligations. I do not regret the life I made, but do not pretend that the return came without accounting." She glanced at the clerk's open ledger and the small phrase 'exchange noted' beside a faded line. "Some ledgers have teeth."

Sal did not speak; she only folded her hands and looked into the lamp, as if the light might show her truth. For an instant Rue thought Sal's silence said more than any explanation.

The Headmistress moved as a woman who must both comfort and buffer. "We will audit these entries," she said. "Where a marginal note affects our charge, we will bring it to counsel and protect those under our care. We will not be reckless in drawing conclusions without examination."

"Examination will take time," Elaine said. "And time is a currency sometimes spent elsewhere. I am not here to demand vengeance; I am here to say the margin exists and that its existence matters."

The clerk closed the sponsorship ledger with a small, reluctant snap and slid it back to the scribe for safekeeping. The act—closing a book whose edges had held another accounting—felt to Rue like a small amputation. The room had changed. Where once she had imagined a ritual to explain away a seam, she now saw a series of counters and quiet notations that could be read by other men with other ledgers.

Loren's hand found Rue's shoulder as if it were a stitch that needed holding. "Keep an eye on the counters," he said. "Write what you see. If you are to be counted, count honestly."

Rue turned back to the Folio, fingers hovering over a page while heat and lamplight made the ink gleam. She did not look for a single word to absolve her; she felt the slow, certain understanding of what her signature had allowed: not a ritual enchantment to be undone, but a set of records that could be used, read, and argued over in a world that kept its own books.

Elaine's scar caught the lamplight as she stepped down from the lectern. She inclined her head to the Headmistress and then to Rue. "Know which ledgers you let count you into," she said simply, then left as quietly as she had come.

The scribe wiped his hands and closed the magnifier. The Folio was shut for a moment, heavy and unyielding. The Ink Room's door closed on the quiet scraping and the whisper of pages turning—on the tally that would be read again and again. Rue left the room with none of the neat, ritual absolution she had sought. She carried, instead, the ledger's new weight: the knowledge that the Academy's myths were recorded in ink and that ink had a way of calling accounts due.
