Counsel met in the hall beneath a lamp that had seen a hundred decisions and would see a few more. Rue came not as supplicant but as guarantor, her palms stained with ink from the hours she had pledged. The Folio lay open and the Pending Release column gleamed like a new seam.

The Headmistress called the council to order with a motion of her hand that had shut down arguments before and would have to hold them now. The clerks drew up the petition lists while the scribes placed witness slates and stamped paper where signatures would be taken. Outside, the quay still smoked; inside, the house was a machine set to make a single kind of work visible.

Rue watched as the clerk passed the first stack to the Headmistress. Each petition was a tight thing: a name, a sponsor reference where present, the clerk's note that an exchange had been logged as 'satisfied by civic exchange' in the sponsorship ledger. The language was careful—'Approved, pending sponsor/municipal acknowledgment'—but the effect of the ink felt immediate. It made space.

When the Headmistress read the first verdict aloud the Hall leaned forward. "By counsel and witness," she intoned, "the petitions listed under Pending Release are approved for provisional release pending sponsor or municipal acknowledgment. The house provides escort; the city will be notified of the civic exchange recorded." Her voice was both sentinel and scribe.

A clerk stamped the first petition with a seal that echoed the sound of a small, decisive coin hitting a table. Rue's guarantor line—her own pledged hours—sat beneath the approved names in a neat clerk's hand. The backing paper was folded and carried to the Ink Room where another scribe made a copy, drew a witness line, and slid it into the Archive under the stamped witness. The mechanical economy of paperwork turned and turned: signature, seal, record.

Eleven petitions were read; eleven names received the provisional stamp. It was not a final freedom—there remained signatures to be gathered, municipal ratification to be sought—but it was a change from the sealed state of the Folio: entries had moved from margin to motion. The Amber Bell, used for more than ceremonies now, was rung once—low, an acoustic stitch of decision—and the sound spread like a ledger being closed on one page and opened on another.

Rue felt each approved name as both gift and subtraction. The rota boards had already been altered: apprenticeship hours redistributed, rota columns narrowed, ration tickets adjusted. The cost was literal—less time at the bench for many, more labor for others—and it was moral: Rue's pledged hours, inked in the Folio, would be drawn down over months as she kept the workshops running for those who stayed.

Elaine watched the proceedings with a steady, private patience. When one of the approved petitions listed a woman who had once sat beside Maris in the gardens, Maris's hand found Elaine's and squeezed. Sal stood apart, watching the clerk's pen glide: she did not smile.

One apprentice—small, breathy—stepped forward when the name that touched her friend was read. The Headmistress offered a quiet nod and the clerk called an escort: two women from the guard and a scribe to carry the petition copy. The escort would not ferry them into freedom unobserved; it would be a measured walk toward a quay where sponsors might sign and municipal officers might stamp. The apprentice's eyes found Rue's and, in the briefest of sounds, said, "For now?"

Rue could only fold the girl's hands into hers and answer with a small, certain pressure. "For now," she said, and meant the provisional part: that the ledger could be the instrument of release but that release would be argued in other rooms as well.

After the petitions were stamped, the Headmistress set a counsel date—three days out, as she had promised—and ordered the clerks to prepare a public summary to be read to the benches. The clerk wrote the lines: 'Civic pledge honoured; Pending Release column updated; eleven petitions provisionally approved; municipal notification requested.' The words would sit in the ledger and be carried out to the city and, with luck and argument, would pull names through.

Rue left the Folio with ink on her fingers and a weight on her shoulders that felt like the weight of a coin pressed into pocket. She returned to the bench not to resume her old life but to begin the work she had promised: extra hours at the loom and the bench, teaching those whose names remained in the house, smoothing stitches as if she could mend ledger lines with thread.

In the weeks that followed the island changed the way islands do: schedules bent, markets thinned, travel across the water was measured by permits and stamped papers. The Headmistress's office filled with civic ledgers and petition slips; clerks moved between rooms like a city's arteries.

Rue's days narrowed and lengthened. She taught at the bench with a calm she had built over years; she kept a register of hours she had pledged and watched the tally fall. Aya, who had tended herbs and wounds, turned some of her remedies into small parcels that went with the escorted women—gifts that smelled of rosemary and steadiness. Loren's hands ached less with age than with the work of keeping a seam from tearing; he stayed at Rue's side, a steadying presence in the cramped and necessary arithmetic of care.

When the first of the escorted women returned with municipal acknowledgment—a small signature on a municipal scroll, a sponsor's stamped endorsement—she left the quay quietly, no trumpet, no pamphlet. The scribe had written 'Approved—satisfied by civic exchange' beside her name in the ledger; the clerk had ticked the Approved column. The Amber Bell rang softly in the Hall the evening her departure was noted; the tone was not triumph but a measured sealing.

Not all petitions would be approved. Some sponsors refused to accept the civic exchange; others required time the city could not yet grant. Those names remained inked and waiting in the Pending Release column. The Folio, patient as ever, held both sorts of entries with the same indifferent neatness.

Rue lived her last months in that steady place between counting and mercy. She watched those who left walk down the quay under escort and noted their names in her private notebook—the family ledger she had kept since her first decision. She wrote little things: 'Anna—approved—left 3rd month; lesson count: 24 hours pledged and served.' She made sure the records she touched were honest.

When the season bent toward autumn, the Headmistress asked Rue to ring the Amber Bell once at the year's closing—an honor in which Rue felt both pride and the ache of price. She struck the bell and the note settled over the Hall like a ledger clause closing for a day. The Three—Elaine, Maris, Sal—stood together for a moment in the doorway, each with a different expression: acceptance, a tremor of cost, silence.

Rue's last act in the Hall was not a speech but a folded paper handed to the clerk: a brief note, her final guarantor accounting—hours pledged and hours owed, a tally she had kept with meticulous care. The clerk took it and slid it into the Folio's Pending Release column with a witness stamp. It was not a dramatic gesture. It was a record.

Outside, the island had lost some of its bustle; inside, its pages had gained a new column. The ending was not the undoing she might have wished nor the full absolution she had once hoped for. It was, instead, a ledger in motion: inked entries, conditional releases, and a slow reweaving of life. Rue walked the glasshouses at dusk, hands empty of binding seals and full of stitches, and understood that bargains leave marks. She had made one more tally—a public act that might let some go—and she had taken the cost upon herself.

What the island owed its people, and what they owed themselves for the choices they made in desperation, would be argued in counsel and in years to come. For Rue, the reckoning had become a daily stitch: to teach, to count honestly, and to keep the ledger from taking more than it should. The Folio recorded the new path; the Amber Bell kept time; and the island, which had been a circled life, had edges that would now be read and negotiated.
