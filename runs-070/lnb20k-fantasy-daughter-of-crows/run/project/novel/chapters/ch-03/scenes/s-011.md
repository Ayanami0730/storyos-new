Rue had taken to standing near the lectern when there were affairs of weight in the Hall; it put the harbor in the windows behind her and let her watch both the city and the house at once. The benches were full in a different way than before—faces alert, fingers folded, a dozen small cloths already smoothed for someone who might be called to hurry.

The War Envoy moved with the same patient, economy she had seen at the steps: precise, unadorned, his coat buttoned against the cold. He did not carry a banner; he carried a notice and a manner that read like a law. When he spoke he did not ask for entry so much as present it, as if the war itself had already written the words for him.

"The island is drawn into the campaign," he said, voice even. "Our supply lines shorten and our needs increase. We come not to take from you blind, but to ask of what can be measured and offered. In time, claims will be made for bonds or levies; in time, support of manpower may be required. We expect counsel and an answer in the period given."

The phrasing was careful—no single demand yet, only the architecture of demands. It was worse for being schematic. Rue heard the list and did the arithmetic: bonds; levies; manpower. Each word landed like a weight placed on the Hall's long table.

The Headmistress heard it the way she heard everything—slowly, as if testing a measure against a rule. She did not blush and she did not bristle; she arranged her reply with the precision of a clerk. "The Mercy Academy is a house of instruction and shelter," she said, palms folded at her wrist. "We can muster kitchens, offer shelter, and keep neutrality within our walls for those under our charge. We will not give names of our girls as a commodity."

There was a small, audible intake in the benches at her refusal. The Envoy's face did not change; his eyes were the unblinking calculation of a ledger. "Neutrality may be of value, but neutrality is accounted for. Kitchens and store are matters of grain and pot; shelter is matter of space and time. The city will ask for something in return—guarantees that sustain the war effort."

The Headmistress's reply turned soft at the edges but not conciliatory. "We will account for what we can spare. We will offer kitchens and shelter as humanitarian assistance. Where civic claims ask for manpower, we will consider how best to meet obligations without breaking our trust to the house's charge. We will not hand over our charges as raw conscription."

It was a bargain wrapped in a refusal and a promise. Rue heard the difference between the two as plainly as if she had been taught to read ink: the Headmistress protected the house's people by moving the payment to accounting—by promising to make a ledger of what the house would give. She would trade counted things, not names.

Someone in the benches could not hold it. A young apprentice—no more than a handful of years beneath the Academy's roof—stood before the Headmistress as if the Hall's ritual let her speak. Her voice trembled, small and raw: "If they come for people, what will we do? What will happen to those with ten years left?"

Her question landed like a thin bell. The Headmistress's eyes softened, and for the first time Rue saw a flash of the woman behind the gloves. "We will shelter those under our charge," the Headmistress said, and there was a syllable in her tone that was promise. Then she added, immediately, with the same clerkly precision: "And we will account it. We will keep a record—who is housed, who is fed, what is pledged. Those records will be how we bargain." The words were both comfort and contract.

The Envoy inclined his head. "Records are useful," he said. "They make claims legible. They will be considered in counsel."

Rue felt a small, private panic she could not put to work. The Headmistress had promised shelter and had promised the ledger. To Rue the two things looked less like alternatives than a single equation: protection in exchange for accounting. The house would not hide; it would count and present its counts for negotiation.

Voices in the benches rose in soft clusters—apprentices whispering, an older woman saying a name as if to test how it sounded under threat. A clerk moved quietly, taking notes. The Hall's machinery engaged itself with the practical speed of a practiced loom: lists were pulled, rotas rethought, kitchens inventoried. Rue watched fingers lift and cross names, the same gestures she had learned in apprenticeship now repurposed for an adult calculus.

Loren stood at the back, sleeves rolled despite the sudden formality, and his jaw set in a way Rue had seen when a stitch would not hold. He gave her a small look—no words—an urging to keep steady and to be counted among those who treated work as defense.

The Envoy's party left as they had come: clean lines, small steps, and a deadline that had the taste of arithmetic in it. He had not named a levy or set a figure; he had only shown how such things might be asked for—bonds, levies, manpower—left to the city's counsel. The Headmistress had named the house's boundaries and promised shelter in exchange for careful accounting.

Rue walked down the aisle after the guests, trailing behind the Headmistress's shadow. The Great Hall felt narrower and more urgent. She thought of the sewn seam of contracts, of the marginal note that had said 'tethering,' of the way the house made its people legible in ink.

Outside, the harbor's air smelled of tar and a hint of smoke on the wind. The Envoy's counsel had not broken the Academy; it had made clear the terms under which the Academy might be compelled. Rue understood, with a sudden, bitter clarity, that the ledger could be pulled into another ledger and that protection might cost a different sort of accounting. The war, in its quiet arithmetic, had already begun to take its place on the page.
