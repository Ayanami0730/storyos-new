I arrived with my pockets full of nothing and the honest calculation that followed. Two coins would stretch two days at the market; one bed at the lodging house meant a back that never stopped aching and a mouth that never stopped asking. There are neat words for bravery and grander sentences for sacrifice. I had twenty years' worth of sentences inside me. What I had in the moment was hunger and arithmetic. So I sold myself.

The Ceremony Hall smelled of beeswax polish and boiled linen: the kind of clean that announces itself rather than earning quiet trust. Light came through high windows in long, patient bars. The hundred benches were arranged like a small amphitheatre; families sat in the outer rings, sober as undertakers. In the middle, on a low table, lay the Folio and the Contract: the Folio a wide, dark book with a ribbon that had once been more important than it was now; the Contract folded into a small, precise packet, the paper rubbed where hands had touched it for decades.

The Folio was open where a scribe had left it, a pale rectangle of invitation: “Folio Entry — Name: __________________; Age: __; Date: ______; Intake: One hundred; Provenance (sold/placed/orphaned): __________; Signature: __________________; Witness: Headmistress / Scribe.” I read it while they adjusted the sash at the back of my shirt. Reading paperwork is a kind of prayer for people like me.

They called the intake. The Bell — a single bronze note struck from a high arch — cut the murmurs and put us under the House's attention. I counted the sound more as an accountant than a believer: one clear note for the beginning, then the low chorus of the Headmistress's voice as she took the liturgy of charity down the line.

The Headmistress was everything polished with use. She wore mercy as other people wear good coats — tailored to fit, and kept for public weather. Her voice carried over the benches in a soft, precise tide. She read the facts aloud in the way a town clerk reads a deed.

"Out of the hundred who enter each year," she said, "only three leave at the end of their ten-year term. The rest remain under the Academy's charge as is recorded." There was no scolding there, only the small hardness of a truth stated so the room could register it. I had expected myth; I had not expected arithmetic.

When she closed, she laid the policy of the House into a sentence that would follow all our names, and repeated the line that the Folio itself required be heard: "So entered under the charge of the Mercy Academy. Let the Folio stand as record and law. Let those within this house remember that mercy is accounted for by service and attention, not by indulgence." The scribe moved an inkwell with ceremonious care, and the Contract was handed to me.

There are ways to refuse that sound like refusal and ways that are simple. I did not make a show of hesitating. I thought of the lodging house's thin broth, the cough from the woman who shared my bed, the child who asked for a scrap she would not get. I thought of work under a roof that would not change its mind because a winter came early.

The Contract's clause was quiet in the packet but not shy: ten years. The writing named it without flourish — a span not measured in noble phrase but in the clear currency of years. I traced the number with a fingertip as the scribe held the folio steady. Ten years is a ledger entry; it is not a promise of comfort.

I signed in a hand that had practised keeping accounts in cramped ledgers. The pen felt like a small instrument of business. My name was a line, small and tidy. The scribe copied it into the Folio in ink that smelled of iron and permanence. The Headmistress dipped a fingertip and made a mark beside the signature — her witness. The page closed.

There was applause, the kind meant to be mindful. A girl near me cried once, sharp and immediate; nobody shushed her. Mourning and commerce live in the same house and know how to move around one another.

Outside the Hall the sun hit the Academy walls. Inside, the Folio rested where it would be put to the Archive later, an obvious book that contained, in its margins, the small remarks that always get made: nicknames, a note if someone arrived with a child, exceptions the institution had allowed itself. I had read enough of such margins to know that there are things only the Archive says aloud, and only later.

I walked out with a small packet in my palm and a larger accounting on my shoulders. The bargain I had made was practical and unsentimental: room, work, a roof, and ten years of measured claims. The Academy had the language for it all — intake, witness, folio, charge — and I had my signature. I had consented.

Later I would learn the ways the house keeps its numbers neat: how the Folio is consulted when a girl asks to leave, how the ledger rooms hold answer-keys to questions you do not yet have. For now, the decision that mattered was simple and cold as coin: I had fewer options than the day before and one guaranteed where-to-sleep. My humor about it was small and private. I told myself the trade was sensible.

When the bronze note sounded again, it sounded like a clock. The Bell finished the ceremony and put the House back into its ordinary rhythm. I folded the Contract into my pocket the way you fold a receipt for a transaction that you do not plan to return. That night, when I lay in my clean bed for the first time in months, I thought of the Folio's blank lines and the Headmistress's closing line, and I felt the arithmetic of my choice settle down into the room: a tidy sum, a term, and a witness.
