She had practiced the weight of the decision in the hours when the city around the island was nothing but a smear of smoke and doorframes: how it felt to fold a life into a ledger, to press one's name so it would not lift again.

The Great Hall smelled of polished wood and boiled tea. Late light came in through the high windows, falling in ruled bars across the benches where a hundred girls sat like a counted thing. Rue stood at the end of one bench, the packet of papers heavy in her hands, the Contract itself wrapped in waxed cloth. It was thinner than she had imagined and thicker than the promise in her head.

Across the room the Headmistress waited behind the Folio, her gloves folded calmly at her wrist. The Folio lay open on a lectern: the page was a page older than the Hall, browned at the edges and written in a hand that did not tire. A template was printed, the heading a tidy law of the house.

“Folio Entry — Name: __________________; Age: __; Date: ______; Intake: One hundred; Provenance (sold/placed/orphaned): __________; Signature: __________________; Witness: Headmistress / Scribe.”

Rue read the printed line the way one reads an account book: as if the blanks were already waiting to be filled. She had no number to write for age she would make herself a number out of the look the clerk gave her and the soft way the girl beside her kept folding the corner of her sleeve. At home the ledger had been smaller, a family book with a soft spine where debts were rounded to a sum and forgiveness was a pencilled margin. She could see the spine now, the cramped handwriting of someone who kept track of coughs and missed dinners as if they were the same thing.

The Contract's clause was quiet in the packet but not shy: ten years. The words sat like a coin at the bottom of a bowl — something to be counted and not to be touched. The Contract promised education and shelter and apprenticeship, the learning of trades and the promise of a place to stand after ten years. In the margins—near the witness line—a single cramped note had been made in an ink older than the paper. It read, in a hand that looked like the hands in the Ink Room had always used, tethering.

When the Headmistress spoke, she spoke the Academy’s sentence as if it were both a comfort and a ledger entry.

“Out of the hundred who enter each year, only three leave at the end of their ten-year term. The rest remain under the Academy's charge as is recorded.”

Her voice had no need for ornaments. It sat in the room the way the folio did: present, hard, final. The girls shifted. Rue felt the ledger of their breathing shift with them, a tide that counted itself in the quiet between words.

The Headmistress closed the folio with a practiced gentleness and read the ceremonial lines that would be ink and law.

“So entered under the charge of the Mercy Academy. Let the Folio stand as record and law. Let those within this house remember that mercy is accounted for by service and attention, not by indulgence.”

A scribe dipped the pen. Rue dipped her own hand into the little bowl of water that was meant to clear the nib, palms damp from the autumn cold on the ferry. She tasted metal on her tongue and thought of the ferryman's hat, of a coin sliding from fingers that did not want to part with it but had to. She did not write her age. She hesitated, feeling the shape of her life as if it were a ledger to be balanced: a small credit here, a small debt there. At home debts had a smell—musty and faintly medicinal—and the memory of that was what had put her in the Hall today.

When she set the pen to the Contract she wrote 'sold' in the space the Folio called Provenance. The nib slipped a little as if surprised by the honesty of the word. She signed her name with a hand that would make do.

The Binding Seal waited on the table, a mound of beeswax and a small iron signet beside a bottle that smelled of tincture. Even in the dry light she could tell it smelled of beeswax and a bitter iron tang. The scribe pressed the wax, the signet sank, and for a sliver of a second Rue felt as if a small chord had tightened around the place where her wrists would rest.

The Amber Bell was on the table, a warm piece of glass-grey amber held in a loop of brass. Someone — the Headmistress or the scribe, Rue could not see past the lectern — took it up. The sound it made was not bright so much as decisive. It cut across the room like a stamp.

"By amber and ink, so sealed," someone intoned, low enough that its syllables felt like a private thing. The bell answered, and its note spread: a single clear ring that seemed to set the page of the Folio into a new place.

Rue felt the weight of the signet at her back and the thinness of the money in her pocket. The Headmistress's hand hovered over the witness line and then pressed her glove to the Folio as if that touch itself were a closing entry in a book. The girls around Rue moved as if they had been measured and set; some smiled slightly, a nervous accounting of relief. A few did not move at all.

When the clerk folded the Contract away, tucking it in the same waxed cloth, Rue did not know whether she had chosen mercy or ledger. She only knew what she had traded: a name, a year, the small liberties of an unrecorded life. Her heel scraped the floor as she left the bench; the Hall kept going, businesslike and slow, the Folio closed like a chest.

Outside, the gardens smelled of late roses and salt. Rue walked toward the ferry with the packet safe against her ribs. The bell's note sat underneath everything she felt — a seal, a promise, a count that would always remember where to place her.
