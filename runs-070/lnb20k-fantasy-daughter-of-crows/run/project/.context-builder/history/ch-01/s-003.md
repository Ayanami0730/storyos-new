### world-rules  <world/rules.yaml>

These govern what is TRUE. They do not say what anyone KNOWS.
A character knows a rule only where canon says so; otherwise they may be ignorant of it, wrong about it, or in the middle of working it out — and if the story is about someone discovering one of these, writing it into their thoughts early is how that story gets destroyed.

- Every year, the Mercy Academy acquires exactly one hundred girls; that number is an institutional constant and must appear in the story's facts.
- After a ten-year period within the Academy, only three individuals ever leave under normal circumstances; this must be stated and preserved as a factual statistic within the narrative.
- Rue entered the Mercy Academy by selling herself; her consent and the Contract she signed must be an established fact in her backstory.
- A war arrives and reaches the Academy's gates during Rue's later years; the war must affect events at the Academy and force reckonings with its past.
- The Folio and the Contract are authoritative objects inside the Academy: they record intake and the terms of binding. Any scene that refers to who is allowed to leave must trace to these artifacts or to a documented exception.
- Do not contradict the above institutional facts anywhere in the story; if new facts about the Academy's practices are introduced, they must be consistent with the above constraints or be revealed as covert exceptions with provenance in the Archive.

### previous-scene  <novel/chapters/ch-01/scenes/s-002.md>

The dormitory smelled like the underside of a roof in rain and the sharp sour of too-white soap. Light came in strips between high windows and fell on rows of narrow two-tier bunks, trunks shoved under the lower pallets as if storage and sleep had been married in haste. A plank floor muttered when you walked; voices moved around it like careful traffic.

They showed us where to put our things with the brisk courtesy of people who have practiced charity until it reads like business. A wooden board stood by the entry, a neat column of names written in the scribe's small hand. "Assigned by staff," the woman who led us said when one new girl asked how it had been decided. The board held our numbers; people counted themselves into a place like a census.

A thin list had been taken from the Folio and pinned below the names — not the full book, only the day's intake transcribed into a narrow column. I read for the sake of knowing where I sat: my name, my witness mark, an assigned bunk number. The Folio's presence was public enough for us to see it; the Archive would take the book later, the woman said, but we had the list.

The Bell's note came through the open doorway like a hand laid on your shoulder. It sounded for meals; it sounded for lessons; it sounded for curfew. You learned the bell as you learned a new gait: it made your stomach empty at noon and made your hands set to work at the sound as if the noise were a summons you could not refuse.

I met three of those who would be nearest me. Lin put her trunk on the bunk above mine and pronounced the thing efficient. She had the clever hands of someone used to making do. "If you pack flat," she said, folding my shirts like she was teaching someone to fold a map, "you get more room for what matters."

Mara watched us from the far bunk with a mouth that thought faster than her eyes did. She had a way of folding facts into a rasp that often left people off balance. "Don't trust the board," she said. "It tells what they want recorded. We tell each other the rest."

Kela did not speak at first. She sat on the edge of her lower bunk and watched the room as if counting the breaths of everyone in it. When she finally spoke it was quiet and accurate. "We have chores tomorrow," she said. "Rota's up on the board. Garden and laundry in the morning. Kitchen in the afternoon."

Chores were given by a rota posted beneath the intake list: garden, laundry, kitchen, and the little jobs that always sit beneath those names — sweeping, mending, running for the scribe. The rota smelled of wet paper and order. There was no flourish to it. Work came down in columns; your name under a task was a contract of a different sort.

Discipline showed itself in small ways: the softness of the woman's voice when she told us what to do, the careful way the head of the dorm would mark a failing with a line on a ledger we could not see, the curt remark that carried the edge of consequence. The House's kindness and its correction lived together like neighbors who keep different hours.

At the edge of that afternoon some of us convened in a dull circle — a stray handful who had not yet learned the economy of friendship within a hundred. Lin brought bread. Mara told a story about a girl who had tried to bargain out a rota and spent a week in the scullery for her efforts. Kela only listened, and then leaned forward and said to me, plainly, "Keep your head down and your hands busy. People notice that less than they notice your mouth."

I kept my contract in the inside pocket of my shirt and my humor small. There was private comfort in the mathematics of it: a bed, a packet of work, a schedule you could set your watch by. I liked order the way you like a clean ledger. It meant fewer surprises, and you could plan small economies inside it — how many minutes to a chore, how many saved spoons in a week.

When the Bell rang for curfew the dorm folded itself tight. The trunks shoved under the bunks like animals settling in. The board under the entry light showed our names in their neat column and gave me a particular, domestic kind of relief. The Academy's public face — the Folio, the scribe's hand, the Headmistress's closing line — had been one thing. The dormitory made that public mercy look like a business whose balance must be kept.

I slept that night with the plank floor under me and three new people breathing close. The rules of the House had been set out plainly: work, witness, record. The ways it was enforced would be learned later; for now the rota and the list were enough to tell me where I stood.
