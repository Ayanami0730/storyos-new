### world-rules  <world/rules.yaml>

These govern what is TRUE. They do not say what anyone KNOWS.
A character knows a rule only where canon says so; otherwise they may be ignorant of it, wrong about it, or in the middle of working it out — and if the story is about someone discovering one of these, writing it into their thoughts early is how that story gets destroyed.

- The Academy accepts exactly one hundred girls into a cohort each year; this number is public and rigid in the story's social systems.
- Historically, after a ten-year period inside the Academy, only three individuals have left and their departures are recorded in the Folio (this historical fact is established early and treated as canon unless explicitly changed by a scena-level event with provenance).
- Contracts the recruits sign are legally and magically binding when sealed with the Binding Seal; the Seal enacts the Academy's claims but requires the Academy's own rituals to be maintained.
- Magic has a cost: any enchantment that binds a person's freedom draws from the same ledger as the Folio and must be accounted for in the Academy's records; bargains cannot be silently created or negated without record.
- The War Envoy represents external political/military forces; he may demand terms, levy burdens, or threaten force, but he cannot invent or erase Academy records — only expose and exploit them.
- The Folio truthfully records entries and departures; it can be read and misread, hidden, or weaponised, but altering historical entries is itself an action with consequences and must be shown on-page when it happens.
- The Headmistress is powerful within the Academy but not omnipotent; she must work within political constraints, her staff and the Academy's rituals.
- No deus ex machina: solutions to the Academy's bindings must arise from revealed rules and the characters' established resources and relationships, not from unexplained new powers.

### previous-scene  <novel/chapters/ch-01/scenes/s-002.md>

The list was already up when we came in: a narrow column of names pinned beneath the Folio’s page, the rest of the book gone for the afternoon as if it were too heavy for public eyes.

Light came in strips between high windows and fell on rows of narrow two-tier bunks, trunks shoved under the lower pallets as if storage and sleep had been married in haste. A plank floor muttered when you walked; voices moved around it like careful traffic. The dormitory smelled like the underside of a roof in rain and the sharp sour of too-white soap.

Loren was there at the doorway, a broad shape in a sober coat, palms folded over a sheaf of paper. He did not call names from the Folio; he watched the room with the slow economy of someone who measured a thing by what it made of itself. When a girl hesitated under the list he stepped forward and, without a smile, tapped the rota pinned beneath the board and said the word that set a place: “Garden.”

Rue had a place that night between two trunks and a girl who kept folding the corner of her sleeve until it was nervous and neat. The girl introduced herself as Aya with a grin that came quick and a little startled; she dropped her trunk on the lower bunk opposite and sat cross-legged as if she had practiced being comfortable in any small space. She kept one hand in her hem, palm wrapped around something dry and small; when she showed it a moment later it was only a crust of bread she had hidden like a private coin.

"Keep your head down and your hands busy," Aya said before Rue could find anything to ask. Her voice hopped the room like a coin. Small things about her—how she turned a question into a joke, the bright angle of her laugh—made Rue notice movement before lineaments. Aya's fingers worried the crust and then tucked it away again, as if saving both the bread and the gesture.

Supper came with the Bell's clear note. The ring cut across our chatter and, as always, words from the Hall followed—ceremony turned to schedule—and we moved like a practiced thing toward the long table. Food was ladled into tin plates and placed before us; the scrape of spoons and the sighs at the edges of the meal sounded like the work the house expected.

After, under the wash of lamplight, the head of dorm stepped into the middle of the room with a blanket folded into a tidy square. She held it up like a tutor showing a diagram and said, "Watch and learn—this keeps the bed from taking you in the night."

A girl near the foot of the bunks, fingers clumsy from travel, reached to copy the fold and the head of dorm took her hands between weathered palms, guiding each finger with a quick, correcting pressure. "Not so soft," the woman said, breath warm and faintly scented of starch. Her nails scraped the canvas of the blanket as she tucked and smoothed. "Pull the corner under, see? Snap the edge so it holds. You won't have a child crawling over you because you left a flabby corner." The girl tried again; the blanket snapped into a square under the woman's hands and a small laugh escaped her—embarrassed and proud at once.

The correction was small and private in its cruelty: the head's hand left a warmth where it had been and the girl sat straighter, fingers clean and more careful. Rue watched the scrape of the woman's nails and the way the light hit the new square. Discipline showed itself then not as a rule written in a book but as a practice applied to a body—fingers guided, a corner folded, a laugh curbed into habit.

When curfew came the dorm folded itself tight. The trunks shoved under the bunks like animals settling in. The board under the entry light showed our names in their neat column and gave Rue a particular, domestic kind of relief. The Bell rang and the room went small and immediate; the noise of the world beyond the garden walls was reduced to the soft hiss of someone breathing.

Aya leaned toward Rue across the narrow blanket and, without drama, said, "Don't trust the board. It tells what they want recorded. We tell each other the rest."

There was a story shared in the low corners of the bunks that night—Mara's story—about a girl who had tried to bargain out a rota and spent a week in the scullery for her trouble. The quiet that followed was not comfort but accounting.

Before sleep Rue felt the Folio as if it were a presence: a book that had been read and then closed, its list copied and put where we could see it. Someone had taken a thin list from the Folio and pinned it below the names; not the full book, only the day's intake transcribed into a narrow column. The Folio's presence was public enough for us to see it; the Archive would take the book later, the woman who posted the list had said, but we had the list.

Rue fell asleep with Aya's hand warm by her elbow and the Bell's clear note sitting under all that closeness like a seal. In the dark she thought of how the house counted things: work, witness, record. It felt precise and heavy and, even then, like something you could trade a day of yourself for and never quite buy back.
