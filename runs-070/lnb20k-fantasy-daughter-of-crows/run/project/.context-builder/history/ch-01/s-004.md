### world-rules  <world/rules.yaml>

These govern what is TRUE. They do not say what anyone KNOWS.
A character knows a rule only where canon says so; otherwise they may be ignorant of it, wrong about it, or in the middle of working it out — and if the story is about someone discovering one of these, writing it into their thoughts early is how that story gets destroyed.

- The Academy accepts exactly one hundred girls into a cohort each year; this number is public and rigid in the story's social systems.
- Historically, after a ten-year period inside the Academy, only three individuals have left and their departures are recorded in the Folio (this historical fact is established early and treated as canon unless explicitly changed by a scena-level event with provenance).
- Contracts the recruits sign are legally and magically binding when sealed with the Binding Seal; the Seal enacts the Academy's claims but requires the Academy's own rituals to be maintained.
- Magic has a cost: any enchantment that binds a person's freedom draws from the same ledger as the Folio and must be accounted for in the Academy's records; bargains cannot be silently created or negated without record.
- The War Envoy represents external political/military forces; he may demand terms, levy burdens, or threaten force, but he cannot invent or erase Academy records — only expose and exploit them.
- The Folio truthfully records entries and departures; it can be read and misread, hidden, or weaponised, but altering historical entries is itself an action with consequences and must be shown on-page when it happens.
- The Headmistress is powerful within the Academy but not omnipotent; she must work within political constraints, her staff and the Academy's rituals.
- No deus ex machina: solutions to the Academy's bindings must arise from revealed rules and the characters' established resources and relationships, not from unexplained new powers.

### previous-scene  <novel/chapters/ch-01/scenes/s-003.md>

Loren's classroom sat where the workshops did: an honest room of a repaired floor and benches that had belonged to other hands before ours. The air smelled of cut wood and iron, and the needle's thumb felt like a metronome tapping the lesson into your palm.

He put a length of rough cloth before Rue and said, without ceremony, "Pull tighter; the seam keeps its promise if you keep your edge." The words were dry as a rule—no sermon, just a sentence that told you what the trade expected. Rue bent her head and let her fingers learn the rhythm: needle in, thread through, pull until the cloth lay like a promise kept.

Loren moved between the benches with the slow, patient economy of someone used to making shortcuts into habits. His hand found Rue's wrist once to turn her stitch, the motion small and correcting. "Not from the elbow," he said, tapping the joint with the blunt of his thumb. "From here." He tapped the base of her palm and the sound of wood on wood punctuated the point. The bench clacked when someone shifted and the room held the noise like a ledger taking an entry.

By the time the lesson changed to the gardens, Rue could count stitches the way she had once counted lop-sided coins: careful, automatic. The gardens were a patchwork of taught beds and wild hedgerows where Loren liked to test patience as much as skill. He showed them how to prune a rose so that it would not take the whole branch—a small, decisive cut that demanded attention to the plant's lean.

"Measure twice, cut once," Loren said, the phrase bringing a boyhood proverb into these beds. He handed Rue a small knife with a worn handle and had her find the right angle, watching the way her jaw set when she decided. "You make that cut as if you're writing into a ledger; you mean it."

They walked the beds with rows of students trailing like a counted thing. Some learned quickly; others learned the lesson of patience by breaking a bud or misplacing a branch and being made to rebind the trellis for an hour. Rue felt the pattern of rules braid into the work: the bell would call lessons and meals, the rota would ask for garden shifts, and every measured cut came with the whisper of consequence.

The Ink Room was not a classroom so much as a thing the house kept in its throat. When they led us there the door was a slab below the library's stacks, heavy and unadorned. Loren did not open it; a scribe's hand, quick and exact, showed us the glass along the inner case where the Binding Seal rested.

Behind the glass the seal looked like a small, round relic, a mound of beeswax capped by a dark iron signet. Rue remembered the smell more than the sight—something of beeswax and a bitter iron tang rose up from her memory of signing. The scribe's finger tapped the display with a care that felt like punctuation; no one touched the seal. Loren's voice, low, said only what could be said: "You see its order. You do not lift the seal."

Stories about the Three were told to us as if they were both threat and promise. In the long hours in the benches and the beds, they came up like smoke: Elaine's name said as a small, astonished hope—she left and carried a scar that meant she had paid; Maris mentioned as a tidy example, the kind the House liked to point at; Sal's name dropped like a cool stone meant to be avoided: leave unasked and the Academy will not be kind.

They were a mantra and a warning in different mouths. One girl said, almost reverent, "Elaine came back to show how it might be done." Another rolled her eyes and said, "Maris is a pamphlet; they've sold her story well enough." A boyish mimic in the corner made Sal's departure into a gesture of secrecy and the room swallowed the laugh that followed it. The Three meant more in the telling than in any fact: miracle, commodity, closed mouth.

In the afternoons, Rue practiced stitches until her hands were small machines. She mended hems and learned the economy of a neat seam: how a stitch could hold a pocket for a coin and how an extra thread could be the difference between a trade well kept and a trade that cost you a reprimand. Sometimes she would sew with one eye on the Ink Room door, imagining the scribe's neat hand and the seal behind the glass. Each time she threaded her needle she felt the ledger close a fraction tighter.

There were compromises that started as small private things. Once, while binding a scrap for the apprentices' chest, Rue let another girl's thread stand because the other girl had been late to class and begged—Rue's hands moved to hide the omission in the seam and, when Loren passed, he gave her a look that held both instruction and calculation. He did not scold; he slid a marked tally into the others' ledger with a pen whose scratch she could not see. Rue felt the quick weight of owing.

So the lessons kept to their trades and to their counts. The work taught skill; the house taught measurement. Rue learned both: to pull the seam tight, to choose the cut that would not demand a second one, to fold a ledger's worth of small favors into the margin of a day. By the time the sun left the gardens and the benches cooled, she knew the rhythm of the place—how a life could be practiced into order, how giving a stitch spared a reprimand, how every small kindness could be recorded as a debt.

Walking back through the courtyard with her palms smelling faintly of sap and stitch, Rue thought of the seal behind glass and of the Three's stories braided into the beds. The Academy taught its girls to measure things precisely: angles and seams, work and witness. It also taught them to measure what they were willing to lose.

She kept her hands busy and, as always, the ledger watched.
