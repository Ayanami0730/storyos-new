### world-rules  <world/rules.yaml>

These govern what is TRUE. They do not say what anyone KNOWS.
A character knows a rule only where canon says so; otherwise they may be ignorant of it, wrong about it, or in the middle of working it out — and if the story is about someone discovering one of these, writing it into their thoughts early is how that story gets destroyed.

- The Academy accepts exactly one hundred girls into a cohort each year; this number is public and rigid in the story's social systems.
- Historically, after a ten-year period inside the Academy, only three individuals have left and their departures are recorded in the Folio (this historical fact is established early and treated as canon unless explicitly changed by a scena-level event with provenance).
- Contracts the recruits sign are legally and magically binding when sealed with the Binding Seal; the Seal enacts the Academy's claims but requires the Academy's own rituals to be maintained.
- Magic has a cost: any enchantment that binds a person's freedom draws from the same ledger as the Folio and must be accounted for in the Academy's records; bargains cannot be silently created or negated without record.
- The War Envoy represents external political/military forces; he may demand terms, levy burdens, or threaten force, but he cannot invent or erase Academy records — only expose and exploit them.
- The Folio truthfully records entries and departures; it can be read and misread, hidden, or weaponised, but altering historical entries is itself an action with consequences and must be shown on-page when it happens.
- The Headmistress is powerful within the Academy but not omnipotent; she must work within political constraints, her staff and the Academy's rituals.
- No deus ex machina: solutions to the Academy's bindings must arise from revealed rules and the characters' established resources and relationships, not from unexplained new powers.

### previous-scene  <novel/chapters/ch-02/scenes/s-006.md>

The Hall had been dressed for ceremony: tapers in iron sconces, banners looped along the rafters, and a band of patrons in muted cloaks gathered where the benches broke for the lectern. The hundred names were not all present in person—the intake was a year complete now—but the room counted them with a single gesture: a long ledger closed on the lectern, its leather dulled and the Folio's page turned like a breath.

The Headmistress stood at the lectern with the amber bell beside her, a small, bright thing on a field of polished wood. A hush fell like a hand when she spoke; her voice did what it always did—smoothed the rough edges of debt and need into policy and purpose. "We mark the year by the work done and the hands taught," she said. "One hundred entered this household this term; they leave different by trade and by duty."

Praise and patronage moved through the room in neat phrases. A patron read a short commendation—charity turned to apprenticeship, coin turned into craft—and the benches responded with measured applause. Somewhere, a woman murmured Elaine's name the way people murmur of distant miracles; someone else lifted a hand in a quiet sign to Maris's tidy example; Sal's name hovered in the air like a warning and the room took it in without exclamation.

When the formal words closed, the Headmistress laid a hand on the Folio's binding and gave a slight, official nod. A scribe—small, quick-fingered—stepped forward and unlatched a clasp. "The ledger for this intake is closed and copied," the scribe announced in a voice linen-thin with formality. He ran a thumb along the edge of the page, and for all the room's music the action felt like a seam being finished. "A thin list will be kept for public record; the full Folio will be carried to the Archive for safekeeping."

Before the applause could rise, the Headmistress lifted her chin and gave the Hall a line that sat the myth beside the praise. "Across a decade," she said, voice crisp in the hush, "only three have been recorded as departing after ten years—Elaine, Maris, and Sal. Let their names stand in the Folio and in our memory as both testament and caution."

A bell rang—clear, decisive. Whether the Headmistress had lifted it herself or some other hand made it speak did not matter in that moment; the ringing set the Hall's hours into a firm place. The notes cut the air and the guests stood like ledgers balanced. The patron ceremony folded into smaller salutations and the murmuring thinned to business.

Loren found Rue at the edge of the benches, needle stilled in her palm as the world slid back into ordinary rules. He put a hand on her shoulder with the easy authority of someone who had always been the first to call a name. "The house will want apprentices assigned by sundown," he said, voice low. "Come to the registry table. You'll take three; make them hold the work."

At the long table, a scribe had unrolled a narrow log—its columns already filled with shifted names and notes. Loren tapped a column with a pen like a baton. "We take the new apprentices from the intake list and pair them with working benches," he said. "Sponsorship is noted in the logs; training hours recorded."

Rue watched as Loren called names and the scribe wrote them in a neat hand. The ritual was small and administrative: a name read, a nod, a pen scratch, a folded slip pinned to a trunk to mark where the apprentice would sleep. Rue felt the apparatus of the house tighten around her—reports, sponsorships, tallies—made visible in ink and slip.

When her turn came she stood at the table and took the slips offered: three folded pieces of paper, each named and numbered. They were anonymous in the way of registers—no grand introductions, only a shorthand that said: learn, serve, be counted. She folded her fingers around the paper and felt the weight like a coin.

Loren leaned close and, as he had a thousand times before in a workshop, set her hands on the first slip and showed her how to smooth the crease that would make the name a working thing. "Pull the stitching taught in the morning; make the seam hold so they can carry a pocket for a coin," he murmured. The instruction was both craft and custody: teach skill, take responsibility.

Rue walked the benches that afternoon with Loren at her elbow, the slips tucked into her apron like small ledgers. She met three girls in the sunlight that came through the high windows: a thin girl who kept her thumb inched into the hem of her sleeve, a broad-shouldered one whose hands were already callused, and a quiet one who watched with the slow calculation of a ledger. They did not speak much; names and work did the introducing. Rue thought of the ledger at home and the pages she had closed for a roof.

She set the girls to their benches, showed them the angle of a cut or the tension of a stitch, and marked their first hours in the house log with a neat pen stroke. Each time she wrote a name she felt a small tally accrue in the house's account and, with it, in her own. Apprenticeship was given as a trade; it was counted as an obligation.

By dusk, as the Hall emptied and candles guttered, Rue sat with the slips—three little folded ledgers of beginning—and felt the double edge of her work. The year's ledger had closed; the Folio would be carried away. She had, in the same movement, been entrusted with other names to hold and teach. The bell's note still seemed lodged under her ribs.

Loren's hand came to her shoulder once more. "Keep careful ledgers, Rue," he said. "Teach well. The house notices both."

She folded the final slip into the inside of her palm as if tucking away a coin. For the first time she understood how a life inside the Academy could become not only a way to be counted but a way to count others. She left the Hall with the slips at her breast and the Folio's closed leather heavy in her mouth of memory—an accounting that had closed one book and opened three more.

### recent-scene-s-005  <novel/chapters/ch-02/scenes/s-005.md>

The Headmistress received Rue where the Hall narrowed toward the lectern, a place that always smelled faintly of old varnish and ink. The benches had been pushed back; only the Folio and the amber bell sat between them, a small island of ceremony in an otherwise empty room.

She stood when Rue arrived, gloves folded at her wrist, and offered a greeting that was all surface polish. "Rue," she said, "sit. We must speak plainly about what the Academy promises and what it asks in return."

The Headmistress's voice was the voice the town heard at the Folio: steady, public, untroubled by the particular. She unfolded the Academy's case in sentences that could have been typed into a pamphlet: rescue from hunger and danger, the conversion of debt into apprenticeship, the shelter of a roof and the promise of a trade. "We take the unsettled and make them steady," she said. "We teach; we provide; we expect service in return."

She touched the Folio as if to make the words physical. The ledger was closed, its leather worn; the amber bell sat like a punctuation on the lectern. "So entered under the charge of the Mercy Academy," she quoted, the ceremonial language finding its place in the room, "Let the Folio stand as record and law. Let those within this house remember that mercy is accounted for by service and attention, not by indulgence."

Rue listened the way she had learned to listen in the classrooms—quiet, counting the gaps between the sentences. The Headmistress's phrasing had a tidy moral arithmetic to it: charity for work, shelter for measure. It sounded, in the empty Hall, like a ledger balanced in public.

After the public lines the Headmistress smiled and folded a different hand: the calculation beneath the gloss. "The Contract's clause is simple in form: ten years," she said, tapping a finger against the binding of a paper packet on the lectern. "On its face we promise education, shelter, apprenticeship. In the margins, we record the particulars that make those promises practicable: orders of apprenticeship, sponsorships, and the logs the house keeps to see a term through." She did not read a margin; she only named it as if naming settled the fact.

Rue's mouth shaped a question and did not speak it. She thought of the sealed wax she had felt when she signed; the smell of beeswax had come back to her in the Hall like a small, unpleasant memory. The Headmistress noted the silence and, with the same polite economy, addressed the unasked concern.

"We are accountable to those who fund and to those who live under our care," she said. "The Binding Seal is not theatre. It makes claims enforceable when we must ensure a promise is kept. But enforcement is not a wolf at the door; it is a tool of reliability. We do not seek to make prisoners—rather, we seek to make citizens of trade and duty."

The words hung in the Hall like a set of scales. To the assembled public the sentence read as reassurance; to Rue the sentence read also as policy. There was a difference, a thinness of brightness: the Headmistress spoke of tools and reliability, and Rue heard the implied cost where a tool became tally.

She tried another angle. "How often is the Folio consulted for departures?" she asked, keeping her voice low so the question would not become a petition in the public hearing.

The Headmistress straightened and folded her hands. "The Folio is our record. Its pages are consulted when the house's obligations are complete and when there is cause for review. Departures are not casual things; they are accounted for and recorded when they are rightly earned."

There was a softness in the words that suggested process, a quiet insistence that the house's work was administrative and orderly. But Rue had seen the people's ledger at home, and she knew how a neat process can take a night's mercy and parcel it into a year's account.

When Rue rose to leave the Headmistress's hand closed on the Folio's edge, the fingers briefly covering the page as if to guard what was written there. The Headmistress offered Rue a small, plain benediction—"Learn; keep faith with the work; be of use"—and Rue took it the way one takes a receipt: practical, necessary, and not an answer.

Outside the Hall the gardens smelled of rose and sea. Rue walked away with the Headmistress's sentences turning under her tongue—rescue, apprenticeship, shelter—and with the memory of the Binding Seal's smell prickling at the back of her throat. The Hall had offered a public scripture and a private ledger; Rue felt the two hands of the place closing around the same accounting and left with more questions than she had when she entered.
