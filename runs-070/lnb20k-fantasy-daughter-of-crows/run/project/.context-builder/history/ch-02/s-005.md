### world-rules  <world/rules.yaml>

These govern what is TRUE. They do not say what anyone KNOWS.
A character knows a rule only where canon says so; otherwise they may be ignorant of it, wrong about it, or in the middle of working it out — and if the story is about someone discovering one of these, writing it into their thoughts early is how that story gets destroyed.

- The Academy accepts exactly one hundred girls into a cohort each year; this number is public and rigid in the story's social systems.
- Historically, after a ten-year period inside the Academy, only three individuals have left and their departures are recorded in the Folio (this historical fact is established early and treated as canon unless explicitly changed by a scena-level event with provenance).
- Contracts the recruits sign are legally and magically binding when sealed with the Binding Seal; the Seal enacts the Academy's claims but requires the Academy's own rituals to be maintained.
- Magic has a cost: any enchantment that binds a person's freedom draws from the same ledger as the Folio and must be accounted for in the Academy's records; bargains cannot be silently created or negated without record.
- The War Envoy represents external political/military forces; he may demand terms, levy burdens, or threaten force, but he cannot invent or erase Academy records — only expose and exploit them.
- The Folio truthfully records entries and departures; it can be read and misread, hidden, or weaponised, but altering historical entries is itself an action with consequences and must be shown on-page when it happens.
- The Headmistress is powerful within the Academy but not omnipotent; she must work within political constraints, her staff and the Academy's rituals.
- No deus ex machina: solutions to the Academy's bindings must arise from revealed rules and the characters' established resources and relationships, not from unexplained new powers.

### previous-scene  <novel/chapters/ch-01/scenes/s-004.md>

Rue kept the family ledger folded beneath the mattress of the ferry bed for a week after she left. At night she would open it by lamplight and trace the cramped lines, the pencilled sums and the pencilled coughs. At home the ledger had held everything that was small and urgent: a debt counted as if it were weather, a promise pencilled in the margin because it made the other debts easier to bear.

That was why she had written "sold" in the Provenance field, though she had thought the word like a coin you could not feel until it had gone. She had promised, not with a seal but with a whisper beside her mother's pillow, to make the numbers stop climbing. She had not said how many coins would be needed or the hours that would be taken; she only said the one practical thing that would move the sum: "I will stand where there is a roof."

Aya laughed at that promise the first night: the sound was too quick to be sad. "You sell yourself and call it a plan," she said, tucking the crust of bread deeper into the hem of her skirt as if hiding a small, private covenant. Rue had no answer that was not a hand-shaped accounting of the cost—just a look that meant she had chosen the roof and would pay its price in stitches and hours.

Kindness was small there. One morning, when Aya's hands trembled from a fever she would not admit, the head of dorm—herself a woman with palms like worked leather and a voice that did not rise—came in and sat on Aya's bunk. She took Aya's wrist, felt the pulse, and smoothed the hair away from her brow. "Wrap your scarf tighter," she said, and then pinned an excusal slip to Aya's trunk: a thin parchment stamped with the dormmistress's cipher. It was an official exemption that would keep Aya from the garden rota that afternoon but note an extra wash shift to be made up at week's end. The gesture was quick and official; it had the taste of mercy measured in chores.

Micro-violence answered kindness like a shadow. The board under the lamp displayed names and small symbols; when a girl left a pan unclean the symbol appeared by her name and a note would be pinned to her trunk: a week in the scullery, an extra hour at the boil. That afternoon a paper slip appeared on Mara's trunk—one hand had set it there with the same economy as a ledger entry—and Mara's face went flat as if a coin had been taken.

Rue mended that afternoon, and stealing small kindnesses was a part of the trade. She took Aya's crust one evening and folded it into a napkin, boiling water to warm it and pressing it between two clean boards until the crust softened. "Share," Rue said, handing the napkin over. Aya's grin was a fold of relief; she pressed the softened bread into Rue's palm like repayment for the touch of warmth.

Loren moved through these intimacies with a quiet that was not pity but calculation. He came to Rue's bench and, without a word, set a small scrap of cloth before her—an old piece from the teachers' basket, a mending the house had set aside. He slit a neat mark with his thumb where the stitch should start and tapped the spot with a blunt finger. "Keep your edge," he murmured. "Make it hold."

His mentorship was not a gift of sentiment; it was a counting of possibility. When Rue hesitated on a stitch because she was thinking of the ledger, he touched her wrist and guided the needle. The motion was finite and firm, and the correction carried both instruction and a tacit accounting: the favor had been noticed and catalogued. Later, when she tucked a seam of another girl's apron so the fabric would not gape in the sink, Loren gave her no praise, only the half-smile of someone who had marked the tally down in a private column.

At night they told stories in the narrow sleep between the bell's last call and the first cough from beyond the gardens. One girl leaned forward and said, "Elaine left—came back with a scar, they say," the name turned into a small, astonished hope that made a girl on the far bench clutch her blanket tighter. Another, bored and practised, rolled her eyes and muttered, "Maris is their example; they've polished her story into a pamphlet." A third made Sal's name into a quick, secret gesture and the room swallowed the laugh that tried to follow. Rue felt her fingers tighten on the ledger beneath her pillow and glanced once at the pencilled sums as if to count what her choice had cost.

Rue thought of the promise beside her mother's bed and the way it had already changed her. She had traded the small freedoms of being uncounted for a place that would teach her how to count and how to be counted. The house taught its girls to be useful: how to make a seam hold, how to prune a rose so it did not waste itself, how to set a pot so the water would not boil over. It taught them, equally, the language of debt: the way a marked tally could be slid across a table and the world would look smaller for it.

One evening as the sun lowered beyond the glasshouses, Aya pressed her palm into Rue's and, without ceremony, said, "They think they have us counted. But we can count each other." She held out the crust in a palm made soft by steam and mending and Rue understood the ledger had another column: loyalty.

Rue's answer was practical; she folded the bread in half and handed it back, careful as if returning a coin. "Then let's start an account," she said, and the words were small and absolute.

She kept her hands busy, not to forget, but to make sure that when the ledger looked back, there would be more than one kind of entry written there.
