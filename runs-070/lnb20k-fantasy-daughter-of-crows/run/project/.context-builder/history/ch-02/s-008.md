### previous-scene  <novel/chapters/ch-02/scenes/s-007.md>

The Ink Room smelled of old paper and iron—a smell Rue had come to know as the house’s private breath. The door was a slab below the library; even in the bright hour the light leaned away from the cases. An attendant sat at the long table, sleeves rolled, eyes like a bird's—quick and watchful. The Folio was under glass on a lectern in the center, its binding banded and its page splayed like the cheek of a careful thing.

Rue had not meant to come that day. She had only meant to check a name on the thin list against a trunk slip and found, instead, the slip pinned to a trunk whose number did not appear on the public column. The slip was neat, folded, a folded knowledge that had not been transcribed. That sort of absence was a hole in a ledger; holes collected in Rue's mind the way rain filled hollows.

She told the attendant. "May I—" she began, and the attendant's hand was already moving toward a tray where a small iron key lay, stamped dark with a crest. The key caught the light like a small coin. He did not look at her, only lifted it and said, "You'll need to sign for it."

They made the little ritual of sign-out beneath the lamp: a name on a page, a careful loop of ink that said who took the key, and the hour in a marginal column that might as well have been a hinge. Rue's name looked small among the clerical loops. The Archive Key rested in her palm as if it belonged there now, its iron cold and definite.

The attendant unlatched the cabinet with a practiced twist. The air that escaped smelled of dust and varnish and something that might have been the memory of wax. Rue watched the clasp give and the cabinet open on the slow hinge of habit. Hands that had known many such openings reached in and, with a precise motion, drew the Folio free.

On the page something had been altered. It was the sort of thing the archives taught you to feel before you read: ink density like tide marks, a careful crossing-out in a column where names should be even, and a faint, scoured smear at the margin as if someone had tried to breathe an erasure into being. There was also a smaller, cramped addition near the witness line—too small to read at a glance, written in a hand that crowded the letters like something hiding.

The attendant closed the cabinet door and watched Rue watch the page. "Discrepancies happen," he said, fingers steepled. "Scribes have their hands—different inks, different days. We note them and we note why."

"Why is a slip pinned to a trunk but absent from the intake column?" Rue asked, and the attendant's quick face smoothed into a practicable quiet. "Perhaps it was misfiled," he said. "Perhaps it was noted elsewhere. The house has logs."

That answer was the same pattern Rue had seen before: a naming of procedures without a showing of margins. It fit exactly where it should: explanation by management rather than disclosure. She felt an edge of something thin and metallic in her chest, the shape of the Binding Seal's memory when she had leaned to sign.

She leaned closer to the Folio and, as if the book were a person, she read the page for the feeling of it. Her fingers hovered but did not touch; the rules were still rules, even when a hole wants to be filled. The crossing-out was not a neat correction but a heavy scrawl, hurried at some later hour; the erasure at the margin looked as if someone had tried to scrape the ink back into air. The cramped note near the witness line sat like a swallowed word.

The attendant shrugged with both shoulders. "If you have concerns, speak with the Headmistress. She hears these things when they are given importance. We keep the record here in the Ink Room; we do not adjudicate."

Rue found the deflection like a hand brushing dust from a ledger page. "I will speak with her," she said.

The Headmistress received Rue in the corridor off the Hall with the practiced ease of someone used to being the country's public margin. Her gloves were folded at her wrist; her voice was the same placid instrument Rue had heard before. Rue showed her the slip and described the crossed entry and the faint scouring.

The Headmistress's mouth tightened, a small, quick measure. She touched the Folio as if to make the book's weight real and said, "We maintain our records with care. Sometimes a later hand clarifies an old line; sometimes a note is compressed into a margin for administrative reasons. These are not, in themselves, conspiracies."

"Then why is the slip missing from the intake list? Why is there an old crossing where a departure should be?" Rue asked, meaning the questions to be simple and precise.

The Headmistress, with the calm of the public scripture she had practiced, named the house's mechanism instead of showing it. "The Folio is consulted when obligations run their course. The Ten-Year term is a crucible, Rue. Those who reach its end are rare because the house's work is not casual; it is a shaping. Records are kept and reviewed; departures are accounted for when there is cause for them."

The way she said the last words—'not casual'—felt like the closing of a ledger to Rue. The Ten-Year period had been a number before; the Headmistress made it a test framed in the language of duty and craft. Rue heard the sentence as both explanation and closing.

She left the Ink Room with the Archive Key returned to its velvet tray and her questions heavier than the iron in the key. The attendant slid the register closed with a soft scrape. The crossing-out sat on the Folio page like a healed scar; the cramped, small addition in the margin remained unreadable to her still. That small, confusing line folded into Rue's mind with the weight of a promise that had not yet been paid.

Outside, the gardens smelled of salt and clipped hedges. Rue felt the hollow of a ledger in her chest and the new, sharp instinct to watch the pages that had been set aside. The Ten-Year crucible, she realised, was not just a term in ink; it was a pressure that could be read in erased margins and in the way a house called mercy by tallying service. She would count, and she would be counted.

The Hall had been dressed for ceremony: tapers in iron sconces, banners looped along the rafters, and a band of patrons in muted cloaks gathered where the benches broke for the lectern. The hundred names were not all present in person—the intake was a year complete now—but the room counted them with a single gesture: a long ledger closed on the lectern, its leather dulled and the Folio's page turned like a breath.

The Headmistress stood at the lectern with the amber bell beside her, a small, bright thing on a field of polished wood. A hush fell like a hand when she spoke; her voice did what it always did—smoothed the rough edges of debt and need into policy and purpose. "We mark the year by the work done and the hands taught," she said. "One hundred entered this household this term; they leave different by trade and by duty."

Praise and patronage moved through the room in neat phrases. A patron read a short commendation—charity turned to apprenticeship, coin turned into craft—and the benches responded with measured applause. Somewhere, a woman murmured Elaine's name the way people murmur of distant miracles; someone else lifted a hand in a quiet sign to Maris's tidy example; Sal's name hovered in the air like a warning and the room took it in without exclamation.

When the formal words closed, the Headmistress laid a hand on the Folio's binding and gave a slight, official nod. A scribe—small, quick-fingered—stepped forward and unlatched a clasp. "The ledger for this intake is closed and copied," the scribe announced in a voice linen-thin with formality. He ran a thumb along the edge of the page, and for all the room's music the action felt like a seam being finished. "A thin list will be kept for public record; the full Folio will be carried to the Archive for safekeeping."

Before the applause could rise, the Headmistress lifted her chin and gave the Hall a line that sat the myth beside the praise. "Across a decade," she said, voice crisp in the hush, "only three have been recorded as departing after ten years—Elaine, Maris, and Sal. Let their names stand in the Folio and in our memory as both testament and caution."

A bell rang—clear, decisive. Whether the Headmistress had lifted it herself or some other hand made it speak did not matter in that moment; the ringing set the Hall's hours into a firm place. The notes cut the air and the guests stood like ledgers balanced. The patron ceremony folded into smaller salutations and the murmuring thinned to business.

Loren found Rue at the edge of the benches, needle stilled in her palm as the world slid back into ordinary rules. He put a hand on her shoulder with the easy authority of someone who had always been the first to call a name. "The house will want apprentices assigned by sundown," he said, voice low. "Come to the registry table. You'll take three; make them hold the work."

At the long table, a scribe had unrolled a narrow log—its columns already filled with shifted names and notes. Loren tapped a column with a pen like a baton. "We take the new apprentices from the intake list and pair them with working benches," he said. "Sponsorship is noted in the logs; training hours recorded."

Rue watched as Loren called names and the scribe wrote them in a neat hand. The ritual was small and administrative: a name read, a nod, a pen scratch, a folded slip pinned to a trunk to mark where the apprentice would sleep. Rue felt the apparatus of the house tighten around her—reports, sponsorships, tallies—made visible in ink and slip.

When her turn came she stood at the table and took the slips offered: three folded pieces of paper, each named and numbered. They were anonymous in the way of registers—no grand introductions, only a shorthand that said: learn, serve, be counted. She folded her fingers around the paper and felt the weight like a coin.

Loren leaned close and, as he had a thousand times before in a workshop, set her hands on the first slip and showed her how to smooth the crease that would make the name a working thing. "Pull the stitching taught in the morning; make the seam hold so they can carry a pocket for a coin," he murmured. The instruction was both craft and custody: teach skill, take responsibility.

Rue walked the benches that afternoon with Loren at her elbow, the slips tucked into her apron like small ledgers. She met three girls in the sunlight that came through the high windows: a thin girl who kept her thumb inched into the hem of her sleeve, a broad-shouldered one whose hands were already callused, and a quiet one who watched with the slow calculation of a ledger. They did not speak much; names and work did the introducing. Rue thought of the ledger at home and the pages she had closed for a roof.

She set the girls to their benches, showed them the angle of a cut or the tension of a stitch, and marked their first hours in the house log with a neat pen stroke. Each time she wrote a name she felt a small tally accrue in the house's account and, with it, in her own. Apprenticeship was given as a trade; it was counted as an obligation.

By dusk, as the Hall emptied and candles guttered, Rue sat with the slips—three little folded ledgers of beginning—and felt the double edge of her work. The year's ledger had closed; the Folio would be carried away. She had, in the same movement, been entrusted with other names to hold and teach. The bell's note still seemed lodged under her ribs.

Loren's hand came to her shoulder once more. "Keep careful ledgers, Rue," he said. "Teach well. The house notices both."

She folded the final slip into the inside of her palm as if tucking away a coin. For the first time she understood how a life inside the Academy could become not only a way to be counted but a way to count others. She left the Hall with the slips at her breast and the Folio's closed leather heavy in her mouth of memory—an accounting that had closed one book and opened three more.
