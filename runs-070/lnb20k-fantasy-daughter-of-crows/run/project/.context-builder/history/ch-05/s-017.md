### world-rules  <world/rules.yaml>

These govern what is TRUE. They do not say what anyone KNOWS.
A character knows a rule only where canon says so; otherwise they may be ignorant of it, wrong about it, or in the middle of working it out — and if the story is about someone discovering one of these, writing it into their thoughts early is how that story gets destroyed.

- The Academy accepts exactly one hundred girls into a cohort each year; this number is public and rigid in the story's social systems.
- Historically, after a ten-year period inside the Academy, only three individuals have left and their departures are recorded in the Folio (this historical fact is established early and treated as canon unless explicitly changed by a scena-level event with provenance).
- Contracts the recruits sign are legally and magically binding when sealed with the Binding Seal; the Seal enacts the Academy's claims but requires the Academy's own rituals to be maintained.
- Magic has a cost: any enchantment that binds a person's freedom draws from the same ledger as the Folio and must be accounted for in the Academy's records; bargains cannot be silently created or negated without record.
- The War Envoy represents external political/military forces; he may demand terms, levy burdens, or threaten force, but he cannot invent or erase Academy records — only expose and exploit them.
- The Folio truthfully records entries and departures; it can be read and misread, hidden, or weaponised, but altering historical entries is itself an action with consequences and must be shown on-page when it happens.
- The Headmistress is powerful within the Academy but not omnipotent; she must work within political constraints, her staff and the Academy's rituals.
- No deus ex machina: solutions to the Academy's bindings must arise from revealed rules and the characters' established resources and relationships, not from unexplained new powers.

The council had been a blur of ledger talk and bitter arithmetic; the siege had made every choice immediate. Rue found herself at the small table in the corner of the Great Hall not because the Headmistress summoned her but because the house wanted someone with hands that still knew stitches and the courage to cut a seam.

The Envoy waited at the lectern like an unread statute. He had returned early, impatient with counsel that moved in circles. The Headmistress stood between him and the Folio, placid and lined with the work of a life spent translating trouble into entries. Rue set her palms on the worn wood and felt the ledgered pressure under her skin.

"We can bargain with numbers," she said, voice small but steady. "We can trade what is countable. But we must do it in a way that leaves people free to be themselves after. I have been counting for a long time. I know how a tally can harden into a chain."

Loren, at her shoulder, tilted his head. "You know the counters, Rue. You know what a ledger will do."

She did. She had signed once as a young woman and learned how a pen could be a rope. The Folio lay open where the clerks had set it; the scribe had put the sponsorship ledger beside it and, beneath the lamplight, the phrase 'Exchange noted' still seemed to pulse like a small accusation.

Rue's plan was not a magic. It was paperwork and witnesses and a bartered sacrifice. "We will offer the civic pledge as we said—kitchens, measured labour hours—but we will make a witnessed transfer mechanism," she told the room. "We will record, here and now, a conditional column in the Folio: 'Pending release—exchange recorded.' The Envoy will accept our civic pledge as partial satisfaction for certain sponsorship claims, provided those claims are publicly reclassified as 'satisfied by civic exchange' with clerks and sponsor or municipal witness. Those entries will be stamped and witnessed, and then the named apprentices may petition counsel for review."

Silence became the room's examiner. The Envoy folded his hands. "Legible claims are legible," he said. "If your civic pledge is recorded and the city accepts it as fulfillment, those claims will be accounted for."

The Headmistress's eyes narrowed, the accountant's knot forming behind her calm. "It requires sponsor acknowledgment and a municipal acceptance—legal witnesses. It will not erase a binding without that. But it will create a path that can be argued in counsel."

Rue knew the cost. "The house will redistribute apprenticeship hours to meet the civic pledge," she said. "Training will slow. Rations will be measured. Some women will carry extra shifts. I volunteer to be guarantor—my time and my instructional hours—to underwrite the first set of petitions. Let the record show a guarantor with a name who will be accountable." She said the words and felt them land like a tally on her chest.

Loren's breath caught. "You would put your name to it?"

"Yes," she said. "If a ledger is what frees them, let it be a ledger with witnesses and with someone to answer the cost. I will take on the shifts in the workshops; I will trade my hours so others may be counted out."

The Headmistress considered, fingers folding a small motion of thought. The Envoy watched as if weighing a contract. A clerk stepped forward to the Archive drawer and signed the register with the inked loop they used for the key; the Archive Key was handed to the scribe who would open the deeper cabinets if needed. "We note the guarantor action," the clerk said aloud, writing: 'Guarantor: Rue — instructional hours pledged.' He set his pen down and looked up. "Now the civic pledge entry must be made canonical."

Scribes bent to work. The clerk wrote the heading into the Folio: 'Pending Release — conditional upon sponsor or municipal acknowledgment; civic exchange recorded.' He left a narrow column for names and another for witnesses. Then, with the Headmistress in attendance and the Envoy's official nod, the clerk read the sentence aloud and the lamp made the ink shine. The Envoy inclined his head and, in a small, efficient motion, noted the city's receipt of the civic pledge—an acceptance in principle, not full ratification.

Maris watched the line as if it might spit fire. Elaine's face showed something like approval, tempered with the memory of what exchanges demand. Sal's silence kept the scar at the temple in shadow.

The cost was immediate. The rota boards were adjusted: apprentices would lose hours; ration tickets were cut; cooks doubled their output. Rue signed her name—the same script that once marked her sold provenance—beneath the new civic column. The pen felt colder this time; it felt like choice.

"Names will not be handed over as raw conscription," the Headmistress said, voice steady. "But a path has been created: public, witnessed, and accountable. Those with claims satisfied by civic exchange may petition counsel for release."

Loren put his hand on Rue's shoulder, rough and certain. "You have set the counters to a certain honesty," he said. "Keep them honest when the world reads them." His eyes held the old warning: counts become chains unless watched.

Rue thought of the years she had traded. She had been practical when she was young; now she was practical in a different way. She had traded some of her future instruction hours as a guarantee; she had asked for a witnessed mechanism that could be read by sponsors and municipal counsel. It might not free anyone immediately. It might not be enough. But it was a path that used the house's own language—ink and witness—to make a claim legible and contestable.

The Headmistress nodded once and tapped the Folio's leather as if sealing a sentence. "We will convene counsel within three days to consider petitions under this entry," she said. "Let the clerks prepare the lists."

Outside, the harbor smoke had not lifted. Inside, a new column stood in the Folio and rue's name shielded one small, human possibility. The Hundred would pay—hours, food, and a debt of patience—but there would be a record to read and a room in which to argue. Rue had chosen the ledger as tool and guarantor, and she felt its weight settle into her palms like an old stitch.

Later, when the clerk copied the new civic entry into the house logs and slid the paper into the Archive under a stamped witness, Rue understood the cost: a slowing of craft, a thinning of bread, and her own time bound as surety. She also understood what she had done: she had used the house's language to carve a way out.

Whether that way would be enough would be decided by sponsors, counsel, and the Envoy's city. Rue had made a sacrifice in ink and hours; she had set a procedure that might, in time, let some leave. She had owned the consequence of her sale and set the Academy toward a new, ledgered reckoning.

"I will need to scrape the blot carefully to see what lies beneath."

It was a small, almost surgical motion: the scraping of ink and the soft raising of paper dust. Rue watched as the blade worked and the lamp made the fibres glimmer. At first there was only paler paper where ink had been; then a shadow of letters resolved. The clerk leaned over and breathed, careful. "Here—an added note," he murmured, and his finger pointed to a cramped set of words beside a name in the sponsorship ledger laid beside the Folio. He read, quietly, as if making the room hold its breath: "Exchange noted."

The two words landed like a coin dropped into a bowl. They were not numbers; they were a notation that promised another page elsewhere. The Headmistress's fingers tightened on the Folio's leather. "If there are entries that affect the house's charge, they will be brought to counsel," she said. "We will review these marginal notations as part of our records."

"Records make things legible," Loren said from the back, his voice low enough that the clerks might write but not that the walls would hear. "Legible things can be traded. Be careful what you make visible."

Rue's chest felt like a ledger being closed and reopened at once. She had come to the Ink Room expecting a ritual answer—something tidy and sanctifying—and found instead the bluntness of notation. Elaine's scar caught the lamplight as she stepped down from the lectern. She inclined her head to the Headmistress and then to Rue. "Know which ledgers you let count you into," she said simply, then left as quietly as she had come.

The Ink Room's door closed on the quiet scraping and the whisper of pages turning—on the tally that would be read again and again. Rue left the room with none of the neat, ritual absolution she had sought. She carried, instead, the ledger's new weight: the knowledge that the Academy's myths were recorded in ink and that ink had a way of calling accounts due.
