### world-rules  <world/rules.yaml>

These govern what is TRUE. They do not say what anyone KNOWS.
A character knows a rule only where canon says so; otherwise they may be ignorant of it, wrong about it, or in the middle of working it out — and if the story is about someone discovering one of these, writing it into their thoughts early is how that story gets destroyed.

- The Academy accepts exactly one hundred girls into a cohort each year; this number is public and rigid in the story's social systems.
- Historically, after a ten-year period inside the Academy, only three individuals have left and their departures are recorded in the Folio (this historical fact is established early and treated as canon unless explicitly changed by a scena-level event with provenance).
- Contracts the recruits sign are legally and magically binding when sealed with the Binding Seal; the Seal enacts the Academy's claims but requires the Academy's own rituals to be maintained.
- Magic has a cost: any enchantment that binds a person's freedom draws from the same ledger as the Folio and must be accounted for in the Academy's records; bargains cannot be silently created or negated without record.
- The War Envoy represents external political/military forces; he may demand terms, levy burdens, or threaten force, but he cannot invent or erase Academy records — only expose and exploit them.
- The Folio truthfully records entries and departures; it can be read and misread, hidden, or weaponised, but altering historical entries is itself an action with consequences and must be shown on-page when it happens.
- The Headmistress is powerful within the Academy but not omnipotent; she must work within political constraints, her staff and the Academy's rituals.
- No deus ex machina: solutions to the Academy's bindings must arise from revealed rules and the characters' established resources and relationships, not from unexplained new powers.

### previous-scene  <novel/chapters/ch-03/scenes/s-011.md>

Rue had taken to standing near the lectern when there were affairs of weight in the Hall; it put the harbor in the windows behind her and let her watch both the city and the house at once. The benches were full in a different way than before—faces alert, fingers folded, a dozen small cloths already smoothed for someone who might be called to hurry.

The War Envoy moved with the same patient, economy she had seen at the steps: precise, unadorned, his coat buttoned against the cold. He did not carry a banner; he carried a notice and a manner that read like a law. When he spoke he did not ask for entry so much as present it, as if the war itself had already written the words for him.

"The island is drawn into the campaign," he said, voice even. "Our supply lines shorten and our needs increase. We come not to take from you blind, but to ask of what can be measured and offered. In time, claims will be made for bonds or levies; in time, support of manpower may be required. We expect counsel and an answer in the period given."

The phrasing was careful—no single demand yet, only the architecture of demands. It was worse for being schematic. Rue heard the list and did the arithmetic: bonds; levies; manpower. Each word landed like a weight placed on the Hall's long table.

The Headmistress heard it the way she heard everything—slowly, as if testing a measure against a rule. She did not blush and she did not bristle; she arranged her reply with the precision of a clerk. "The Mercy Academy is a house of instruction and shelter," she said, palms folded at her wrist. "We can muster kitchens, offer shelter, and keep neutrality within our walls for those under our charge. We will not give names of our girls as a commodity."

There was a small, audible intake in the benches at her refusal. The Envoy's face did not change; his eyes were the unblinking calculation of a ledger. "Neutrality may be of value, but neutrality is accounted for. Kitchens and store are matters of grain and pot; shelter is matter of space and time. The city will ask for something in return—guarantees that sustain the war effort."

The Headmistress's reply turned soft at the edges but not conciliatory. "We will account for what we can spare. We will offer kitchens and shelter as humanitarian assistance. Where civic claims ask for manpower, we will consider how best to meet obligations without breaking our trust to the house's charge. We will not hand over our charges as raw conscription."

It was a bargain wrapped in a refusal and a promise. Rue heard the difference between the two as plainly as if she had been taught to read ink: the Headmistress protected the house's people by moving the payment to accounting—by promising to make a ledger of what the house would give. She would trade counted things, not names.

Someone in the benches could not hold it. A young apprentice—no more than a handful of years beneath the Academy's roof—stood before the Headmistress as if the Hall's ritual let her speak. Her voice trembled, small and raw: "If they come for people, what will we do? What will happen to those with ten years left?"

Her question landed like a thin bell. The Headmistress's eyes softened, and for the first time Rue saw a flash of the woman behind the gloves. "We will shelter those under our charge," the Headmistress said, and there was a syllable in her tone that was promise. Then she added, immediately, with the same clerkly precision: "And we will account it. We will keep a record—who is housed, who is fed, what is pledged. Those records will be how we bargain." The words were both comfort and contract.

The Envoy inclined his head. "Records are useful," he said. "They make claims legible. They will be considered in counsel."

Rue felt a small, private panic she could not put to work. The Headmistress had promised shelter and had promised the ledger. To Rue the two things looked less like alternatives than a single equation: protection in exchange for accounting. The house would not hide; it would count and present its counts for negotiation.

Voices in the benches rose in soft clusters—apprentices whispering, an older woman saying a name as if to test how it sounded under threat. A clerk moved quietly, taking notes. The Hall's machinery engaged itself with the practical speed of a practiced loom: lists were pulled, rotas rethought, kitchens inventoried. Rue watched fingers lift and cross names, the same gestures she had learned in apprenticeship now repurposed for an adult calculus.

Loren stood at the back, sleeves rolled despite the sudden formality, and his jaw set in a way Rue had seen when a stitch would not hold. He gave her a small look—no words—an urging to keep steady and to be counted among those who treated work as defense.

The Envoy's party left as they had come: clean lines, small steps, and a deadline that had the taste of arithmetic in it. He had not named a levy or set a figure; he had only shown how such things might be asked for—bonds, levies, manpower—left to the city's counsel. The Headmistress had named the house's boundaries and promised shelter in exchange for careful accounting.

Rue walked down the aisle after the guests, trailing behind the Headmistress's shadow. The Great Hall felt narrower and more urgent. She thought of the sewn seam of contracts, of the marginal note that had said 'tethering,' of the way the house made its people legible in ink.

Outside, the harbor's air smelled of tar and a hint of smoke on the wind. The Envoy's counsel had not broken the Academy; it had made clear the terms under which the Academy might be compelled. Rue understood, with a sudden, bitter clarity, that the ledger could be pulled into another ledger and that protection might cost a different sort of accounting. The war, in its quiet arithmetic, had already begun to take its place on the page.

It was late morning and the light fell flat across the flagstones; the benches were half-filled with people folding away the day's work. They arrived modestly: a small boat at the quay, a party stepping ashore, coats buttoned against a wind that smelled of tar and iron. The War Envoy walked with the economy of someone who had been taught how to occupy space without wasting it. He wore no colors Rue could read; his voice later would be like a law. For now he moved between porters and clerks and the Headmistress's own attendants like a question that did not take shape until it reached the lectern.

The Headmistress met him on the steps, gloves folded at her wrist as she had folded them for ceremonies and private audiences. She greeted him with the courtesy she reserved for those who could make trouble into an expense. Rue watched their two shapes as if she were looking at a pair of ledgers: one public and polished, the other austere and unreadable.

The Envoy's words were a careful thing. He placed his hands behind his back and said, slow and precise: "Conflict moves toward your shore. Armies that were distant are now marked by their approach. I come with terms and with notice: the war seeks the island."

There was no flourish in his voice; the sentence landed like a flat statement of arithmetic. The room folded itself taut. Someone's spoon stopped midair; a child's laugh died where it had been. Rue felt the Hall contract around the words.

The Headmistress listened with the expression Rue had learned to read as both shield and calculation. When he finished she said, politely, "We take notice, and we will consider any terms in counsel. Mercy Academy serves not only its students but the city that keeps it. We will respond in kind to any legitimate need, within the bounds of our charge."

Her answer was public and careful—an immediate translation of threat into procedure. But Rue watched the way she paused on the word "consider" and the small narrowing at her temple when she said "within the bounds of our charge." That narrowing was not a refusal so much as the sound of her measuring what she might give away.

The Envoy did not press. He placed a folded paper on the lectern—a dry thing of parchment and seal—and inclined his head. "This is notice," he said. "We will return in ten days for a formal answer."

At those words the Hall changed shape. The Headmistress's attendants moved like hands at a loom, gathering lists, speaking names in low voices that Rue did not catch. A clerk stood and took the parchment from the lectern and, with a small, official flourish, carried it toward the Archive corridor as if its presence should be filed where things of weight were kept.

Rue felt the shift like an animal senses a sudden wind: the everyday loosened. The benches that had hummed with quiet industry came alive with a different sort of business. Apprentices pulled sleeves down and smoothed aprons; a woman ran a glance toward the garden gates; somewhere, a bell's distant note—ordinary, and suddenly not—took on the sound of a last marking.

She descended the stairs as the Envoy prepared to leave, and for a moment the two of them were near enough that she could see the Envoy's face close: pale, controlled, with the small, implacable set of someone who carries bad news as fact. She wanted to ask him if the house should do something now, but there were questions that had procedures and questions that had to wait.

She imagined the Headmistress in her study, counting the hours as she counted ledgers, measuring what could be promised and what could not.

People began to move with a new cadence. The Headmistress spoke to a cluster of senior staff in clipped sentences—orders, not panic—and Rue watched the machinery begin to engage. Lists were pulled from drawers, names spoken and crossed; a rota was reconsidered in a whisper. The Academy's insulated life had been given a poke and its surface rippled into practical concern.

Rue stood at the window and watched the harbor where the envoy's boat had waited: a small dark shape against the bright water, a reminder that the world beyond the walls still had force. She thought of the contracts she had signed, of the seam and the margin where 'tethering' had been written; she felt suddenly that the house's tallies might meet the tally of a war and that the things recorded in ink could be claimed by a different account.

At dusk the Hall felt altered in a way that would not be fixed by one night's sleep. The Headmistress had bowed to the formality of the Envoy and had met notice with a considered line; Rue had seen the private calculation behind the public answer. Outside, the harbor wind came in with a taste of salt and smoke. The island that had seemed a circled life to Rue now felt like a place on a map with edges that could be pressed.

She left the Great Hall with a small, precise unease. The Envoy's notice had a time attached to it—ten days—and with that number in the air Rue understood that the Academy's ledger would have to account for more than apprentices' hours. The house's tallies might be called upon; someone would have to answer for them.

Years folded like new hems under Rue's hands. The montage began in the small seams of ordinary days: a bench that creaked the same, a lamp that burned low in the same corner, a slip of paper folded and handed across a table until the motion became a rite.

Loren's hand found wrists the way it always had, but his hair silvered at the temples and the river of his teaching voice ran a touch slower. He still tapped a thumb at the base of a palm—"From here"—and a new apprentice's elbow stiffened into the proper seam of a stitch. Once, as he showed a new apprentice the angle of a cut, Rue caught the old, patient economy in his face and thought how many small corrections could hold a life together.

Aya kept her hands in herbal pots and brews more often than in her once-hidden crust, and the scent of camomile and rosemary threaded the workshops. She passed a jar across a bench: "Soak it in three nights," she told a girl whose fingers had split on a needle. Her hands moved like a remedy; the kindness was clinical and not sentimental. Sometimes Rue would find Aya in the glasshouses pressing leaves between pages for a poultice and laughing low at a joke the years had taught her to keep small.

The Folio came and went in the rhythm of the house: closed, copied, a thin list pinned for the Hall, the full book carried away. Each closure was a small ceremony—tapers in iron sconces, the bell's note—an accounting that smelled of varnish and ink. A scribe's thumb would run along the edge of a page and the announcement would fall like a seam being knotted: "The ledger for this intake is closed and copied." The bell that answered knotted a year's hours into place.

Sal's name was used differently: a curt injunction in dorm speeches—"Remember Sal"—that stopped conversation like a struck match.

After the hall emptied Rue walked the benches with slips tucked into her apron. Friendships formed as quick as a folded napkin and dissolved as quietly: a knot of laughter over shared stew, a silence at a trunk when a slip had been pinned and a week in the scullery waited. The House used those human arrangements as it used its ledgers: material to be shaped. Where a girl once needed bread and found the crust, she might, in years, be the one teaching another how to soften and share it.

Occasionally the Folio's margins whispered back. Rue would catch a clerk's fingers tracing a line in a closed book, or see a note pinned that did not appear on the thin public list. Sometimes the crossing-out she had once peered at in a single page reappeared in other hands: an erasure here, a cramped marginal addition there. The Ink Room kept its private work below the library and the Archive's doors moved like a slow pulse; every time the book was taken the house preserved and re-hid its decisions.

At the year's end, at a patron gathering where candles burned low and the bell made the same decisive note, Rue would stand among the benches like a fulcrum—older, surer, and aware that she was both product and producer of the house's counting. She carried apprentices' slips in her apron and returned names to the register with a pen that had learned to weigh.

The montage slowed then into the long present: apprentices learned, ribbons were tied, the bell's note marked endings and beginnings, and the cycle turned. Rue's life had become a ledger in motion—stitches, hours, names, the small mercies and bruises kept in a margin only she sometimes read. She had become an anchor in the house's machinery: someone who taught, who kept accounts, who remembered the cost and the craft. When she left the Hall that night the gardens smelled of rose and sea, and the Folio's closed leather left a quiet pressure in her palm—a reminder that the house counted, and that she counted with it.
