### world-rules  <world/rules.yaml>

These govern what is TRUE. They do not say what anyone KNOWS.
A character knows a rule only where canon says so; otherwise they may be ignorant of it, wrong about it, or in the middle of working it out — and if the story is about someone discovering one of these, writing it into their thoughts early is how that story gets destroyed.

- The Academy accepts exactly one hundred girls into a cohort each year; this number is public and rigid in the story's social systems.
- Historically, after a ten-year period inside the Academy, only three individuals have left and their departures are recorded in the Folio (this historical fact is established early and treated as canon unless explicitly changed by a scena-level event with provenance).
- Contracts the recruits sign are legally and magically binding when sealed with the Binding Seal; the Seal enacts the Academy's claims but requires the Academy's own rituals to be maintained.
- Magic has a cost: any enchantment that binds a person's freedom draws from the same ledger as the Folio and must be accounted for in the Academy's records; bargains cannot be silently created or negated without record.
- The War Envoy represents external political/military forces; he may demand terms, levy burdens, or threaten force, but he cannot invent or erase Academy records — only expose and exploit them.
- The Folio truthfully records entries and departures; it can be read and misread, hidden, or weaponised, but altering historical entries is itself an action with consequences and must be shown on-page when it happens.
- The Headmistress is powerful within the Academy but not omnipotent; she must work within political constraints, her staff and the Academy's rituals.
- No deus ex machina: solutions to the Academy's bindings must arise from revealed rules and the characters' established resources and relationships, not from unexplained new powers.

### previous-scene  <novel/chapters/ch-02/scenes/s-008.md>

Rue went back to the Ink Room as if she were returning to a wound. The light under the library's stacks was low and the cases threw long teeth of shadow. Loren met her at the door with a bench of cloth and didn't ask what she wanted; he only set a lamp and a tray of papers between them, the old ease of two hands who had mended the same hole before.

She had signed for the Archive Key before she came—an inked loop in the register, the hour written like a hinge—and the key had felt cold and final in her hand. Now she eased the leather packet open and the smell found her first: beeswax and iron and a bitter tang that the memory of signing had kept like a bruise. The Binding Seal was not here; but the page she had unfolded sat like a throat open to speak.

The Contract itself was older than the packet she had first signed; the paper was thin in places and the margin had been crowded with hands that had tried to add notes without making a show. Rue ran her thumb along the witness line and found a tucked half-clause: a strip that had been folded into the seam and then stitched over as if to hide it in plain sight.

She eased it free. The letters were cramped and the ink had pooled in tiny rivers where a later hand had gone back and tried to scrape the stroke. In the seam she could make out a single, breath-sized phrase—one word pressed into the margin like a thumbprint.

"tethering."

It was not a sentence; it had no ritual words attached, no syndicate of verbs that explained how it worked. But the word sat against the witness line like an instruction and the margins had the small earnestness of a thing that had been argued for and then whispered into place.

Rue felt the old willing self in her chest, the young woman who had put pen to paper without knowing the weight of a particular word. She remembered the lamp's light then, the way the wax had pooled under the signet and the way her hand had smelled after she pressed her name. She had signed willingly; she had believed in the roof and the trade and in the promise that a counted life could be steadier than the one she left behind.

But the seam and the cramped word made that willingness look smaller, like a coin pressed flat in a palm. The Binding Seal's memory rose in her mouth—beeswax and a bitter iron tang—so familiar she could taste the room where she had signed. If the word meant that there was a tether, then what did the tether take? The books said nothing. The margins had been crowded and then smudged as if someone had changed their mind.

"You found it," Loren said, not looking up from the tray where he was smoothing a scrap. His voice was small and steady, a thread that ran down the bench.

"One word," Rue said. "Tethering." She let the syllable sit between them. "Near the witness line. Folded into the seam."

Loren's fingers stilled for a second; he did not take the packet from her. "Margins hold a lot," he said carefully. "Notes, sponsors, conditions. Sometimes a phrasing is left that looks smaller in a book than it operates in practice."

"Does it bind?" Rue asked.

He looked at her then—Loren's face weathered in a way that did not entirely hide the private calculation beneath. "It ties people to an obligation of service and craft," he said, the answer a pared-down fact and not a ritual. "What it costs depends on what the house logs against a name. Often it's time. Often it's trade. It is rarely what someone thinks when they sign: a neat exchange of bread for hours."

"So it takes something else," Rue said.

"Everything has a ledger," Loren said. "We count craft, we count hours, we count the work you do. The binding makes the counts legible. That's all I can say that won't be a lecture."

He might have meant it as mercy; Rue heard it as the house explaining itself with the blunt tools it had—measurement and record. She had come looking for a liturgy and found arithmetic instead: a hand that rendered decisions into tallies.

She thought of the Three then—the names the Hall had set in a public sentence—and of the small, cramped line in the seam that had no flourish but carried force. The Ten-Year span that had seemed a number at the Folio now sounded like a pressure: time pressed into a shape until someone fit its opening.

Rue folded the strip back into the seam the way she had found it, not to hide, but so the line of the page would hold together. She did not want to tear the paper; she wanted the book whole enough to be read again.

Loren slid a scrap across the table and the lamp made the ink shine. "Be careful with what you ask the books to show you," he said. "They will give you facts, but they will not give you remedies."

She left the Ink Room carrying the packet like an accusation. Outside, the gardens smelled of cut roses and distant sea. The Binding Seal's scent hovered in memory and the word tethering followed her like a small, precise footstep. She had signed in hope and now she understood the shape of what hope had cost: not only hours and apprenticeship, but the way a name could be counted into a house's account. The moral fog that had softened her first choice thickened; she would have to reckon with the cost of a tether she had agreed to without seeing its full length.

The Ink Room smelled of old paper and iron—a smell Rue had come to know as the house’s private breath. The door was a slab below the library; even in the bright hour the light leaned away from the cases. An attendant sat at the long table, sleeves rolled, eyes like a bird's—quick and watchful. The Folio was under glass on a lectern in the center, its binding banded and its page splayed like the cheek of a careful thing.

Rue had not meant to come that day. She had only meant to check a name on the thin list against a trunk slip and found, instead, the slip pinned to a trunk whose number did not appear on the public column. The slip was neat, folded, a folded knowledge that had not been transcribed. That sort of absence was a hole in a ledger; holes collected in Rue's mind the way rain filled hollows.

She told the attendant. "May I—" she began, and the attendant's hand was already moving toward a tray where a small iron key lay, stamped dark with a crest. The key caught the light like a small coin. He did not look at her, only lifted it and said, "You'll need to sign for it."

They made the little ritual of sign-out beneath the lamp: a name on a page, a careful loop of ink that said who took the key, and the hour in a marginal column that might as well have been a hinge. Rue's name looked small among the clerical loops. The Archive Key rested in her palm as if it belonged there now, its iron cold and definite.

The attendant unlatched the cabinet with a practiced twist. The air that escaped smelled of dust and varnish and something that might have been the memory of wax. Rue watched the clasp give and the cabinet open on the slow hinge of habit. Hands that had known many such openings reached in and, with a precise motion, drew the Folio free.

On the page something had been altered. It was the sort of thing the archives taught you to feel before you read: ink density like tide marks, a careful crossing-out in a column where names should be even, and a faint, scoured smear at the margin as if someone had tried to breathe an erasure into being. There was also a smaller, cramped addition near the witness line—too small to read at a glance, written in a hand that crowded the letters like something hiding.

The attendant closed the cabinet door and watched Rue watch the page. "Discrepancies happen," he said, fingers steepled. "Scribes have their hands—different inks, different days. We note them and we note why."

"Why is a slip pinned to a trunk but absent from the intake column?" Rue asked, and the attendant's quick face smoothed into a practicable quiet. "Perhaps it was misfiled," he said. "Perhaps it was noted elsewhere. The house has logs."

That answer was the same pattern Rue had seen before: a naming of procedures without a showing of margins. It fit exactly where it should: explanation by management rather than disclosure. She felt an edge of something thin and metallic in her chest, the shape of the Binding Seal's memory when she had leaned to sign.

She leaned closer to the Folio and, as if the book were a person, she read the page for the feeling of it. Her fingers hovered but did not touch; the rules were still rules, even when a hole wants to be filled. The crossing-out was not a neat correction but a heavy scrawl, hurried at some later hour; the erasure at the margin looked as if someone had tried to scrape the ink back into air. The cramped note near the witness line sat like a swallowed word.

The attendant shrugged with both shoulders. "If you have concerns, speak with the Headmistress. She hears these things when they are given importance. We keep the record here in the Ink Room; we do not adjudicate."

Rue found the deflection like a hand brushing dust from a ledger page. "I will speak with her," she said.

The Headmistress received Rue in the corridor off the Hall with the practiced ease of someone used to being the country's public margin. Her gloves were folded at her wrist; her voice was the same placid instrument Rue had heard before. Rue showed her the slip and described the crossed entry and the faint scouring.

The Headmistress's mouth tightened, a small, quick measure. She touched the Folio as if to make the book's weight real and said, "We maintain our records with care. Sometimes a later hand clarifies an old line; sometimes a note is compressed into a margin for administrative reasons. These are not, in themselves, conspiracies."

"Then why is the slip missing from the intake list? Why is there an old crossing where a departure should be?" Rue asked, meaning the questions to be simple and precise.

The Headmistress, with the calm of the public scripture she had practiced, named the house's mechanism instead of showing it. "The Folio is consulted when obligations run their course. The Ten-Year term is a crucible, Rue. Those who reach its end are rare because the house's work is not casual; it is a shaping. Records are kept and reviewed; departures are accounted for when there is cause for them."

The way she said the last words—'not casual'—felt like the closing of a ledger to Rue. The Ten-Year period had been a number before; the Headmistress made it a test framed in the language of duty and craft. Rue heard the sentence as both explanation and closing.

She left the Ink Room with the Archive Key returned to its velvet tray and her questions heavier than the iron in the key. The attendant slid the register closed with a soft scrape. The crossing-out sat on the Folio page like a healed scar; the cramped, small addition in the margin remained unreadable to her still. That small, confusing line folded into Rue's mind with the weight of a promise that had not yet been paid.

Outside, the gardens smelled of salt and clipped hedges. Rue felt the hollow of a ledger in her chest and the new, sharp instinct to watch the pages that had been set aside. The Ten-Year crucible, she realised, was not just a term in ink; it was a pressure that could be read in erased margins and in the way a house called mercy by tallying service. She would count, and she would be counted.
