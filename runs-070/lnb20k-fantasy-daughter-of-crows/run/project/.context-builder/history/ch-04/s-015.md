### world-rules  <world/rules.yaml>

These govern what is TRUE. They do not say what anyone KNOWS.
A character knows a rule only where canon says so; otherwise they may be ignorant of it, wrong about it, or in the middle of working it out — and if the story is about someone discovering one of these, writing it into their thoughts early is how that story gets destroyed.

- The Academy accepts exactly one hundred girls into a cohort each year; this number is public and rigid in the story's social systems.
- Historically, after a ten-year period inside the Academy, only three individuals have left and their departures are recorded in the Folio (this historical fact is established early and treated as canon unless explicitly changed by a scena-level event with provenance).
- Contracts the recruits sign are legally and magically binding when sealed with the Binding Seal; the Seal enacts the Academy's claims but requires the Academy's own rituals to be maintained.
- Magic has a cost: any enchantment that binds a person's freedom draws from the same ledger as the Folio and must be accounted for in the Academy's records; bargains cannot be silently created or negated without record.
- The War Envoy represents external political/military forces; he may demand terms, levy burdens, or threaten force, but he cannot invent or erase Academy records — only expose and exploit them.
- The Folio truthfully records entries and departures; it can be read and misread, hidden, or weaponised, but altering historical entries is itself an action with consequences and must be shown on-page when it happens.
- The Headmistress is powerful within the Academy but not omnipotent; she must work within political constraints, her staff and the Academy's rituals.
- No deus ex machina: solutions to the Academy's bindings must arise from revealed rules and the characters' established resources and relationships, not from unexplained new powers.

The first sound was a far knock that made the glasshouses shutter. Then the harbor answered: a raw, doubled report that ran along the water and came at the Academy like a hand. Smoke came up low from the quay and the light in the Great Hall went flat; someone began to ring the Amber Bell as if by muscle memory.

"By amber and ink, so sealed," the bell's phrase cut through the Hall—an emergency made to sound like a ritual. The note dropped into the room and every head turned. Rue's hands went still on the cuff she had been mending; the Hall became a place where people were counted and called.

Outside, the approaches burned in stuttering tongues—boats thrown to the shore, ropes on fire, a line of black shapes moving where the harbor should have been quiet. No one in the Hall named the attackers; they were a shape and a noise. The Envoy's notice had become action.

The Headmistress rose and made a single, sharp order. "Clerks, the civic ledger is active. Scribes—Ink Room. Clerks—draw the civic pledge's columns. Muster points now. Kitchens, double your knife shifts. Rota—prioritise shelter. No names to be given." Her voice cut the air like a tally being set.

Clerks moved as if on cue. One slipped to the drawer where the narrow register lived and took it to the lamp. With an inked loop he signed the Archive Key's register and took the key from the tray; another unlatched the cabinet as he had done in calmer times. "Folio—consulted for obligations," the scribe said as the cabinet opened; hands used the practiced economy of people who had handled weight before, extracting the thick book and laying it under lamplight.

Rue followed them to the threshold of the Ink Room and watched the small stage where the Folio was opened. The Binding Seal sat behind glass in its case in the corner; its beeswax and iron scent rose to meet her memory like a bruise. A scribe drew out a clean sheet and set a quick column: "Civic pledge—kitchens; labour rotation; sheltering lists." The clerks bent to their pens and the Folio's page caught the light like a witness.

In the dorms the excusal slips became shelter passes. Rue took a stack and wrote with a hand that did not tremble: name, trunk number, 'shelter pass.' She pinned the slips to the trunks for the ones who would be moved into the Hall—little papers stamped with the dormmistress's cipher—passes to let someone in where there might be no other room. The practice was the same as before but the purpose had been sharpened: these slips now meant a place in the Hall rather than a skip of chores.

Loren had already moved to the glasshouses. "Buckets and lines!" he called, his voice flint. He hauled a length of rope and pointed where women should stand. The older apprentices answered as if a stitch had been called: hands that knew knots became hands that would move water and rope, a makeshift defense against flame and wet wood. They did not pretend it would stand against everything; they did what they could.

A scribe at the Folio read a heading aloud, low and crisp: "Sheltered—names to be logged." The ink gathered in a thin river and the clerk's pen scratched as if counting time. Every name written was a count; every count might be a future claim. Rue felt the weight of each letter as if it were a small coin dropped into a communal fund.

The Amber Bell rang again, not as ceremony but as command. Rota boards were cleared and rewritten; cooks moved like a practiced army, ladling and measuring. The Headmistress walked the benches, touching shoulders with a touch that was both reassurance and a tally. "Keep the logs," she told the clerks. "Record who is sheltered, who is fed, what is pledged. We will argue from the numbers."

But the Ink Room did more than note. A scribe removed a clean vellum and, following the Headmistress's instructions, pencilled a margin note: 'Civic pledge in effect—kitchens and labour rotation active.' It was not an enchantment; it was a document, and the document itself would be the thing negotiators read later. Rue watched the scribe's hand, thinking of how a line of ink could shape a life.

In the Hall the Hundred moved with the steady panic of people who had practised together. A small girl clung to Rue's sleeve and would not let go; Rue knelt and tied a knot in the girl's apron, a small domestic action that felt like a shield. The Headmistress paced and, at intervals, checked the Folio's open page, touching the leather as if to make the book's weight real.

The defenders on the walls worked with the mundane instruments available: ropes, buckets, quick barricades of benches and trunks. Rue helped where she could, carrying a bucket, pressing a wet cloth into a hand, counting the names of girls as they passed through the Hall into the shelter. Each count was a promise made under pressure.

There were no miracles. The Binding Seal stayed behind glass, its presence a recalled scent rather than a released force. The scribes read from the Folio and wrote into the house logs; the Headmistress's tactics were clerical and tactical rather than sorcerous. The academy's defenses were human and ledgered: shelter by count, food by ration, labor by rota.

Smoke thickened along the quay and the sound of the assault moved from the water toward the walls. Someone shouted that a small boat had been grounded and was burning. A woman in the front fainted and apprentices bent to help, voices breaking into prayer and orders.

Loren gripped Rue's shoulder once, in a quick, fierce clasp. "Keep the counters honest," he told her. "Record who goes in and who comes out. Hold them." His eyes, usually patient, had the sharpness of someone who had seen a seam tear.

Rue did as she had always done: she counted, she comforted, she recorded. The Folio's open page absorbed names, the clerks' pens moving like the tide. The Amber Bell's note—half ritual, half alarm—kept time for them all.

When the first hush of night came the Hall smelled of smoke and broth; the lamplight tilted and the Folio lay open with new entries. The Academy had bound itself—by choice and by tally—to a civic pledge that would have to be defended in counsel. The cost of that accounting was already manifest: sleepless names, rationed bread, and a ledger that would be read by people who kept their own books.

Rue sat on a low bench, hands inked and tired, and felt the Folio's weight in her thoughts. The house's bargains had history now—inked and immediate. The Hundred had paid in fear and in work and in the slow tallying of strong, necessary things. Outside, the sound of the siege kept coming in waves; inside, their records would have to hold.

The Hall had a new electricity in it, the kind that gathers when a machine is about to be set to work. Rue felt it as a thrum beneath the benches; the benches themselves seemed to edge toward the lectern as if the room expected a balance to be struck.

The War Envoy had gone; his deadline hung in the air like chalk on a slate. The Headmistress had called a council and then opened the Hall to witnesses. It was a tactical move—the public watching made the bargains easier to justify later. Rue stood where she always did in affairs: near the lectern, near the Folio, where ink and speech met.

People divided as if by geography. On one side were the clerks and the registry women, the sort who thought every problem could be solved by the right column; they spoke of making an account the Envoy would read. On another side were the instructors and the older apprentices—Loren among them—who said fortify, close gates, make the place a citadel. Voices rose in the low, urgent way of people who had worked in close quarters for years.

Loren's voice was low when he spoke to those clustered near him. "Counting is what we do," he said, "but beware that counts become chains. We can offer hours and skill; we cannot make the girls into columns on an enemy's tally."

A clerk, sleeves rolled, tapped a finger against the ledger's margin. "The Envoy asked for legible claims. Legible claims are what bargainers trade. If we present kitchens and a schedule of labour, we can keep names off the table."

"Kitchens and labour will be counted against us," an older woman in the back said. "Counts can be used to hold us. I have seen how a ledger eats a life."

The Headmistress moved among them with the sure economy of someone used to making difficult arithmetic look like policy. She listened, palms folded, and when she spoke the room leaned in.

"We are not reckless with our charge," she said. "We must protect the girls in our care and the house's work. I have considered fortification. I have considered refusal. But the Envoy's counsel showed that neutrality has cost. We cannot shelter if the city collapses around us."

A murmur. Rue felt the memory of Elaine's words—"transparency is a ledger's cousin"—slide behind her eyes.

The Headmistress lifted a hand. "We will make a civic pledge. We will offer the kitchens and a measured rotation of labour hours as a civic contribution. We will not hand names. We will keep records so that any claim may be accounted for and argued on the house's terms. I will instruct the clerks to draw a civic ledger entry now, stating what we pledge to spare."

The words were a cut. A bargain wrapped as protection. Someone near the front hissed; a young apprentice threw up her hands as if the air itself had been made heavy.

Loren's jaw tightened. "So we bind hours in exchange for shelter?" he asked, voice more dangerous than Rue had heard it in years.

The Headmistress's answer was steady. "We bind counted aid, not persons. That is our line. We offer what can be measured and reclaimed. It keeps names from being traded." Her eyes skimmed the benches as if to see whether the house accepted the calculus.

A clerk moved to a drawer and produced a narrow register, the kind Rue had seen in the Ink Room. He opened it beneath the lamp and set a pen in readiness. "Civic pledge—kitchens, rotation of labour to be scheduled and recorded," he murmured as he wrote the heading aloud. His voice had the official brass of ritual. Fingers pointed to columns; numbers were left blank for the house's accounting.

There was argument immediate and sharp. A woman who ran the garden rotas spoke up: "If we promise labour, the girls will lose hours for their apprenticeships. That reduces their trade, not only their time."

"We will structure rota so training continues," the Headmistress said. "We will not break apprenticeship promise. We will prioritise the inexperienced under shelter."

"And who decides?" someone demanded. "You, from your desk? The clerks? The Envoy's counsel?"

A shout rose—an old, raw sound from a bench where someone remembered scullery weeks. Rue felt it like a struck rod. The Hall had split into people who trusted the ledger to save them and people who had seen ledgers take more than they gave.

Rue thought of her own signing, the wax, the signet, the seam where 'tethering' had been pressed. She felt pulled: to the Headmistress, who preserved roofs and work; to Loren, who warned that counted hours could harden into obligations no one meant to sign for. She walked to him as the clerks wrote the civic heading, feeling the pull of both arguments.

"You know counts," she said to Loren quietly. "If we pledge hours, can we keep apprentices' craft?"

He looked at her as if weighing a stitch. "We can try to shape the rota. But the ledger will be used by others. Records make things legible. Once legible, they can be transferred. The binding—legal or otherwise—follows a tally. Be careful."

The Headmistress, seeing the stir, rose and addressed the room. "We will make the pledge public and record it in the house logs. We will name no individuals. We will set limits. We will review claims as they come to counsel."

That was the choice: a public bargain recorded as a civic pledge, offering counted aid rather than names. The Envoy would have what he wanted—an account to read—and the house would answer with its books. To some that would be compromise and prudence; to others, a surrender of principle.

A small girl at the front—a new apprentice—began to cry. Rue stepped toward her, smoothing the girl's sleeve with hands that had tied many ribbons and steadied many stitches. The Headmistress touched the girl's shoulder with a counter of comfort and calculation. "We will shelter you," she said, voice clear. "We will write what we pledge and we will hold to it."

Loren's hand went to his coat pocket, where he had kept an old scrap of folded cloth for luck. He did not speak, but his eyes met Rue's and in them she read both warning and instruction: hold the craft; keep the counters honest.

The Headmistress's decision would be written into house logs; the clerks would note it, the rotas would shift, and somewhere—she did not know where—sponsors and city counsel would translate the numbers into need.

Rue left the Hall with her hands in her apron and the Folio's leather like a pressure in her palms of memory. The house would count; the world would count back. The choice had been made for now. It forced a division that would not be healed by a single ledger entry.

Her voice had the cleric's cadence of promise—and the clerk's addendum: "And we will record it."

Elaine inclined her head slightly, the small motion of someone who has been offered a measure and can name its limit. "Remember what you tell the girls," she said. "The world outside will always have its own ledgers. Know which ones you let count you into them."

She stepped down from the lectern without fanfare. The Hall stayed a little unsettled after she left, the way a table stays trembling after someone has set a weight upon it and gone. Rue touched the margin of her own faith and found it thinner; the Headmistress's public lines had been complicated in one tidy moment by a life that could not be reduced to a pamphlet.

When the clerk closed the sponsorship ledger and slid it back into the drawer, he did it with a small, perfunctory flourish. The room bent back to business with a new thread in its pattern: the Headmistress would defend with rhetoric; some returns carried costs not spelled out in the Folio; whatever the Academy promised, the world outside kept its own accounts, and those could touch the girls in ways the house's pages did not always show.
