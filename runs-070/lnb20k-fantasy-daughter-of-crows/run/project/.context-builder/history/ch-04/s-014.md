### world-rules  <world/rules.yaml>

These govern what is TRUE. They do not say what anyone KNOWS.
A character knows a rule only where canon says so; otherwise they may be ignorant of it, wrong about it, or in the middle of working it out — and if the story is about someone discovering one of these, writing it into their thoughts early is how that story gets destroyed.

- The Academy accepts exactly one hundred girls into a cohort each year; this number is public and rigid in the story's social systems.
- Historically, after a ten-year period inside the Academy, only three individuals have left and their departures are recorded in the Folio (this historical fact is established early and treated as canon unless explicitly changed by a scena-level event with provenance).
- Contracts the recruits sign are legally and magically binding when sealed with the Binding Seal; the Seal enacts the Academy's claims but requires the Academy's own rituals to be maintained.
- Magic has a cost: any enchantment that binds a person's freedom draws from the same ledger as the Folio and must be accounted for in the Academy's records; bargains cannot be silently created or negated without record.
- The War Envoy represents external political/military forces; he may demand terms, levy burdens, or threaten force, but he cannot invent or erase Academy records — only expose and exploit them.
- The Folio truthfully records entries and departures; it can be read and misread, hidden, or weaponised, but altering historical entries is itself an action with consequences and must be shown on-page when it happens.
- The Headmistress is powerful within the Academy but not omnipotent; she must work within political constraints, her staff and the Academy's rituals.
- No deus ex machina: solutions to the Academy's bindings must arise from revealed rules and the characters' established resources and relationships, not from unexplained new powers.

### previous-scene  <novel/chapters/ch-04/scenes/s-013.md>

The Hall had a new electricity in it, the kind that gathers when a machine is about to be set to work. Rue felt it as a thrum beneath the benches; the benches themselves seemed to edge toward the lectern as if the room expected a balance to be struck.

The War Envoy had gone; his deadline hung in the air like chalk on a slate. The Headmistress had called a council and then opened the Hall to witnesses. It was a tactical move—the public watching made the bargains easier to justify later. Rue stood where she always did in affairs: near the lectern, near the Folio, where ink and speech met.

People divided as if by geography. On one side were the clerks and the registry women, the sort who thought every problem could be solved by the right column; they spoke of making an account the Envoy would read. On another side were the instructors and the older apprentices—Loren among them—who said fortify, close gates, make the place a citadel. Voices rose in the low, urgent way of people who had worked in close quarters for years.

Loren's voice was low when he spoke to those clustered near him. "Counting is what we do," he said, "but beware that counts become chains. We can offer hours and skill; we cannot make the girls into columns on an enemy's tally."

A clerk, sleeves rolled, tapped a finger against the ledger's margin. "The Envoy asked for legible claims. Legible claims are what bargainers trade. If we present kitchens and a schedule of labour, we can keep names off the table."

"Kitchens and labour will be counted against us," an older woman in the back said. "Counts can be used to hold us. I have seen how a ledger eats a life."

The Headmistress moved among them with the sure economy of someone used to making difficult arithmetic look like policy. She listened, palms folded, and when she spoke the room leaned in.

"We are not reckless with our charge," she said. "We must protect the girls in our care and the house's work. I have considered fortification. I have considered refusal. But the Envoy's counsel showed that neutrality has cost. We cannot shelter if the city collapses around us."

A murmur. Rue felt the memory of Elaine's words—"transparency is a ledger's cousin"—slide behind her eyes.

The Headmistress lifted a hand. "We will make a civic pledge. We will offer the kitchens and a measured rotation of labour hours as a civic contribution. We will not hand names. We will keep records so that any claim may be accounted for and argued on the house's terms. I will instruct the clerks to draw a civic ledger entry now, stating what we pledge to spare."

The words were a cut. A bargain wrapped as protection. Someone near the front hissed; a young apprentice threw up her hands as if the air itself had been made heavy.

Loren's jaw tightened. "So we bind hours in exchange for shelter?" he asked, voice more dangerous than Rue had heard it in years.

The Headmistress's answer was steady. "We bind counted aid, not persons. That is our line. We offer what can be measured and reclaimed. It keeps names from being traded." Her eyes skimmed the benches as if to see whether the house accepted the calculus.

A clerk moved to a drawer and produced a narrow register, the kind Rue had seen in the Ink Room. He opened it beneath the lamp and set a pen in readiness. "Civic pledge—kitchens, rotation of labour to be scheduled and recorded," he murmured as he wrote the heading aloud. His voice had the official brass of ritual. Fingers pointed to columns; numbers were left blank for the house's accounting.

There was argument immediate and sharp. A woman who ran the garden rotas spoke up: "If we promise labour, the girls will lose hours for their apprenticeships. That reduces their trade, not only their time."

"We will structure rota so training continues," the Headmistress said. "We will not break apprenticeship promise. We will prioritise the inexperienced under shelter."

"And who decides?" someone demanded. "You, from your desk? The clerks? The Envoy's counsel?"

A shout rose—an old, raw sound from a bench where someone remembered scullery weeks. Rue felt it like a struck rod. The Hall had split into people who trusted the ledger to save them and people who had seen ledgers take more than they gave.

Rue thought of her own signing, the wax, the signet, the seam where 'tethering' had been pressed. She felt pulled: to the Headmistress, who preserved roofs and work; to Loren, who warned that counted hours could harden into obligations no one meant to sign for. She walked to him as the clerks wrote the civic heading, feeling the pull of both arguments.

"You know counts," she said to Loren quietly. "If we pledge hours, can we keep apprentices' craft?"

He looked at her as if weighing a stitch. "We can try to shape the rota. But the ledger will be used by others. Records make things legible. Once legible, they can be transferred. The binding—legal or otherwise—follows a tally. Be careful."

The Headmistress, seeing the stir, rose and addressed the room. "We will make the pledge public and record it in the house logs. We will name no individuals. We will set limits. We will review claims as they come to counsel."

That was the choice: a public bargain recorded as a civic pledge, offering counted aid rather than names. The Envoy would have what he wanted—an account to read—and the house would answer with its books. To some that would be compromise and prudence; to others, a surrender of principle.

A small girl at the front—a new apprentice—began to cry. Rue stepped toward her, smoothing the girl's sleeve with hands that had tied many ribbons and steadied many stitches. The Headmistress touched the girl's shoulder with a counter of comfort and calculation. "We will shelter you," she said, voice clear. "We will write what we pledge and we will hold to it."

Loren's hand went to his coat pocket, where he had kept an old scrap of folded cloth for luck. He did not speak, but his eyes met Rue's and in them she read both warning and instruction: hold the craft; keep the counters honest.

Outside the Hall the harbor light had changed. The ten-day clock ticked on the walls of Rue's mind. The Headmistress's decision would be written into house logs; the clerks would note it, the rotas would shift, and somewhere—she did not know where—sponsors and city counsel would translate the numbers into need.

Rue left the Hall with her hands in her apron and the Folio's leather like a pressure in her palms of memory. The house would count; the world would count back. The choice had been made for now. It forced a division that would not be healed by a single ledger entry.

They said Elaine would come back like a story—soft on the edges, the kind you pass around a bench to steady a night. When she came into the Hall, she came like proof: not triumphant but plain, a scar at her temple pulling the light into a thin, private line.

The benches shifted when she walked to the lectern; the hundred held its breath the way a person holds a measuring cup steady. Rue stood near the side, hands folded into the cuff of her apron, and felt a small, precise astonishment at the sight of her. Elaine had been the name people used when they wanted to believe something could be done—soft praise on a poster—then a private hope passed hand to hand. Now she was neither poster nor pamphlet but a woman with a narrow set of shoulders and a look that suggested she had counted the cost of things.

The Headmistress rose with the utmost courtesy and introduced Elaine as one of the Academy's returned. "We welcome testimony of life beyond our walls," she said, voice even and gleaming. "Tell us, Elaine."

Elaine's voice came without ceremony. She set her palm on the lectern as if steadying a page and spoke to the benches more than to the lectern. "Markets have narrowed," she said. "Sponsors keep accounts that have teeth. There are bargains made in port houses and in sponsor ledgers—debts tied to ships' manifests; claims that ask for more than coin. I do not say the Academy trades in names, but I say this: the world outside keeps accounts that are not always written in the Folio's neat hand."

The words went through the room like a draught. They did what a sudden wind does to loose paper: exposed the seams. The Headmistress's face did not betray the flinch Rue expected. But her gloves tightened, and the polite smile thinned at the edges. "We publish successes and we celebrate return," the Headmistress said slowly. "The stories of Elaine and others are public examples of craft and steadiness. If there have been arrangements beyond the public pages, they are matters for the house's counsel and procedure."

"Arrangements," Elaine said, and the single word made the benches speak in whispers. "I learned a trade beyond the town when I left. I found sponsors. I found ways to feed a scar. Those sponsors asked for accounting in turn. Sometimes accounting is coin. Sometimes it is names or hours. The forms change, Headmistress. The cost is not always visible where you set your lectern." Her look swept the room and rested on Rue for a second—an acknowledgement, a passing of a thing that had been said elsewhere.

The Headmistress stepped forward, palms flat, the posture of someone restoring a document's margin to order. "We do not trade our girls as goods. The Academy's charge is to train, shelter and provide trade. We will not—"

A voice cut through then, not from the benches but from the far end of the Hall: a kitchen woman who had seen tides and barns and men go hungry. "When grain is short and the city calls, accounts are tallied in ways that leave no loose sums," she said, voice blunt and sure. "We all know what counting can ask."

Elaine let a small, humorless smile touch her mouth. "Transparency is a ledger's cousin," she said. "You can put down what you choose to tally. You can turn a name into a line if that's what the ledger asks. I'm here to say that sometimes the world asks for entries that are not made in a public page. And when it asks, the choices someone makes are not merely administrative. They mark a life."

Rue felt, in the back of her throat, the remembering of her own signing: the wax, the signet, the small sharp breath when the pen left the paper. The word 'tethering' slid like a memory along her ribs. She thought of small favors that had been tallied as debt, of Loren's soft thumbs correcting her stitch, of a tally slid with a pen no one noticed.

The Headmistress, for the first time that morning, looked less like a clerk and more like someone who kept a whole house's decisions in her hands. "We serve the city," she said, voice steady but thinned. "Where obligations are raised we will review them and seek to protect those under our care. I will not have the Academy's example used as a cudgel where we can conceive better—"

"Conceive better," Elaine echoed, not unkindly. "Sometimes those better conceptions are made at a price. I have come to say that leaving was not only leaving but also taking a place in other ledgers. It made my life possible, and it made me owe things that are not all paid in coin."

A young apprentice near the front sobbed softly, the sound small and jagged. The Hall shuttered inward for a moment as if to keep the sound from flying out. Rue moved to the girl's side as others did; she felt the tautness of fear that the war had leveled into a new kind of accounting.

The Headmistress, seeing the apprentice, softened and stepped toward the benches. She took the girl's hand with an efficiency that was almost tenderness. "We will shelter those under our charge," she said to the room, then, carefully, to the girl: "We will not let you be taken as currency. We will record, we will feed, we will hold." Her voice had the cleric's cadence of promise—and the clerk's addendum: "And we will record it."

Elaine inclined her head slightly, the small motion of someone who has been offered a measure and can name its limit. "Remember what you tell the girls," she said. "The world outside will always have its own ledgers. Know which ones you let count you into them."

She stepped down from the lectern without fanfare. The Hall stayed a little unsettled after she left, the way a table stays trembling after someone has set a weight upon it and gone. Rue touched the margin of her own faith and found it thinner; the Headmistress's public lines had been complicated in one tidy moment by a life that could not be reduced to a pamphlet.

When the clerk closed the sponsorship ledger and slid it back into the drawer, he did it with a small, perfunctory flourish. The room bent back to business with a new thread in its pattern: the Headmistress would defend with rhetoric; some returns carried costs not spelled out in the Folio; whatever the Academy promised, the world outside kept its own accounts, and those could touch the girls in ways the house's pages did not always show.

Rue had taken to standing near the lectern when there were affairs of weight in the Hall; it put the harbor in the windows behind her and let her watch both the city and the house at once. The benches were full in a different way than before—faces alert, fingers folded, a dozen small cloths already smoothed for someone who might be called to hurry.

The War Envoy moved with the same patient, economy she had seen at the steps: precise, unadorned, his coat buttoned against the cold. He did not carry a banner; he carried a notice and a manner that read like a law. When he spoke he did not ask for entry so much as present it, as if the war itself had already written the words for him.

"The island is drawn into the campaign," he said, voice even. "Our supply lines shorten and our needs increase. We come not to take from you blind, but to ask of what can be measured and offered. In time, claims will be made for bonds or levies; in time, support of manpower may be required. We expect counsel and an answer in the period given."

The phrasing was careful—no single demand yet, only the architecture of demands. It was worse for being schematic. Rue heard the list and did the arithmetic: bonds; levies; manpower. Each word landed like a weight placed on the Hall's long table.

The Headmistress heard it the way she heard everything—slowly, as if testing a measure against a rule. She did not blush and she did not bristle; she arranged her reply with the precision of a clerk. "The Mercy Academy is a house of instruction and shelter," she said, palms folded at her wrist. "We can muster kitchens, offer shelter, and keep neutrality within our walls for those under our charge. We will not give names of our girls as a commodity."

There was a small, audible intake in the benches at her refusal. The Envoy's face did not change; his eyes were the unblinking calculation of a ledger. "Neutrality may be of value, but neutrality is accounted for. Kitchens and store are matters of grain and pot; shelter is matter of space and time. The city will ask for something in return—guarantees that sustain the war effort."

The Headmistress's reply turned soft at the edges but not conciliatory. "We will account for what we can spare. We will offer kitchens and shelter as humanitarian assistance. Where civic claims ask for manpower, we will consider how best to meet obligations without breaking our trust to the house's charge. We will not hand over our charges as raw conscription."

It was a bargain wrapped in a refusal and a promise. Rue heard the difference between the two as plainly as if she had been taught to read ink: the Headmistress protected the house's people by moving the payment to accounting—by promising to make a ledger of what the house would give. She would trade counted things, not names.

Someone in the benches could not hold it. A young apprentice—no more than a handful of years beneath the Academy's roof—stood before the Headmistress as if the Hall's ritual let her speak. Her voice trembled, small and raw: "If they come for people, what will we do? What will happen to those with ten years left?"

Her question landed like a thin bell. The Headmistress's eyes softened, and for the first time Rue saw a flash of the woman behind the gloves. "We will shelter those under our charge," the Headmistress said, and there was a syllable in her tone that was promise. Then she added, immediately, with the same clerkly precision: "And we will account it. We will keep a record—who is housed, who is fed, what is pledged. Those records will be how we bargain." The words were both comfort and contract.

The Envoy inclined his head. "Records are useful," he said. "They make claims legible. They will be considered in counsel."
