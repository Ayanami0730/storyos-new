The blue door comes up like a goalpost ahead of us, and the porch smells exactly as I remembered: "A tidy blue door set on a low porch where ferns droop in green pots; the porch boards smell of lemon oil and old tea." I feel the brooch in my palm like a small, guilty coin and the tin safe in Jun's pack like a promise that will be kept.

Mrs. Halloway opens the door before we knock, as if she has been listening for footsteps. Her face folds into a kind, surprised smile when she sees us. "Oh my—it's home. Thank you, darling. Some things like to be put back where they belong," she says, and my knees go light.

I hold the brooch out on my palm like an offering. Her fingers hover and then close around it with a gentleness that surprises me. She turns it over between her thumb and forefinger and her eyes go somewhere soft. "This brooch belonged to my mother. She wore it when she forgave someone she thought she could never forgive — and it somehow reminded her that courage could be small and steady, like returning a thing where it ought to be." Her voice holds the kind of distance stories have when they have already been digested.

Jun nudges his backpack forward and I put the tin into her hands. He grins at me sideways, embarrassed and proud all at once. Mrs. Halloway taps the brooch on her palm as if checking that it is really here and then she reaches to the little dish on her hallway table and fishes out something wrapped in tissue.

"You two have more courage than you know. Take this — a stamped token from my knitting group. For when you need to remember today." She presses a small brass coin into my hand; it's stamped with a clumsy thimble and worn smooth at the edges. It fits in my palm like a secret.

We say our thank-yous and a car crunches up the curb. Milo is there again, shuffling like he was meant to be elsewhere. He looks at the brooch and then at us, and his face does something like deciding. He digs into his pocket and scuffs something into our hands — a damp, scuffed toy car — and mutters, "Sorry. Here." It is the least heroic gesture possible, and somehow it matters more for being small.

Mrs. Halloway watches him with a little smile and then clasps the brooch to her cardigan as if it had been waiting for that place all along. "You children have done me a favor I did not know I needed," she says. Her hands are steady when she arranges the fern pots and the door shuts with a polite creak.

Jun and I stand on the sidewalk with the brass coin between us and the afternoon folding around our knees. The tin is returned and the brooch is in her care; the promise has been kept. Milo shuffles away with his pockets a little emptier and his shoulders a fraction straighter.

I put the token in my pocket and feel the day settle into something that will photograph itself in my memory. Ordinary chores are brave quests when you share them with a friend.
