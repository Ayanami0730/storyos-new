- loc-riverbank: A stretch of muddied bank below the bridge, cluttered with reeds, fallen branches and the current's eddies. The place where the brass collar is lost and recovered.

- obj-cardboard-map: A piece of cardboard Hollis has turned into a map with inked landmarks and dramatic annotations. It organizes the quest and names places in Arthurian fashion.
- obj-wooden-staff: An old broom handle Hollis has lacquered with tape and a broken paintbrush tied to one end. Becomes a staff, then a sword and a tool for reaching and balance.
- obj-knotted-rope: A short coil of rope Arden carries because Mrs. Blythe insisted on some precaution. In the quest it becomes a lifeline and a symbol of the oath between friends.

### world-rules  <world/rules.yaml>

These govern what is TRUE. They do not say what anyone KNOWS.
A character knows a rule only where canon says so; otherwise they may be ignorant of it, wrong about it, or in the middle of working it out — and if the story is about someone discovering one of these, writing it into their thoughts early is how that story gets destroyed.

- Imagination is powerful but not supernatural: when the children call objects by quest-names (e.g., 'King's Sigil') or imagine transformations, only their perception and behavior change; physical laws (gravity, water current, friction) remain real and must be respected in the plot.
- All action takes place within a single afternoon; no characters or events occur off-stage unless previously established in the plan (no surprise visitors or miraculous helpers).
- Objects keep consistent physical properties across scenes (the obj-king-collar is small brass and can be lost in reeds; the obj-wooden-staff is a broom handle, sturdy but not magical).
- Characters cannot be in two locations at once and must behave in ways consistent with their sketches (Hollis is imaginative and loud; Arden is quieter and cautious).
- The story may echo Arthurian motifs (oaths, naming, coronation), but there is no real magic or prophecy—these are narrative devices the children use to give meaning to ordinary acts.
