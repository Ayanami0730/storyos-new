I asked them all back into the study—Rao because he needed the record, Henry and Marta because they were the ledger's axis, and Derek because he had been the quiet presence. We set a spare plank across the hearth and, with Rao watching every move, built a safe mock-up from the clockwork's visible parts. Safety came first; the demonstration was a simulation, not an attempt to make anything lethal on the spot.

"Watch the rod," I told them. I let the wound spring run under supervision. The cam turned, the push-rod struck the plank with a dry, hard thud and a small split opened where the wood took the blow. It was not a cinematic thrust; it was a single, precise, designed action. The plank showed the line where a narrow rod would pass. I tapped the split with my knuckle so they could see the angle.

"That could go through a watch-case," I said. "Straight, fast, no theatrics. The timing is the point."

I picked up the flake of red sealing-wax from where I'd pocketed it. "This was on the mantel that morning," I said, and then, because the catalogue exists even when the files do not, I told them what evidence boards later confirmed: a matching impression had been found on a crate label in Derek's shed. Their faces tightened; a small, guilty press took Derek's jaw.

Next I set the brass plate where everyone could see the tiny nick. "I recognised this at once," I said. I told them how the nick is a maker's tic, the sort of filing mark a man leaves like a signature. Derek's hand went to his sleeve as if to cover a tool. He was quiet; the room got very small.

I laid out the logic plainly. The device is one shot; the push-rod needed only a short travel; the watch shows a puncture that matches a narrow, driven rod; the scrap in Evelyn's hand was a schedule, broken into slots around the hour; the garden ladder and the scuff on the hatch made the cellar route practical. Put the pieces together and the path is bench, ladder, hatch, hearth.

A silence followed the list, the kind that has the weight of accusation without the phrase. Derek looked at the plank, looked at the nick, and then at Henry and Marta. "They—" he began, and then stopped, the word breaking off like a live wire.

He had a last, clumsy reflex. He lunged for the ashtray on the desk as if to throw it, a desperate gesture that was too late. Rao was quicker: a hand on his shoulder, a cuff on his wrist. Marta cried out. Henry rose but did not strike. Derek's shoulders sagged the instant he found himself held.

When the motion left him he spoke in a low, level voice that had the honesty of defeat. "I was paid," he said. "Paid to take something and make it look like—like an accident. She was to be... silenced. There was a debt. I was told to fetch a page. I didn't mean—" The confession was not a ledger entry and it did not quote the scrap; it gave motive as money and retrieval, which is different from a transcript but perfectly sufficient to explain why he had tools, access and opportunity.

Rao read the admission into his notebook. He would add the formalities, the photographs and the evidence numbers later; the custody chain and lab reports still had to be written. I did not pretend the case was closed by words alone—those come later in a court and in reports. But confession, a matched wax impression, a maker's tic and the physical demonstration had converted hypothesis into something that could be charged and tested.

When Derek stopped talking, the room felt like a workbench after the lathe is turned off: the components are all there and the shape they made is visible. I told them quietly, while Rao made the notes that would become paperwork, that a craftsman's skill is a moral tool as much as a useful one. There is a fine edge between making and making harm. Derek had crossed it; that is what invention looks like when it is used as a weapon.
