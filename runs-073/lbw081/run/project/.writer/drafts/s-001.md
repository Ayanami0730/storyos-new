People had gathered before I stepped over the threshold: a pair of uniforms doing their polite, slow circuit, Henry Crane standing as if he were a model of restraint, Marta planted like a hinge beside the desk, Derek from next door lingering in the hall with the careful stillness of a man who watches mechanisms, and Inspector Rao keeping to the edge with his notebook closed but ready.

The heavy oak door's deadbolt had been slid from the inside. The sash window was latched; the sill showed a faint dusting of garden pollen. Light lay across the floorboards in a flat, indifferent pool. Evelyn lay where she had fallen, half-turned toward the fireplace; her fingers were clenched around a small, stubborn thing.

Her hand still clutched an engraved pocket watch; its hands had stopped at 9:07, and the case bore a hairline puncture and a faint film of oil. A small folded scrap of paper was clenched in her fingers, sealed with a trace of red sealing-wax. On the desk a crystal ashtray held three short, rough-ended butts of a coarse tobacco I did not recognise from the household's usual brand. Near the mantel I found a small flake of red sealing-wax.

There was the smell of machine oil about the hearth—sharp and metallic in the air, like a lathe left running and then abandoned. It sat in the room as insistently as any witness.

Those are the things the room offers first: how it was closed, what was in a body's hold, what time a stopped instrument insists upon. But there were other absences I noticed at once. I had not seen any precise wording of Henry Crane's statement to the press. There was no record I could find of how long Marta Reyes had worked here. I couldn't tell from the material in my hands whether Rao and I had ever met before. The scrap bore a two-line cipher; I had not been given a transcription of its exact letters. What I lacked was a close, micro-description of the study's carpets and mantel—those small details the inventory summary overlooks.

Those absences matter. Henry's grief read public and sharp; he held himself with the taut courtesy of someone who thinks performance protects feeling. Marta moved around the desk as if its surface were hers to defend: a hand on a corner of the blotter, a look that dared anyone to reach for the watch. Derek's presence was a different note—he lingered like a neighbour who knows the machinery of houses and notices when a machine smells wrong.

Rao and I exchanged the professional gestures required of men who are to be expected to work together: a nod, a brief mention that evidence would be photographed and the scene logged. The study would be sealed again. For now, there was only the list: a deadbolt slid from the inside; a latched sash window; a stopped watch beneath a dead woman's hand reading 9:07 with a puncture and oil on its case; wax on the note and a flake by the mantel; a crystal ashtray with three out-of-place cigarette butts; the smell of machine oil at the hearth. And the gaps—the things I could not yet put in my ledger.

I made notes. Photographing and cataloguing are someone else's ordinance; deduction is mine. A locked room is a contract that begins with a set of physical sentences and leaves you to supply the connecting verbs. I closed my notebook on that edge and let the room keep its silence a little longer—waiting is part of listening.
