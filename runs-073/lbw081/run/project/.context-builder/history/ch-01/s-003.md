### previous-scene  <novel/chapters/ch-01/scenes/s-002.md>

I started where the smell was strongest and worked outwards. Inspector Rao and I prised up the study's low hearth strip; it came away complaining. Behind it was a narrow pocket, black with old soot and the kind of neatness houses give to things they'd rather forget. Nestled there was a compact clockwork — tiny plates of brass, a wound spring and a cam-driven push-rod — its surfaces darkened where oil had pooled.

"Bag it and photograph it," I said. "Label the cavity." Rao's pen made the concession of the official record and the room rearranged itself into objects to be logged.

I handled the piece with gloved care. The oil on its plates matched the smudge on the watch-case Clara—no, Evelyn—had been holding. (Names have a way of slipping in a room like this; I corrected myself quietly.) That shared oil made a mechanical link I could not yet account for. One brass plate bore a tiny filing nick, a maker's tic I had seen on pieces worked at Derek Lyle's bench. Recognition points you toward questions; it is not accusation.

After the hearth we moved into the garden. An aluminium extension ladder leaned against the boundary wall; one rung carried a fresh scrape consistent with being used to reach the coal-hatch. The external coal chute hatch's bolt showed recent abrasion and coal dust in the latch groove.

While Rao began the inventory I went through them one by one.

"Where were you at nine?" I asked Henry.

"In the morning room — same chair by the hearth; I was reading the paper. Ask Marta; she brought my tea." He glanced to Marta; his throat tightened.

"Did you see him then?" I asked Marta.

"I was serving breakfast. I put his tea down in the kitchen at nine; he wasn't in the morning room." She pressed her lips and, under her breath, muttered, "She said he'd taken money."

"And you?" I looked at Derek.

"In the shed. Been filing brass all morning — had a stubborn plate this morning." He looked at the disassembled clockwork and his hand went to his shirt as if for a tool. When I said the word "ledger" his jaw flicked; the room took the motion in silence.

There. Henry's chair and Marta's kitchen time could not both be true. Marta's mutter about money gave me the small, sharp motive I needed to file separately: accusation without a page. Derek placed himself with brass and tools and gave me the one concrete connection to the mechanism beyond my recognition.

There were loose motives in the house like pages waiting to be balanced: Evelyn's ledger was spoken of in low tones, overdue accounts hinted at, and a recent angry exchange was referred to without quotation. I had not been shown specific entries, nor the exact text of that conversation, but Marta's aside and the tension between Henry and Marta were concrete enough to be worth following.

The oil smelled of small machines: industry in a house that preferred its mechanisms tucked away. It sat in the hearth, in the cavity and on the watch like a repeated signature. Noticing that the oil had crept into the mechanism's creases and into the punctured watch-case suggested they had been in contact with the same source rather than merely the same room.

Rao was methodical, and he would write where the device had been found. What I did not have was the forensic line that tells you exactly where the oil took hold — mortar, lead hearth, the joining timbers — a small omission that could matter if somebody later argued whether the device had been placed in the cavity or had been there long enough to leave transfer stains. I put a note against the entry for later.

Marta stood with the posture of someone who has kept a household's wrongs in order; Henry's hands were clenched in his pockets; Derek's forearms carried the permanent thumb-gouge of someone at a bench. The ladder and the scuffed bolt on the coal-hatch were, for now, the cleanest things in the narrative: they were the sort of traces you could measure without pleading a story into them.

When Rao moved away to brief his sergeant, I closed my notebook and set a small list against the case: secure and photograph the clockwork, photograph the hatch and ladder, get access to Derek's bench and request a copy of Evelyn's ledger. Concrete verbs, enough to start the work. I folded the book and went back inside.

People had gathered before I stepped over the threshold: a pair of uniforms doing their polite, slow circuit, Henry Crane standing as if he were a model of restraint, Marta planted like a hinge beside the desk, Derek from next door lingering in the hall with the careful stillness of a man who watches mechanisms, and Inspector Rao keeping to the edge with his notebook closed but ready.

The heavy oak door's deadbolt had been slid from the inside. The sash window was latched; the sill showed a faint dusting of garden pollen. Light lay across the floorboards in a flat, indifferent pool. Evelyn lay where she had fallen, half-turned toward the fireplace; her fingers were clenched around a small, stubborn thing.

Her hand still clutched an engraved pocket watch; its hands had stopped at 9:07, and the case bore a hairline puncture and a faint film of oil. A small folded scrap of paper was clenched in her fingers, sealed with a trace of red sealing-wax. On the desk a crystal ashtray held three short, rough-ended butts of a coarse tobacco I did not recognise from the household's usual brand. Near the mantel I found a small flake of red sealing-wax.

There was the smell of machine oil about the hearth—sharp and metallic in the air, like a lathe left running and then abandoned. It sat in the room as insistently as any witness.

Those are the things the room offers first: how it was closed, what was in a body's hold, what time a stopped instrument insists upon. But there were other absences I noticed at once. I had not seen any precise wording of Henry Crane's statement to the press. There was no record I could find of how long Marta Reyes had worked here. I couldn't tell from the material in my hands whether Rao and I had ever met before. The scrap bore a two-line cipher; I had not been given a transcription of its exact letters. What I lacked was a close, micro-description of the study's carpets and mantel—those small details the inventory summary overlooks.

Those absences matter. Henry's grief read public and sharp; he held himself with the taut courtesy of someone who thinks performance protects feeling. Marta moved around the desk as if its surface were hers to defend: a hand on a corner of the blotter, a look that dared anyone to reach for the watch. Derek's presence was a different note—he lingered like a neighbour who knows the machinery of houses and notices when a machine smells wrong.

Rao and I exchanged the professional gestures required of men who are to be expected to work together: a nod, a brief mention that evidence would be photographed and the scene logged. The study would be sealed again. For now, there was only the list: a deadbolt slid from the inside; a latched sash window; a stopped watch beneath a dead woman's hand reading 9:07 with a puncture and oil on its case; wax on the note and a flake by the mantel; a crystal ashtray with three out-of-place cigarette butts; the smell of machine oil at the hearth. And the gaps—the things I could not yet put in my ledger.

I made notes. Photographing and cataloguing are someone else's ordinance; deduction is mine. A locked room is a contract that begins with a set of physical sentences and leaves you to supply the connecting verbs. I closed my notebook on that edge and let the room keep its silence a little longer—waiting is part of listening.
