Lord Harrow discovered the hole in the house's skin at a table in his private room: a leather tray where the signet usually lay was empty. He did not shout; his face went to work in the quiet way of a man who sorted storms into lists. "The ring is missing," he told Mistress Anne when she came with her hands still smelling of the scullery. "Search the crates and the cellar. Bring me anything that has been moved from the orchard to the house today."

Mistress Anne moved with the efficiency of a woman set to unpicking a pattern. She sent a man to the crate store and went herself, pulling linen back from a stack until one crate's lining drew her eye. There, folded into the soft linen among small papers, lay a heavy gold ring stamped with the Harrow crest and a single browned petal as if a blossom had been pressed and kept with the writing. She drew them out and brought them up the stone steps under Anne's steady hands.

Lord Harrow held the ring and turned the crest like a question. "Where did you find it?" he asked. Mistress Anne answered plainly: "In the lining of a cider crate, my lord. Among some papers. It was a crate that came from the orchard this morning; Thomas Byrd handled the orchard runs today." Her voice was neither loud nor small; it was a ledger.

The ring on the table made the room feel colder. Lord Harrow set it down as if it were a verdict and called for the family to come to the chapel. The great pews took their weight like a court. Lady Margaret Stone sat near the front, hands folded in the way propriety taught a young woman to make herself an instrument of the house. The carvings under the stained glass made small noises when people shifted; the light cut the room into the house's colors and made the ring look like a question burned into gold.

Mistress Anne stood before them and laid out her small chronology: the missing apron, the scrap of paper that had fallen from a crate, the pot-washer who had found it and given it to her. She held the scrap up so the narrow of the chapel could see the clear, hurried hand. "This was found in the lining of the crate," she said. "These papers were folded into the linen. The crate came from the orchard on Thomas Byrd's watch. He lingered often by the wall, and I saw a man tuck things into a lining while he worked."

Lord Harrow's brows closed. He turned then to Thomas himself and, with the formalities of the house about him, asked the servant to speak. Thomas's hands were stained with orchard soil; he kept himself small in a way that attempted dignity. "I have no ring, my lord," he said. "I have only what I was given to carry." The phrase meant more than the words; it set his place.

Against that, Anne laid the evidence: the ring, the scrap in her palm, and a pressed blossom found with them. A woman from the chapel pew—Evelyn Harrow—stood at once and went to the rail as if the bars were suddenly fewer than the air beyond them. Her voice when she spoke was close and terrible in its steadiness.

"It is true," she said. "He is mine. Thomas and I—" She stopped once, the breath taken like a small animal. "I gave him my mother's locket. I have written to him. I have met him here, in the orchard. If there is blame to be borne, let it be mine." She did not beg. The confession fell like a stone into the room and the room kept its echo.

Lord Harrow rose; the chapel was a thing he could command like a ship. "Honour is our law," he said, and his voice filled the carved space. "This house answers for what its name implies. There will be a hearing at dawn to determine the house's sentence. Until then both the ring and the papers will be kept in my charge; Mistress Anne, you will catalogue and secure them." He did not pronounce a punishment—yet—but the gravity of his words set the hours dangerous.

Lady Margaret watched Evelyn with an expression that was not unkind but wholly uninvested in mercy; she was the house's future, and her look was of someone measuring whether the wood beneath her feet would hold. Thomas bowed his head and did not look to Evelyn; the act read like a final accounting.

Mistress Anne stepped forward and placed the scrap and the ring on the table before Lord Harrow. "I observed patterns, my lord," she said quietly. "The missing apron, the scrap in the crate, his lingering by the wall. I bring them as I said I would." Her testimony had the shape of a hand that had folded a small thing into its palm and then changed its mind about what to do with it.

Evelyn's confession left the chapel as if the air itself had been altered. The hearing at dawn—Lord Harrow's promise of decision—hung over them like a bell to be struck. Outside, the orchard kept its hush, the same coping under the trees catching the light as if nothing had changed; inside the house, the law had already begun to make replacements where love had been.

The orchard held the late light like a secret it had been saving. Apple and pear trees threaded the air with a fragrance that made the house's polished words feel thin and brittle; a low stone wall, its broad coping smoothed by years of sitting, cut the world off from the manor. Shadows pooled under the trees, and a worn bench took the sun in a strip of warmth.

Evelyn had slipped away with more ceremony than she meant — an absent curtsey here, a hand delayed at the hem of a visiting lady's sleeve. The house was loud with plans; the orchard was not. She kept her skirts shallow from the soil and walked where the light let her see the dust motes lift like small approvals.

He was there with a shallow basket at his feet, hands grey with oiled leather and earth. Thomas's back was to her when she approached; he was loosing a twig that had bound itself into a collar. He turned at the sound of her breath.

He straightened with the steadiness of someone who had practised being small in rooms that expected his boots to be silent. "Miss Evelyn," he said, the name careful and low. He set the basket down with both hands. "I've a basket for the scullery."

Her throat tightened at the plainness of that. The orchard had its own grammar: people spoke less of who they would please and more of what the wind picked up. She sat at the bench so that the warm wood held her like an answer.

"We've only ever had stolen glances and passing bows," she said, and the sentence was almost a question.

He laughed, the sound small. "There has been nothing like this, no. Not ever, Miss." He kept the soft distance that spared them both.

She took from under her bodice a small coin-warm weight. It was cold silver at first, then warmed by her palm. She opened it with fingers that remembered a hand that was not there.

Inside was a tiny rose chased into the metal, and beneath it a pressed blossom, browned at the edges — pressed earlier that season while the trees still bloomed — but still smelling faintly of apple. Along the hinge, inside the lid, two initials had been scratched small enough to be private: E. H.

She said, simply, "It was my mother's. On her deathbed she put it into my hand and told me, \"Keep the heart she loved safe.\"" The words were a thing she had kept folded until she could not hold them longer. "I want you to have it."

He took it as if the world might tilt; his fingers closed over the locket with a gentleness that said he knew how easily entrusted things could break. He did not open it again — the sight had been hers to give. "I will keep it, Miss Evelyn," he said. "I will not show it to anyone." The promise came from a place where promises were small and exact.

She watched his hands. Earth had worked its way into the ridges of his palms, callused crescents at his thumbs where rope had bitten him. He folded the locket into a pocket of plain cloth and held it to the hollow of his shirt as if it might find room there.

The basket by his feet rattled; the apple skins carried a warm, thin perfume of sugar and sun. "I'll take these to the scullery now, then," he said, as if the world outside the wall required his footsteps. "They'll make tonight's puddings." The sentence belonged to any of his days and yet, in the way he said it, left room for staying a little longer.

She wanted to say everything — that the house's rules were a chain she had been born into, that she hated being measured by expectations she had not asked for — but she put her hand into the pocket of her skirt and felt the outline of a folded scrap of linen, a private map of what she would and would not risk. The orchard softened decisions; it did what walls do when two people want to be small together.

He bowed his head once, a careful shape of habit and respect. "If you need me," he said, and he did not say how or when, leaving those for breath and for the secret meetings that might come.

She rose; for a moment she let the sun lay itself over her shoulders, a warmth that did not count her hours. "Good-bye, Thomas," she said.

He watched her go until the skirts blended with the path and the coping hid her. When the manor's murmur returned through the stone, he tucked his hand into the pocket that held the locket and felt the small, sharp comfort of silver against his palm.
