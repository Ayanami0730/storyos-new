Dawn came as a thin, hard thing over the orchard, the coping slick with mist. The household gathered with a practised hush; men-at-arms stood near the gate like an ordinary detail of the morning. Lord Harrow stood with folded hands as the sentence was carried out, his face a map of duty that did not look like grief.

They brought Thomas down the path in a plain way, as if to a task. He kept his shoulders steady, the silver locket still warm against his shirt. There was no pleading from him when the noose was fitted; the orchard's light made the rope a small, functional thing. Mistress Anne had, before the hour, catalogued the ring and the letters and set them in a small wooden box, laying each folded paper flat as if to preserve its shame.

Lord Harrow spoke once, the voice that had commanded hearth and house for years. "By the law of Harrow, for a breach of trust that stains the family name, this man is to be hanged at dawn." The words finished and the world obeyed. They hanged Thomas Byrd at the orchard wall at dawn.

The rope took him quickly. The silver locket lay against his shirt, its rose catching the weak light as the knot did its work. Where it had been pressed by a palm the cloth darkened; hands that had been steady in the scullery now moved with the same efficiency and lifted what must be lifted.

Evelyn watched from the coping, a figure carved in the thin light. She had come because names must be faced, not because she expected mercy. When the men stepped back and the work was done, she climbed the wall without asking and looked down at the yard as if it were a map she had misread.

She whispered the injunction her mother had left her—told to guard what the heart had loved—and the words fell between them like a last bequest. For a single instant the orchard held its breath.

Then Evelyn threw herself from the orchard wall. The drop was brief and decisive; the hush that followed was longer. Men moved with the same ordered hands and set her where they set him. The letters lay in their folded linen, unread. The little wooden box with the ring sat closed.

Mistress Anne crouched beside the bodies when the work allowed it. She opened the small box to check the papers again and then, with a motion almost private, slipped Thomas's silver locket into the inner pocket of her apron. After the bodies were carried away, Anne kept the locket close in her apron.

She boxed the Harrow ring and the folded papers and carried them into the chapel where Lord Harrow waited to give his account to the house. "As I observed, patterns emerged—the missing apron, the scrap in the crate, his lingering by the wall," she told him again, and her voice had the flatness of work completed. She had done what she had said she would: she had observed and she had brought the evidence.

Lord Harrow made no speech beyond the facts he had already declared. The manor resumed its hours with the same rigor as always; servants were given new tasks and old routines were reasserted. Outside, the orchard kept its hush, the coping slick with dew and catching the first weak light. The unread letters lay folded in linen, the ring was boxed and set aside, and the silver rose stayed warm in Mistress Anne's palm—an act of pity that would be whispered about or buried in silence.

Order returned; the house, having balanced its accounts by the measure it knew, carried forward. The orchard kept its witness, and small things—the pressed petal, the closed box, the hidden locket—remained to mark what had been taken from it.
