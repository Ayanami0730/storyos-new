The orchard kept its hush as if secrecy had been planted along with the trees. Light sifted through leaves and landed on the same worn coping that had sheltered their first meeting; where the wall shaded the ground the air smelled of blossom and cool damp earth. Thomas moved among the trunks like someone tracing a familiar map, tucking small folded papers into the soft linen that lined a cider crate.

He did not wait for the sun to insist on setting. When Evelyn slipped through the low gate she carried the same impatience she carried everywhere: a wish for the rest of the day to stop deciding for her. They spoke no names; their mouths had learned the economy of moments. She slid her hand to the hollow at his shirt where the locket rested; the silver was a small, steady weight against the cloth.

From the crate came the faint scent of crushed apples and linen warmed by the sun. Thomas drew a letter free and held it to the light long enough for her to see the edges of a quick, familiar hand. The first line he read—soft and without show—was not a flourish but a small command: "Hide me in the places they do not look for me. Stay away from them, not from me."

That tenderness—urgent, blunt—made her stomach go light and dangerous. She folded her hands around the coping and let the board press between her palms like a secret. He slid the paper into the crate's lining and smoothed the linen so the fold would not give under inspection; the crate was ordinary to those who watched for banners, but its linen moved between orchard and cellar with modest attention.

They spoke then of small things: which tree had given best fruit last year, what the scullery asked of him that week, where the path had made a new rut. The talk kept danger light by being ordinary. When she leaned forward at some private joke, his fingers brushed hers—an almost accidental contact, and therefore explainable.

In the heat of the kitchen the world was all steam and clatter. Bread rose like pale suns in steel bowls; the scullery boys moved in a practiced swarm. Mistress Anne's eye, which had learned to read the room, saw patterns where others saw only motion. A maid burst in, breath gone from running. "Mistress, my apron is gone," she said. "I left it by the drain and it's no longer there."

Mistress Anne folded her hands, listening as if she could weigh mischief by the way the maid's breath hit the air. She sent a girl to check the crate store without hurry; such things could be misplaced. In the cellar the crates waited in rows, smelling of sun and yeast. A pot-washer, moving a crate to the press, frowned when a scrap of paper slid from the lining and fell into his palm.

The scrap was small and creased, a sliver of handwriting more private than the workday allowed. He handed it up to Mistress Anne. She read enough to see the curl of a particular letter and the urgency of a short instruction, and folded the scrap into her palm as one folds a small thing that might become evidence or consolation. She did not read it aloud; she did not need to. The hand that wrote it told her there was a line of service and something beneath it she had no authority to name.

That evening, as dusk made the orchard a silhouette of trunks, Thomas lingered on the far side of the coping, looking toward the house. He touched the hollow at his shirt where the locket rested and listened to a distant laugh from the manor that sounded like a bartering of futures. Mistress Anne watched him from the scullery door, a tray forgotten at her hip. Her face did not flinch; she measured and held.

When the maid came to tell her the scrap had been found, Anne folded it again into the palm of her hand and kept it there. She did not yet raise the matter to Lord Harrow—there was time for patterns to prove themselves—but she set a weight to the evening now in place of the house's ordinary hum. The small paper in her palm had the potential to be a slip or a snare; she would let it become one or the other on its own timetable.

Thomas crossed the yard and, as he did each night, felt the weight of the locket like a pulse at his chest. Evelyn slipped back through the gate, shoulders squared to go where the house kept its rehearsals for life. The orchard held them both for a moment more, an interval the house could not see into, and then the ordinary world reclaimed them with its chores, its calls, its watchful women who could fold a scrap into a palm and set ruin in motion.
