The platform smelled like the town — yeast and sugar from the bakery, a faint oil of damp timber — and under it all the steady hiss of a kettle from an upstairs kitchen. From the main street came the bakery's bell and the smell of baking; a kettle hissed through an open kitchen window. Ellie stood with the battered bench at her back, her grey suitcase at her feet, the blue cloth strip knotted once around its handle.

She held the little boat as if it might move on its own, cupped in both hands. The varnished grain fit into the hollows of her palms; the wood was warm from Mr. Hart's fingers. He had been in his shed all morning, she remembered, the way you remember the exact angle of a light when it has to do a job. He pressed a thin, hand-carved sail—bright with fresh varnish—into the boat's mast, where the wood fit it as though it had always been there.

Rufus padded up beside her and sniffed the boat twice, as if to reassure himself it was the same talisman he'd watched her cradle since he was a scruff of dog. He limped to the foot of the train steps, hesitated, then sat on the platform and watched. His collar caught the morning sun in a dull, familiar way.

Mr. Hart folded his hands once and handed her the notebook. The cloth cover looked smaller than she remembered, corners softened, the spine taped in a long, careful strip. Before she could find words, he eased a folded scrap of paper into the notebook's taped spine, the corner of it warm from his thumb. She felt the small square press against her palm, a private weight.

There were no speeches. Mr. Hart's blessing smelled of sawdust and lemon oil and was as plain as everything in Marlow: a new sail to catch whatever came next, a scrap of paper tucked away where only she would find it. He touched the boat with the heel of his hand, as if to set the varnish, and then he stepped back to let something that had been steady for years make its small movement.

Ellie set the notebook under her arm so the taped spine made a soft bar against her ribs. The suitcase sat close enough she could catch it with her toe. She let Rufus bump her shin with his snout once, the way he always did when she needed to remember home, and then she climbed the train steps with the boat cupped in both hands and the notebook tucked under her arm.

She did not slam the door on Marlow. The doors closed softly on her; through the window Marlow slid back, small and steady — the bakery's bell still ringing, the low curve of the main street leading the eye toward the sea. Rufus watched from the platform, then turned and padded slowly toward the lane. The boat lay warm on her lap, the folded scrap of paper hidden in the notebook, and the town unspooled itself into a square of glass.
