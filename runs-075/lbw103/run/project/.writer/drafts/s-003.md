Notebook — 22:12

The rail yard at night is all edges. Freight cars like sleeping whales, chains that held once now list with rust. You think the dark hides you and then it remembers to breathe: a boot on gravel, a tin can rolling, a sleeve catching a spike. We crept with our shoulders turned to the black, the truck's engine gone quiet behind us.

I checked the shotgun before we left the tailgate. Five shells, I told myself—Dan had fired one in the ER and that took a weight off my hands and left a new one where fear lives. I clicked the safety and slid the stock under my arm like a promise.

The red can still sat in the bed, six liters lazy in its belly. The flare was folded in its pouch, unused; I could feel the metal in my pack like a heart that might beat for us or against us.

We moved low between cars, whispering like thieves. River Road ran nearby, a ribbon dark and useless. The rail yard has places you can hide — low reeds and a mud lip where the river leans — but hiding is a trick; the yards are open when the moon is gone and sound is an accusation.

Lina walked too fast. She kept coming up behind us and then darting again like a dog with a hummingtail. Her feet kept telling the world she was made of motion and not waiting. I heard Dan hiss at her and Maya's hand reach for the strap of her pack.

"Keep it slow," Dan breathed. "No sudden moves. Stay with us."

She answered by stepping on a soda can so loud it sounded like someone slamming a door. The sound rolled and took a breath and then something from the far end of the yard answered it — low, wet, like a thing having remembered how to be a person. They came quick because the yard has no small corners.

Maya grabbed Lina and Lina pulled. She yanked free and ran.

"Lina!" Lina's name was a flake; it left both of us after it. I heard the scrape of shoes and the smack of cloth and then a sound I will always know: the ragged catch in another person's throat, the animal sound when humanity leaves the room.

She was twenty yards away when one of them hit her. She fell like a marionette cut. Her hand scrabbled for a rail and didn't find it. Dan and I went toward the noise, Maya toward Lina, Otto groping with his bad knee. I struck out with the pickaxe like I was closing a door. I hit the thing and it made the sound of old wood. Dan fired the shotgun twice — quick and ugly — and the dark around Lina's body was suddenly pieces.

One of them caught her shoulder as she went down and sank teeth into flesh. It was fast and small and perfect in the worst way — Lina's scream cut off and then was gone. There was blood and a ragged, shaking mouth and she didn't get up.

She didn't bite anyone else that I saw. She didn't get up.

The yard answered to Lina's fall. More of them moved in the wash of sound, heads up, stupid and terrible. We dragged what we could; Maya stuffed a handkerchief in the place where the blood wanted to be and tied it. Otto was shaking and muttering numbers; his arm bled under the strip of cloth.

We had to move before dawn, before the sounds of a town that remembered it had been a town pulled everything out of hiding. Dan's face looked older. "We can't carry her and he at the same time," he said. The words were flat. There is a certain math to weight and speed and fuel and grief; we all did that algebra in our heads and found the answer small and sharp.

I pulled the flare out because it felt like the last option that wasn't a sin. "One flare," I said. "We make noise and light on the far side — draw them that way. Then we run the bank and leave at first light."

Maya's hand hovered over the morphine in the med kit. Otto looked at his sleeve where the bite had been. He smiled at me like a teacher who'd been given the wrong answer and then laughed and it broke my chest. "You're not leaving me," he said, more steady than I felt.

I struck the flare. The flare threw up red like a fist. Smoke and a hiss and then the yard turned toward it like cattle to a gate. I rolled it between two freight cars. It skittered and caught and spat flame. For a minute there was a pool of red and the dark moved there.

We ran the riverbank the way we'd practiced only in our heads. The mud took the edges of our boots and the reeds spoke in creaks. It was enough cover to get three of us — Dan, Maya and me — under the treeline. Otto couldn't move the way he used to. He didn't protest much; he sat with his back against a tree and watched Lina's scarf in my hands.

We held a quiet council with our shoulders. "We try the highway at first light," Dan said. "We leave Otto here with blankets and the radio battery — check on him every week—" He said 'week' like it was a breath instrument and not a promise.

Maya's face turned to stone. "We can't carry him and get to the highway," she said. "We get everyone who can go."

Otto's eyes were bright and funny and wholly terrible. He said, "You go. Take the highway. Make it mean something."

I wanted to argue; I wanted a thousand sentences that would make the world bend. Instead I folded a tarp and wrapped it around his shoulders and jammed the med kit into his lap. I put the flare's empty tin by his foot. "We'll check on you at first light," I lied and meant it and didn't.
