Notebook — 14:03

The pump kept clicking and spitting like a patient that won't breathe. We worked at it the same way you work a frozen nut: patient, sideways force, the kind of hands that know where the stubborn lives.

We were already three people too many for comfort in the kiosk when I flipped up the counter light and found the radio half-buried under a stack of dog-eared tabloids. It snapped to life with a cough. Static. A word. Static. Then nothing.

The handheld radio worked, but only in fits — intermittent bursts of signal between the static.

Maya sat on the cooler with her med bag at her knees, eyes like river stones. Dan had his sleeves rolled and a cigarette he wasn't smoking. Otto stared at the street the way he used to stare at the blackboard — patient, fierce. Lina paced and chewed the inside of her cheek until the skin matched her nerves.

"Siphon?" Dan said. He'd already wrestled a length of hose out from under the counter and handed it to me when I blinked. "Pump's dead. No power."

The pump was dead — power out. We siphoned straight from the underground tank and into cans.

We learned how to be efficient quick. Dan held the can, I held the hose, Lina held the tarp under the spout like she had some tiny faith that fabric could stop an ocean. The red jerry can on the seat was roughly six liters of fuel when we finished. I say 'roughly' because my eye is a tool, not a gauge.

The pickup idled in the street, its white paint scored like a grief. Dan's pickup had maybe a quarter tank left — probably ten liters, give or take. He cursed at the gas gauge like you curse at a temperamental dog and came over to help move the cans into the bed.

I checked the shotgun while he worked. Four in the tube, two spares in the glovebox. The shotgun had four in the tube and two spares tucked into the glovebox — six shells total. Not enough for fireworks. Just enough for the first foolish thing that finds us.

"Radio?" Lina asked. She wanted movies and sirens and a story to explain why the town had folded so fast, but she couldn't ask for a better one. We gave her the radio and Dan clipped the clip to his belt like a priest getting ready for mass.

We didn't have a working truck stereo; the console was dead and dark. The truck stereo was dead; only the handheld radio had any signal.

On the radio, a voice came through like it had been dragged through gravel. "— get to the highway. Move. Highway clear—" Then the voice broke into static and the station sprayed sparks of other voices, none that stayed long.

Dan tapped the map on the counter, the one we'd borrowed from the station and left to go soft at the folds. The old bridge is marked in pencil: OLD BRIDGE — WEIGHT LIMIT. The handwriting was mine from a week ago, when Otto asked me which way to the city and I joked about the bridge. Didn't think the joke would age like this.

The map has 'OLD BRIDGE — WEIGHT LIMIT' penciled at the bend, and the county detour adds about twenty-five minutes and crosses the rail yard.

"Main road's fastest if the bridge holds," Dan said. "Old bridge is the short cut."

"Short cut if it doesn't collapse under our weight," I said. "If that bridge gives, we don't just waste time — we get stuck."

Otto's voice was small and sharp. "Wood's old. Empire built it when they milled the oaks. There's a sign outside the county yard— weight-restricted."

Maya said, "Noise draws them like moths. Fast roads mean more sound. Highway might be clear now, but it won't stay that way."

We'd all heard pieces of it on the radio and from the mouths on the street; Maya was the one who said it out loud to the room.

I wrote it down, because writing makes things more solid than the promises we made each other. My notebook is in my back pocket; I curl its leather while I think.

Dan's plan was the calculator kind of plan — distances, fuel math, who goes where and when. He wanted to get across the bridge, burn time and get the highway. Lina wanted to go now, fast and furious; she wanted motion like a talisman.

I thought about the truck battery. The battery's weak; it will turn once or twice, but we can't afford long cranks. If we keep it from hunting for life we'll make the next town think we were ghosts and not people.

We argued with the bluntness of people who know what the stakes are worth. Maya said hospital route, hit the ER for morphine and antibiotics. I said the hospital is a time sink and we don't have time. Otto wanted the bridge avoided; he named every plank. Lina said something about leaving and I stopped listening because she was breathing like someone who wanted to forget gravity.

Dan tapped the radio. "We could set it on the highway and use it like bait," he said. "Draw them where we want them."

"Use the radio as bait," he said. The words sat in the room like a second heartbeat.

"No," Maya said. "Not unless we can watch it from cover."

Otto's hands were small and papery; they folded into each other. "We go to the highway. We don't get clever. We go fast, low and together."

So we made a thing of it. We shoved what we had into the pickup: the red jerry can, the radio, a single roll of duct tape, Lina's backpack with her comic books turned useless, my toolbox. Maya took her med bag on the passenger floor like a child clutching a stuffed animal for courage.

Dan slid into the driver's seat and checked the rig again. He turned the key. The engine bit on the second try and settled. It sounded like hope that had been smoked and then smoked again until it was thin.

We load the truck and agree: get to the highway before dark.

I write the time because time looks different now. Seconds taste like nails. I write because the radio gave me a voice and the notebook will be what people squeeze for answers later. If anyone ever reads this and thinks we were brave, know that we were afraid and that we kept moving because we had to.

Outside, someone slammed a car door two blocks over. The sound hung, then was gone. We all went quieter. Lina put her head between her knees and breathed as if she was trying to push the sound past her ribs.

Dan shut the kiosk door. He tucked the shotgun in the cab and slid the red can beside the wheel. He kept the radio loose in one hand.

Otto stood on the steps and looked back toward town, then at the highway shadowed on the map. "Get the bridge or take the long way, but go," he said. "Don't let the noise find us."

We started the truck and drove out like a bad prayer.
