Notebook — 17:08

Hospital doors are heavy in the wrong way — not the promise of help but the way metal bites your palm. We rolled up with the truck on two cylinders; the engine sounded like it was trying to cough a house out of its throat.

By the time we limped up to the hospital, the pickup had maybe a quarter left. Dan killed the engine and put his hand on the wheel like he was holding the whole world steady.

Maya took one look and went past the intake like she was pulled by gravity. "Back to triage," she said, voice flat. "Morphine, antibiotics—if the pharmacy's still sealed we get what we can from the nurses' cart." She ran.

Otto moved slower than the rest of us. He has a bad knee that takes his balance when the world leans; I know because his steps are the kind you apologise for. He hiked his coat up and tried to keep up anyway.

We found the ER open and wrong: lights blown in one bay, a gurney tipped and wet with a dark smear, two stretchers jammed against the counter. The place smelled like copper and fear.

There were three of them tucked into the rooms and the hallway when we came through the door — one on a gurney clutching at a sheet, one hunched behind the nurses' station, and another that came out of a supply closet with a sound like a slammed fist.

They were fast when the space was small. I put it in the notebook: close quarters make them cruel. Dan swung the butt of the shotgun against a door frame and we moved as a shape, not as five separate people.

Otto went to help a woman whose face was already the wrong color, and the nearest thing to a headline in my hands was the pickaxe in the truck. Lina grabbed it because she thinks tools are how the world makes sense.

We fought with what we had. The pickaxe wouldn't be poetry in a hallway but it was a hard note that made them stumble when Lina drove it between shoulders. Dan put a slug into the one on the gurney and the sound broke the room open. Maya worked at a man's arm that had been ripped; she pressed gauze and murmured like a litany.

Otto was old to a sprint. He ducked into the supply closet to drag out a cart and one of them lunged around the cart's edge. I saw the hand go over his sleeve and his mouth open like he was saying a name. There was a snap — a horrible small sound — and blood where there wasn't before. I kicked and shoved and a tray clattered and the thing that had him was gone.

He was bitten on the forearm. "Otto!" Lina yelled. "He's bitten!"

Maya swore, ripped off a strip of her own shirt and tied it above the bite. "We need morphine and field dressings. Now." She was all business and hands.

The pharmacy door was locked. It had been, like every good place in the town, built to stop the world from saying yes. There were controlled meds behind it — bottles lined and neat — and a safe with trays of ampoules. Maya checked the nurse's keys and cursed. As an EMT trainee she doesn't have the legal right to open the controlled cabinet. Legality stopped meaning much when someone is sinking into a bite.

We forced it.

Lina wedged the pickaxe handle into the lock and Dan rammed the door. It snapped with a sound like breaking a promise. We pulled the trays down. The safe had three ampoules of morphine and a stack of antibiotics. I shoved them into Maya's med bag while she counted and prayed and tried not to make eye contact with Otto who sat on a gurney and breathed like a man measuring his life.

Notebook — margin: morphine x3 taken from pharmacy; medkit now has: 3 morphine vials, 1 tourniquet, 2 antibiotic packets.

The fight spilled us out of the ER and into the street. People were moving like driftwood; noise made them rise. Otto's shirt was tied up and the bite looked small and obscene. He was talking about remembering things — a student, a lesson — like a man trying to trade time for steps.

We went back to the pickup. Dan was white around the mouth. "Bridge run first," he said. He pointed at a scratch on the map I keep folded in my pocket. "Old bridge is nearer. Quick in and out."

Lina wanted to go the other way, straight to the highway. She said, "Screw the bridge. Highway now. Fast and gone." She reached for the keys with the wrong kind of hunger in her hands.

Dan jabbed the map. "If the bridge holds, it's twelve miles and we can make the highway in thirty. At this burn rate we get one shot."

Maya cut her off without looking up from the med bag. "We don't have one shot if Otto can't keep up."

We drove. Otto rode the middle bench and didn't complain. The bridge that had been the short cut was worse than pencil on paper — planks gone, beams splintered like someone had used the bridge for a kind of violence. Cars sat on the lip of the span, bumper to bumper, and a mound of wreckage had been heaped across the far side. A snapped support leaned like a broken finger.

We parked and looked. "Blocked," Lina said, and it felt small as a word for the wrongness of it.

The map's detour paperwork would toss us the rail yard. Dan pointed and said, "Rail yard's the only route left. Less cover, more noise — but it gets us past the bridge."

I went to the back of the truck and pulled the battery access and the little bundle of wire I'd been keeping for a job that wasn't happening. The pickup's battery is weak; I scraped the terminals, taped a cracked vacuum hose enough to keep the hand pump from wobbling, twisted a spare cable into a bypass and cranked the starter relay so she would spin smoother. It isn't a miracle, only a promise to the next mile.
