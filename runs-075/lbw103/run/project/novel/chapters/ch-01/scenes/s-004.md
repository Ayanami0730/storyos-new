Notebook — 04:12

Dawn is a thief. It steals color and doubles sound until the town becomes a choir you can't ignore. We rolled toward the highway with the truck feeling like a borrowed thing — Dan's hands calm on the wheel, Maya's jaw clenched, the radio warm and useless in my palm. The jury‑rigged starter clicked like a man agreeing to something painful.

Six shells originally, I remind myself, because numbers are the only obedient things left: four in the tube, two spares. One gone in the ER, which leaves five. I check the stock again and count: five. That's what we have.

We'd been creeping since the hospital — idling, cranking, inching around blockades and into gaps — and small drips of fuel add up. By the time we pulled up to the blockade the gauge had slid under a quarter; call it a fifth now.

The highway looked like a promise that had been crowded out. Cars were stacked across the on‑ramp like someone had decided to stop mid‑life. Beyond them the interstate was a smoke line and a horizon of bad things moving. The horde flowed between the cars, a slow, hungry tide. We nosed the truck forward and found a gap two cars wide — a seam more luck than plan.

Dan tapped the handheld and I set the radio on a channel that still coughed into voice now and then. It would be a liar if it could. The promise we made earlier about the radio — use it as bait — sat in my throat like a pill. This is where it pays, or it doesn't.

"We do this quick," Dan said. "You get a diversion, we punch. I drive. Maya watches inside."

I nodded. I had the flare in my pocket and three tools and a small, stupid hope. Maya cinched the medkit to her belt. The medkit felt heavier than its weight; the ampoules made noise when she breathed.

The plan was stupid and precise: set the radio and flare on the far side of the blockade, roll the radio to a crackly loop of snatches — a voice saying 'highway clear' and some static — and let the flare and the radio argue for attention. When the horde answered, we gun the truck through the seam and take the highway. The radio could lie; the flare would not.

I taped the radio to a beer crate, wrapped its antenna with a strip of tape and a battery pack we couldn't afford to waste. I tuned it until a voice broke through like gravel: "— get to the highway — move —" then static, then the hollow scrape of another station trying to be a voice. It was enough.

Maya rolled down the window and I handed her the flare. "Go put it on the far side of the cars. Light it, then run back to the truck. Get under the wheel well if you have to."

She looked at me like she wanted to ask why and instead she took the flare. She ran like a person with instructions tattooed to their bones.

She dropped the flare near a dented pickup three cars down. The flare spat red like a small sun and the scratchy voice came alive on the crate — "— highway clear — move now —" I hit the timer on the radio and it started its thin lie as the flare began to bleed light into the morning.

The horde lifted its head. Sound is the key, the thing we've known and feared. They turned toward the flare and the voice like iron filings to a magnet. They gathered at the hiss of light and the whispering machine. For one long, terrible minute the world narrowed to the red pool and the sound of feet.

We gunned it.

Dan slammed the truck into the seam. The tailgate shook from the first bumps of bodies. I saw hands claw at the bumper like people trying to hold on to grief. I fired the shotgun twice — the shots were ugly and expensive and they threw bodies back like someone yanking at a curtain. I fired again when the cab filled with a shadow that smelled like copper. Three shots total from me. The recoil tasted like everything that had gone wrong.

Maya kept her head down and shoved at the gear while Dan craned, finding the gap, threading us through the teeth of cars and flailing limbs. Our truck hesitated on a rusted bumper and then roared through.

The radio tolled and died as its batteries were spent. The flare burned down to a smoking tin and the horde answered the light with a funeral of motion. They flooded toward the far side where the radio had whispered to them. It bought us something we didn't deserve: space to breathe, pavement that meant speed.

I looked back through the rear window and saw the concrete mouth of the highway opening like salvation. Dan pushed the truck and the tires bit into the on‑ramp. Then things got quieter in the way a stage does after a scream. Maya slapped my shoulder and pointed; two lanes clear. We slipped onto the interstate like sinners taking flight.

But the promise that bought that lane cost me. The shots I'd taken weren't free. When we were steady I counted again. Five at the start, three fired here, one earlier in the ER — that leaves one spare in my pocket and one in the tube. Two left. Numbers keep their shape if you let them.

We didn't get a clear road for long. The horde was thick where the flare burned and thin where we had driven; they would fill the seam again, even as we tasted speed. Dan and Maya leaned into the motion, folding the truck onto the interstate as if it were a thing they could make obey.

They pulled away, really pulled away, the brakes singing in a way that will live in me. I kept the radio empty in my lap and the shotgun across my knees until the sound of their taillights shrank. Maya leaned over the dash and yelled, "Go! Go! Go!" The words were the right ones for the living.

I couldn't get in. The truck was already momentum and prayer. I had one choice that wasn't a lie. I pushed the empty battery pack into the radio and kicked it toward the flare's smoking tin. I tossed the last shells into a pouch and clapped the pickaxe to my shoulder. The horde would be back to the noise. Let them have the noise I made.

I watched the two of them drive away until the morning wrapped them in light. Then I turned and walked back toward the heat and the red. This is what I am good for.

Notebook — 05:02

If anyone writes about this and gives us tidy adjectives, know that adjectives are a small comfort. Dan and Maya made it to the highway. I made a diversion. I fired until my arms forgot to hold the gun steady. The radio lied; the flare screamed; the road opened for a moment.

If this is the last thing I leave: put the radio on a crate. Put the flare tin in my pack. Don't make my death a thing of metaphor. Tell it clean — we used signal and light to buy a gap. I am tired.

— E.
