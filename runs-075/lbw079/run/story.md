I parked on the gravel drive and let the engine die; the house leaned in the salt‑wet air like something that had been waiting long enough to be polite. The lane had filled back in with seaweed and winter grass since I'd been a child, but the ruts still remembered the tyres, and the porch sighed when I stepped across it. I told myself I was here to be practical — inventory, boxes, signatures — the solemn work of settling a place that had already begun to forget people.

Inside, the kitchen was a narrow, scoured thing: a sink pitted with thin rust, a cracked window that looked out to wind and bramble, jars on a high shelf with their labels gone fuzzy. On the sideboard, half‑hidden under a dented tea tin, sat a scratched tin music box. It was smaller than I remembered and more ordinary: paint flaked from the lid, the metal dulled, a little dent on one corner where someone's elbow had once carried it too fast. Whoever had wound it last had not left a hand behind—yet the latch on the box looked recently moved, like a mouth that had just been opened and closed.

Mrs Harper was at her gate when I left the kitchen; she had the kind of expression memory carves into faces — condolence worked thin as papier‑mâché, and an edge under it like old wire. "Condolences, dear. You always did leave things behind," she said, as if the missing years were one of my possessions. She didn't name what was missing. She didn't have to.

I went up to the attic because attics are where a house's small economies are kept: lost mittens, a score of tickets, one child's treasure hoard. The ladder scraped as I climbed; the air changed to paper and dust, the light to the colour of old postcards. Beams leaned in a pattern like impatience. Trunks were stacked two deep and a tarpaulin draped something cool and rectangular. A tall mirror stood upright against the far wall; its glass was not a polish but a depth that ate light. It had a carved frame, the kind that remembered the carver's wrist in every leaf. Against that dark face, my reflection came late, a suggestion rather than a claim.

I set my palms to the mirror's frame because hands are how I check what is solid. The wood was cool and the glass colder. For a moment the attic became the size of my breath; I had the sudden sensation of being observed, not by a face in the glass but by the glass itself. It wasn't reflective in the way a mirror should be — it swallowed the edges of the room and left me looking at a distance that was not mine.

Under the tarp the trunk was iron‑bound and roped with dust. Its lock stared black and patient. I lifted the edge of the cover and a stale summer smell slipped out: lemon‑sugar and rain. It was what you smell when something is kept for a reason and the reason is shame.

I was making lists in my head — furniture, paper, one dented music box, the shape of a trunk to be catalogued — when a thin, absurd sound threaded up through the floorboards. From downstairs came the faint, impossible motion: the music box winding itself, as if the house had turned the key. The latch outside on the sideboard made a small, precise click; a sieve of notes spilled up through the joists.

The tune was a thin, watery lullaby — all tin and memory — tinkling up through the floorboards. It was small, like a child's breath, and wrong in the way that exactness is wrong: not random, not a trick of wind, but deliberate and patient. I felt it in my chest the way an old bruise remembers an accident. It wasn't a ghost‑story shiver; it was recognition: the house was not empty.

I stayed where I was, hands on the mirror, listening. The sound threaded the house together like a seam. Whatever had been waiting here was patient enough to wind itself and set the day moving again.

The tarp came away like a guilty thing. Under it the trunk smelled of lemon and rain the way old boxes smell of decisions; folded on top was a leather book, soft at the corners and stained with a child's economy of ink. The cover had no title, only a loop of string that had been slipped through itself for a fast, private binding. I sat on an upturned crate and opened it.

Handwriting filled the first half of the journal: small, slanted, with a careful unevenness that belonged to anyone who had practiced saying the same secret over and over. The sentences began child‑short and then grew oddly formal where the paper asked for it, as if the hand and the instruction were not the same voice. I read aloud because the house had started doing things when I spoke.

The line I said made the glass go thin. In the mirror the attic changed: light softened to a warm dust, the trunks wore old cloth and, by the trunk I had just moved, a child sat with bare knees and a hair ribbon. She wound a little box on the sideboard below — not the tin one in my kitchen but the same shape — and her hands were small and exact. The movement in the reflection matched the notes that were threading up through the floorboards; first a hairsbreadth of latency, then perfect alignment, like a clock catching itself.

The journal held a recipe more than a confession. I copied the lines into my head and read them back because they hurt in a useful way. They were literal and terrible.

"Sing the lullaby at the well, and name it true.

1. Bring the lullaby to the shallow well in the basement.
2. Wind the box until its key will not turn again; let the tune sit in the stone.
3. Lay the brass key on the lip, say what was done, and drop your eyes."

Beneath the instructions, in the same small hand, a single line sat like a signature: "Cost: there will be a price."

There is a bluntness to children's writing when a thing matters. I could feel, in the margin flourishes and the sudden adult hush of step‑instructions, the person who had written it — patient, careful, wanting a witness with rules. The brass key had been tucked into the back pages. It was warm as if someone had just closed their fist around it; the shaft bore a nick like a small tooth. Up close the head of the key looked cut to the trunk's lock — the nick mirrored the roughness of the hole in the attic floor — and I could tell, by the way it sat in my palm, that it would likely fit the lock cleanly if I turned it there.

I did not try it. The lock in the trunk watched, patient and black. The journal gave me a place and a motion and a cost; it did not give me consent. Instead I held the key in my palm and watched the mirror.

The reflected child looked up at one point, not at me but out through a wall that no longer existed. Her mouth worked like she was singing and the tune arrived in my attic as the same thin, watery thing that the music box made downstairs. Where the glass showed her winding, the real house answered by winding now. The sync was exact enough to be deliberate: each bell in the lullaby struck as her lips separated on the same breath. The mirror wasn't showing me a lesson. It was showing me an act that had been done, by a hand I had just read about and a throat that answered my attic.

So the book named a place and an action and a price. It also named someone who wanted the telling to be true. I tightened my fingers on the key and, because the house had begun to call the past forward, I put the journal under my arm and climbed down to the stairs, the sound of the lullaby falling ahead of me through the house.

The wind had teeth by the time I reached the cellar steps. Out on the lane the storm had turned the drive into a shallow inlet; ruts filled with spit‑salt and the porch threw gravel like a small cough. My phone had died somewhere on the lane and the house's electricity stuttered twice and then went to a begrudging dark. There was no reason to leave, and fewer to go back.

I went up to the attic first because the house required its rituals; the trunk sat patient under its tarp and the key in my pocket warmed to the shape of my palm. The brass turned with just enough resistance to feel like fitting a hand into a glove — a small tug, a clean click — and then the iron gave. Paint cracked. The lid rose on a lazy hinge and a lemon‑sugar breath spilled out.

Inside were rubbers, a folded handkerchief, and a stack of photographs like a small, stubborn history. The top one was three children on a sunlit porch; faces bright, a place in it that future albums left empty. Water had lanterned the corners and a thumb had smudged the bottom right until the words on the back were half‑gone.

Looking at it, my head filled with a ghost of sensation: a hand slipping into a pocket, the heat of a porch like an oven, a shadow across a heel. It wasn't a memory so much as a single ache — nausea, a tilt, the metallic taste of something I couldn't name. When I tried to hold it the image fell away, thread by thread. The room tilted with me and the house obligingly answered with the plumbing's low, dated knock.

Mrs Harper was at her gate when I came out, phone to her ear, hair pinned tighter by the wind. She offered the same condolence she'd given me before — practiced politeness — and then, as if the wind had loosened some small thing, she added, voice low and hedged: "I might've popped in to check the place the other day. If the box was wound when I left, well — old habit. I can't be sure." She said it like something that could be true and not truth at all.

Her hedged answer didn't close anything. It suggested possibility without ownership, and that was worse. I wanted her to tell me the summer's name or the trunk's sins, but she only folded away, eyes avoiding the porch like a hand that doesn't want to pick up a hot plate.

Back inside, the kitchen light blinked once and went, and the music box began again downstairs — that thin, watery lullaby threading up through floorboards. Its notes landed in my chest and a whisper rode with them: a child's syllable, a breath of sound the length of a name. It wasn't a replay; it was a coaxing, timed to the music rather than explaining it. Pipes thudded like a slow, surprised heart. The bulb over the sink caught for a beat and then gave nothing.

The photograph sat in my hands like a small accusation. Whoever had arranged this had done it with care; gaps had been tended, not left by chance. The house had become methodical: the music would wind and the mirror would answer when asked; the plumbing and the lights would mark my confusion with physical punctuation.

I folded the photos back into the trunk with fingers that felt borrowed and, with the journal under my arm and the key warm in my fist, I went down the cellar steps. The lullaby fell ahead of me through the dark, patient as a promise and twice as insistent.

I brought the journal up like contraband. The attic felt smaller in the storm's lull; the mirror waited with the same indifferent depth it always had. The music box sat on the floorboard beside me, keys and dents catching the weak light. There was a ragged hole in the attic floorboard near the mirror — the size a key could find — a scar of saw and splintered wood that had never been sanded out. I set the box on the mirror's carved frame, the brass key heavy and warm in my palm, and read the line the book had kept folded in its throat.

"Say her name, wind the song, pour the memory into the well."

My voice scraped across the glass and the mirror answered by filling with sound. The attic's dark went thin and then tore: light from another day bled out of the glass, bringing heat and lemon‑sugar and the smell of old rain like a thing that had been simmered and put aside.

It began as a family photograph made whole. I saw the porch, the three children, and the adults — my mother, my father, and Mrs Harper — their faces small and panicked with the practical panic people get when they must choose between truth and consequence. The music box was in my mother's hands; she wound it with fingers that didn't look old enough to know what they were doing. The lullaby started, bright and thin, and a child — Margaret — slipped on the stones and fell into the shallow well. It was not cinematic. It was precise: the slap of water, the sound of a small body hitting stone and growing still. My hands remembered how to reach even as my head tried not to.

They did not call for help first. They did what the journal would later ask: my father crouched, eyes frantic, and took the brass key. He laid it on the lip while my mother kept the music winding and Mrs Harper pressed a hand to her mouth and then to the child's forehead. They read something — a line that might have been the one I had read earlier — and then my father said my name with a voice that was not angry and not gentle, simply urgent. The next thing the glass gave me was not sight but the feeling of being walled off. I felt my mind contract, like a hand closing on a ribbon. The world of that day shrank, and a seam stitched across it.

When the replay finished the mirror showed them weeping and doing the tidy, practical things: buckets, towels, a blank face put on the photograph stack. They sealed the truth in silence because it was easier than explaining why a child had disappeared from a family's life. They had used the book's words as a tool. The consequence was immediate and exact: something in me had been taken so that what happened could be kept from me.

There was no dramatic music; the room smelled of lemon, and my father's face was the last thing I could name before a white nothing moved in. The attic felt suddenly too loud, the carved frame too close under my fingers. Margaret's small, patient voice threaded through the lullaby as I wound the box on the mirror until the key would not go further. I laid my brass on the floor's hole, said what the book told me — her name, blunt and careful — and let my eyes drop as though I were following instructions I had long been trained to obey.

Pain is a blunt, honest thing. The moment the key touched the wood the house seemed to take a breath and the world in my head went clean in a way that left a new absence. Where my father's laugh used to live there was a white place that tasted faintly of salt and old tea. I reached for it and found only the shape of wanting. Margaret's reflection stilled in the mirror and then, as if a tide had finally turned, she walked out of the glass and into the attic air. She looked at me long enough for the house to be satisfied, and then she moved through the rafters and was gone.

The lullaby unwound itself to the last bell. I stood with the journal under one arm and a gap where a laugh had been. The house had been cleared; the truth had been made to hold. I had paid and the price had been taken.
