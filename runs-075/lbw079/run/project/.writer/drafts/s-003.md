The wind had teeth by the time I reached the cellar steps. Out on the lane the storm had turned the drive into a shallow inlet; ruts filled with spit‑salt and the porch threw gravel like a small cough. My phone had died somewhere on the lane and the house's electricity stuttered twice and then went to a begrudging dark. There was no reason to leave, and fewer to go back.

I went up to the attic first because the house required its rituals; the trunk sat patient under its tarp and the key in my pocket warmed to the shape of my palm. The brass turned with just enough resistance to feel like fitting a hand into a glove — a small tug, a clean click — and then the iron gave. Paint cracked. The lid rose on a lazy hinge and a lemon‑sugar breath spilled out.

Inside were rubbers, a folded handkerchief, and a stack of photographs like a small, stubborn history. The top one was three children on a sunlit porch; faces bright, a place in it that future albums left empty. Water had lanterned the corners and a thumb had smudged the bottom right until the words on the back were half‑gone.

Looking at it, my head filled with a ghost of sensation: a hand slipping into a pocket, the heat of a porch like an oven, a shadow across a heel. It wasn't a memory so much as a single ache — nausea, a tilt, the metallic taste of something I couldn't name. When I tried to hold it the image fell away, thread by thread. The room tilted with me and the house obligingly answered with the plumbing's low, dated knock.

Mrs Harper was at her gate when I came out, phone to her ear, hair pinned tighter by the wind. She offered the same condolence she'd given me before — practiced politeness — and then, as if the wind had loosened some small thing, she added, voice low and hedged: "I might've popped in to check the place the other day. If the box was wound when I left, well — old habit. I can't be sure." She said it like something that could be true and not truth at all.

Her hedged answer didn't close anything. It suggested possibility without ownership, and that was worse. I wanted her to tell me the summer's name or the trunk's sins, but she only folded away, eyes avoiding the porch like a hand that doesn't want to pick up a hot plate.

Back inside, the kitchen light blinked once and went, and the music box began again downstairs — that thin, watery lullaby threading up through floorboards. Its notes landed in my chest and a whisper rode with them: a child's syllable, a breath of sound the length of a name. It wasn't a replay; it was a coaxing, timed to the music rather than explaining it. Pipes thudded like a slow, surprised heart. The bulb over the sink caught for a beat and then gave nothing.

The photograph sat in my hands like a small accusation. Whoever had arranged this had done it with care; gaps had been tended, not left by chance. The house had become methodical: the music would wind and the mirror would answer when asked; the plumbing and the lights would mark my confusion with physical punctuation.

I folded the photos back into the trunk with fingers that felt borrowed and, with the journal under my arm and the key warm in my fist, I went down the cellar steps. The lullaby fell ahead of me through the dark, patient as a promise and twice as insistent.
