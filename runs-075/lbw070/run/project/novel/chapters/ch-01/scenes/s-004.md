We stumbled into the schoolyard with five minutes left before the bell. The tarmac smelled faintly of chalk and yesterday's rain; children clustered around games like islands. Under the pine, Finn sat on his backpack, knees swinging, relief softening his face when he saw us.

Ms. Ramirez, who had been watching the cluster from near the school steps, waved us over with the patient attention she always carried. "Knights of the Cafeteria," Ms. Ramirez called, smiling. "Report to the lunch table, heroes." Her voice made the whole thing feel official in a way no one had any right to expect.

I set the Crown of Crumbs into Finn's small hands and felt something like the end of a sentence settle in my chest. He made a face of exaggerated gratitude — the sort kids do when they owe you nothing — and then he hugged the lunchbox to his ribs as if it were something alive.

Goliath lay on the bench by the pine tree, head on his paws and watching us with the patient expression he'd used on the bridge. In the distance, near the far hedge by the bike rack, Mr. Bennett stood with his hands in his pockets, watching too; his face had that softened look we'd seen before, like a door left ajar.

Mara stamped the back of our homework flag into Finn's hands and declared, as if reading from a scroll, "By order of the King's Road, these knights are hereby charged to eat heartily and share snacks when necessary."

Ms. Ramirez laughed and clapped once. "Knights of the Cafeteria," she said again, and the phrase stuck like a sticker on the morning. A couple of kids cheered and someone tossed an imaginary crown; Finn pretended to curtsy and then bolted for the lunch line.

For a moment after he left, the three of us stood in a small triangle: Mara with her helmet crooked, me with jam still faint on my palm, and Finn's backpack looking suddenly enormous and whole. The morning had been a list of tiny dangers — a loose plank, a barking dog, a grumpy neighbor — but each one had asked for something small and honest of us.

I realized the run had been a proving ground: small acts, not big deeds, had made us something like knights. Mara nudged my shoulder with her elbow and smiled the way she always did when a game had become true. I put my hand to the dented metal lid of the lunchbox as if to check it was still there, and the weight of it was the same; what had changed was what the weight meant.
