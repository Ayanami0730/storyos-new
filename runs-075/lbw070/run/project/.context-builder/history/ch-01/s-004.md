### previous-scene  <novel/chapters/ch-01/scenes/s-003.md>

The bridge loomed sooner than I expected — a short wooden span over water that chattered and ran bright between damp stones. There were about eight minutes until the bell when we reached the bank.

A plank near the left-middle had a gap where a nail had worked loose; it tipped when I pressed it with my toe and made the wood complain. I had the Crown of Crumbs hugged to my side and the helmet shoved back so I could see, and for a second the moat felt very real.

Then Goliath exploded out of the park: tail high, slobber shining at his jowls, bounding as if he'd been waiting to be a dragon that day. He barked, loud and surprised, and his paws sent up the wet scent of the grass. Mara froze for half a breath, then did something braver than pretending: she crouched, helmet tilted, and spoke to him in a sing-song voice. "Easy boy, it's only knights with sandwiches today," she said, and patted the ground like she was offering a treaty.

Neighbors peered from behind curtains; the dog slowed, head cocked. He wasn't mean — his bark was big and his tongue was slobbery, but his eyes were softer than his noise.

The sensible part of me wanted to step back and reroute, but the part that had promised Finn tightened like a cord. "The rope's on my bike on the porch," I said, and before I fully decided I had already slipped past the helmet, out to where my bike leaned against the rail. I grabbed the short coil of rope from the basket. "Loop it round the oak post," I told Mara, because plans with steps don't ravel as easily.

Looping the rope around the oak post at the near bank, I tied three quick hitches and held the free end as a handline. Then, following the second idea — a low guide — I threaded the rope across to a squat root on the far bank and cinched it up low, testing the tension. "One at a time," Mara said, sounding like a commander and a friend.

I put one foot on the good plank, kept my weight off the left-middle board, and edged across, the rope rubbing into my palms. The water hissed below and the plank creaked, but the handline saved me from wobble and the low guide steadied my hips. Mara moved to the bank and made soft noises to Goliath, who watched us with the patience of a dog considering a puzzle.

When I reached the far side and set the Crown of Crumbs down, my hands ached and felt proud for being honest about how scared I was. Mara was already grinning, the cardboard helmet sitting crooked on her head like a crown. The bridge had been a test; we had unfairly short time and a big, barky dragon, and we had passed both.

The King's Road wasn't paved in gold — just patched flagstones and hedges trimmed like a barber's careful work — but when Mara set the cardboard flag to flutter, it felt like a proper procession. Mr. Bennett stood where the path narrowed, planted by his hedge like a tollkeeper. He looked at us the way a gate looks at a cart: expectant and a little annoyed. "State your business," he said, voice low and knobby as a walking stick.

Mara stepped forward as if she'd been rehearsing for this exact moment. She bowed so loudly her helmet nearly slid over one eye, then pulled a ridiculous, triumphant face and recited, in an oracle voice, a rhyme about knights and lost lunches. A couple of neighbors glanced out; Mr. Bennett's mouth twitched the smallest bit.

He held up a hand. "Toll is a riddle. Answer right, you pass. Answer wrong, you circle the block." He folded his hands together like he was offering a coin. "What costs nothing, but is paid by every good knight?"

My stomach did a small, traitorous flip. Twelve minutes, I thought, and the number set my teeth toward motion.

Mara made a show of thinking, fingers at her chin, then did a drumroll on the back of her homework flag and said, "Bravery!" loud enough to make a crow flinch.

Mr. Bennett's eyebrows climbed. "Bravery," he repeated, and the sound in his voice softened, as if a hinge he'd kept stiff had given a small creak. He looked at me then, appraising me as if I were the sort of person who might drop what was expected of him.

I took a breath. "It's not a knight's deed this time, sir," I said. The words surprised me by how steady they sounded. "It's a promise. Finn's seven. He'll wait all morning if he has to. I won't make him."

Mr. Bennett regarded me for a long second, the sort of long second that a school bell measures in small intervals. Then he pushed back a sleeve and rubbed his palm against his hip. "Fine," he grunted. "Keep your promise. But mind you don't dawdle on the King's Road." The grunt held a warning and, if you wanted to read it kindly, the barest scrap of mercy.

Mara squealed and hugged my arm before I could be all noble about it, and the compass bumped my zipper like a polite reminder. We stepped past Mr. Bennett; the hedge turned back into an ordinary wall of green. The King's Road slid into a narrow sidewalk and the school bell, a drum somewhere ahead, began its slow, patient sound.

The backpack strap dug into my shoulder like an impatient elbow and a slant of light from the narrow kitchen window cut the counter into a bright river. Books leaned against the far wall, their spines like quiet sentries; flour dust freckled the surface where Mara had been practicing pancakes and the smell of browned batter hung in the air.

Mara was at the counter, cheeks dusted with flour and grinning as if she’d been waiting for an errand to become important. She popped the lid and held the lunchbox up with mock solemnity. "Behold: the Crown of Crumbs," she said, as if presenting a crown at court. Inside was Finn's ham-and-cheese sandwich, three thin apple slices tucked in a corner, and a tiny cardboard juice box with a cartoon fox smiling at me.

She didn't waste time on ceremony. She grabbed the back page of my math homework and wrote, in a hurry that somehow still looked decisive: "Quest List." Then she folded the paper into a flag and read aloud: "1. Cross the moat (bridge). 2. Pay the toll (Mr. Bennett). 3. Return Crown to Finn at the schoolyard." She handed me the flag like it was the map that mattered.

From under the cereal box she produced a cardboard helmet — battered, the Frosty O's logo ghosted along its side, a strip of silver duct tape across the dented brow, and a crease where someone's elbow had made a valley. She clipped a small blue plastic compass to the kitchen's old keyring; the glass over the face was spidered but the needle still swung, stubborn as a promise.

There were twelve minutes until the bell. Saying the number out loud made our feet decide for us.

Mara appointed roles before I could think too long. "You're the Bearer of the Crown," she announced, tapping the lunchbox into my palm. "I'm Keeper of Maps. If anyone tries to steal our quest, I'll distract them with a sonnet. Or with very dramatic eyebrows."

The lunchbox felt absurd in my hands and also exactly right, like a weight that taught you how much you cared. For a second I pictured Finn waiting under the pine tree in the schoolyard, small and stubborn and convinced he didn't need anyone's help — and that turned the ridiculous into something necessary.

We moved through the kitchen with the practiced silence of people trying not to miss a thing: don't trip, don't spill the juice box, don't bang into Mr. Bennett on the path. Mara shoved the cardboard helmet onto my head like a coronation and hooked the compass to my zipper. The Crown of Crumbs bumped against my side as we stepped onto the sidewalk; the King's Road waited.

Outside, the morning smelled like wet pavement and possibility.
